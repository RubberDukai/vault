# Britain from the Stone Age to now

**What they need first:** *Reading a map*, because history happens in places and this lesson keeps sending you to the map.

History is not a list of dates. It is the answer to *why is it like this?* — why the road bends where it does, why the church is where it is, why the field is that shape, why we say the words we say. Teach the order first, so there is a shelf to put things on, and then hang everything else on it.

**The one thing to get straight at the start: how long ago.** Children hear "a long time ago" and hear one distance. Draw a timeline along a wall or a path, 10,000 years long, and walk it: every metre a few hundred years. The Romans are surprisingly near the end. The Normans are nearer still. Your great-grandparents are a few centimetres. Do this once, physically, and the rest of the lesson has somewhere to live.

## The order, with what changed

| Roughly when | Who and what | The change that mattered |
|---|---|---|
| before 10,000 BC | **[Old Stone Age](wiki:Paleolithic\|Stone_Age)** hunters | ice; people follow the animals; Britain joined to Europe |
| 4000–2500 BC | **New Stone Age** — [farming arrives](wiki:Neolithic_British_Isles) | food is grown, so people stay put: villages, clearance, [Stonehenge](wiki:Stonehenge), long barrows |
| 2500–800 BC | **[Bronze Age](wiki:Bronze_Age_Britain\|Bronze_Age)** | metal: better tools and weapons, and trade to get tin and copper — round barrows, field systems |
| 800 BC–AD 43 | **[Iron Age](wiki:Iron_Age)** [Celts](wiki:Celts) | iron is common, so everyone can have good tools; hillforts, tribes, the first coins |
| AD 43–410 | **[Romans](wiki:Roman_Britain)** | roads, towns, writing, stone building, law, money — then they leave |
| 410–1066 | **[Anglo-Saxons](wiki:Anglo-Saxons)** and **[Vikings](wiki:Viking_Age)** | English begins; villages and shires; Christianity; raids and settlement |
| 1066 | **[The Norman Conquest](wiki:Norman_Conquest)** | castles, French in the language, the Domesday survey, everything owned by somebody |
| 1066–1485 | **[Middle Ages](wiki:Middle_Ages)** | cathedrals, [Magna Carta](wiki:Magna_Carta) (1215), the [Black Death](wiki:Black_Death) (1348) which killed a third of everyone |
| 1485–1603 | **[Tudors](wiki:Tudor_period)** | the break with Rome, printing, ships and the first voyages, Shakespeare |
| 1642–1660 | **[Civil War](wiki:English_Civil_War)** and after | a king executed; Parliament settles it that the Crown is not absolute |
| 1750–1900 | **[Industrial Revolution](wiki:Industrial_Revolution)** | coal, steam, iron, mills, railways; people move to towns; [the Empire](wiki:British_Empire) |
| 1914–1945 | **[First](wiki:World_War_I) and [Second World Wars](wiki:World_War_II)** | vast loss; women's work and the vote; the end of the old order |
| since 1945 | **Modern Britain** | the [health service](wiki:National_Health_Service\|Health_care), the end of empire, electricity and the motor car everywhere, computers |

Learn that column of dates in order. Eight or nine anchors are enough — 4000 BC, 43, 410, 1066, 1348, 1485, 1642, 1750, 1914 — and everything else can be placed between them.

## Six things worth more than the rest

**[Farming](wiki:Agriculture) changed everything (about 4000 BC).** Before it, everyone moved and nobody owned much. After it, food could be stored — so there was surplus, so there could be people who did not farm: priests, soldiers, smiths, rulers. Every other change in the table follows from that one. Ask the child *what could you do, once there was more food than you needed this week?* and let them work out the rest themselves.

**The [Romans](wiki:Ancient_Rome) brought writing, and then it stopped.** Roman Britain had towns, straight roads, baths, coins and written records. After 410 much of it fell out of use — not instantly, but within a couple of generations. It is the clearest example in our own island of how much can be lost and how fast, and worth sitting with. Their roads, though, are still under half the A-roads in the country.

**1066 is the hinge.** The Normans took the land and gave it to their own, built castles to hold it, and wrote down who had what in the [Domesday Book](wiki:Domesday_Book) — which is why we know so much about 1086 and so little about 1050. Half the everyday words in English are Saxon and half the grand ones are French, which is why we *eat* **pork** (French) from a **pig** (Saxon), and **beef** from a **cow**.

**The Black Death (1348).** A third to a half of the people died in about two years. With so few left to work, those who survived could demand wages and move — so the old bonded system broke. A catastrophe changed the balance of power more than any law had.

**[Steam](wiki:Steam_engine) and coal (from about 1750).** For the whole of history before it, work meant muscle, wind or water. Steam meant power anywhere, at any hour, all year. Towns grew around it, half of Britain moved off the land in a century, and the air and the rivers paid for it. The cost and the gain belong in the same lesson.

**Rights were argued for, not given.** [Magna Carta](wiki:Magna_Carta) 1215, Parliament against the King in 1642, the vote widening through the 1800s, women voting in 1918 and 1928. Teach it as a long argument that is still going, not as a gift that arrived.

## How to find the evidence in your own parish

This is the part that makes it real, and it needs nothing but boots and the map.

- **Place names tell you who came.** *-chester/-caster* = a Roman fort. *-ton*, *-ham*, *-ley*, *-field*, *-worth* = Saxon. *-by*, *-thorpe*, *-thwaite*, *-toft* = Viking. *-combe*, *-pen*, *tor*, *avon* = older Celtic, usually a hill or a river. Look up every settlement within ten miles on the map and sort them. The pattern on the map is a map of invasions.
- **The church** is often the oldest building standing. Round arches are usually Norman; tall pointed windows are later. Read the memorials and the war graves.
- **The shape of the fields.** Small, irregular, thick-hedged fields are usually very old. Long, gently S-curved strips are medieval ploughland. Big, straight-edged rectangles are enclosure, mostly 1750–1850. You can date a landscape from a gate.
- **Ridge and furrow** — long low corrugations in old pasture, best seen in low evening sun or under snow. Medieval ploughing, never since disturbed.
- **Holloways** — lanes worn metres below the fields by centuries of feet and hooves.
- **A straight road over long distances** is Roman until proved otherwise.
- **The oldest people you know.** Ask them what the village had when they were ten — shops, trades, who had a car, what work there was. Write it down with the date and their name. In fifty years that page is a source, and nobody else will have made it.

## How to think like a historian

- **A source is something left behind**: a document, a building, a tool, a bone, a photograph, a story handed down.
- **Primary** (made at the time) or **secondary** (written later about it). Both are useful; they fail differently.
- **Every source has a point of view.** Ask: who made this, when, for whom, and what did they want the reader to think? A monk's chronicle, a landlord's rent book and a soldier's letter home tell three different truths about the same year.
- **Two sources that agree, made independently, are worth far more than one.**
- **Absence is not proof.** We know little about ordinary people before 1500 because almost nobody wrote about them, not because nothing happened to them.

## Exercises

1. Make a timeline along a wall or a path for the last 10,000 years and mark on it: farming arrives, Stonehenge, the Romans arrive and leave, 1066, the Black Death, the first railways, the First World War, the year you were born.
2. Put in order, earliest first: Vikings, Romans, Normans, Iron Age Celts, Tudors, Bronze Age.
3. What changed when people started farming? Give three consequences that follow from having more food than you need this week.
4. Why do we know so much about 1086 and so little about 1050?
5. Using the Maps tab, list every settlement within ten miles and sort the names into Celtic, Roman, Saxon and Viking. What does the pattern suggest about who settled where you live?
6. Explain why we eat *pork* from a *pig* and *beef* from a *cow*, using 1066.
7. The Black Death killed perhaps a third of the population. Give two ways that made life better for some of those who survived, and explain why.
8. Go to the oldest church you can reach. Find the oldest part of the building and say how you can tell. Copy down the oldest date you can find anywhere in it.
9. Walk a field boundary. Is it old and irregular, medieval strip, or straight-edged enclosure? What does that tell you about when it was laid out?
10. Interview the oldest person you can. Ask what the place had when they were ten, and write it up with their name and the date. Keep it.
11. A landlord's rent book and a labourer's letter describe the same village in 1830 quite differently. Are either of them lying? What should you do with the two accounts?
12. Name three things the Romans had in Britain that were gone within a few generations of their leaving. What does that suggest about how securely knowledge is held?
13. Choose one of the anchors from the table and write half a page on what the change meant for an ordinary family — not for the king.

## Answers

1. No fixed answer — the point is the scale. Farming should be nine-tenths of the way along, the Romans in the last fifth, 1066 in the last tenth, and a lifetime a few centimetres.
2. Bronze Age, Iron Age Celts, Romans, Vikings, Normans, Tudors.
3. Food can be stored, so people stay in one place; there is a surplus, so some people can do work other than farming (smiths, priests, soldiers); there is something worth owning and defending, so land, wealth, rank and war all follow.
4. Because the Normans wrote it down. The Domesday survey of 1086 recorded who held what across nearly the whole country; almost nothing comparable survives from 1050. What is recorded and what happened are not the same thing.
5. No fixed answer — look for correct sorting by ending and a sensible conclusion (Viking names clustered in the north and east, Saxon names everywhere, Celtic names on hills and rivers and in the west).
6. The Normans, who spoke French, owned the land and ate the meat; the Saxons, who spoke English, kept the animals. The animal in the field kept its Saxon name; the meat on the table took the French one.
7. Wages rose and people could move to find better work, because so few were left that labour was suddenly scarce and landowners had to compete for it. The old system of tied, unpaid labour began to break down.
8. Look for evidence-based reasoning: round arches and thick walls for Norman, pointed arches and big windows for later, different stone or a straight join where a part was added.
9. Old and irregular with thick mixed hedges — ancient, possibly pre-medieval. Long and slightly S-shaped — medieval strip fields. Straight lines and thin single-species hedges — enclosure, mostly 1750–1850.
10. No fixed answer. Insist on the name and the date; that is what turns it from a chat into a source.
11. Not necessarily lying — they saw different things and wanted different readers to believe different things. Use both, note who wrote each and why, and look for the facts they agree on.
12. Towns, straight metalled roads kept in repair, central heating, glass windows, coinage, widespread writing and record-keeping, aqueducts and baths. It suggests that knowledge and infrastructure are held only as long as the society that maintains them holds together — which is, in short, why this vault exists.
13. No fixed answer. Mark it on whether it stays with ordinary life — food, work, land, family, danger — rather than kings and battles.
