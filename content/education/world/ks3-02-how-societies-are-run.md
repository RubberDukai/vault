# How societies are run

**What they need first:** *The modern world and how it was made*; *Speaking and debate* for the practical half of this lesson; *Thinking clearly*.

Wherever more than a few people live together, three questions have to be answered: **who decides, how are disputes settled, and what happens to people who cannot fend for themselves?** Every system of government in history is an answer to those three. Teach them as questions first, and the forms afterwards make sense as attempts rather than as a list to memorise.

This lesson ends somewhere unusually practical: with how to run a meeting and make a decision that people will actually abide by. For a household, a workshop or a village, that last section may be the most useful page in the subject.

## What government is for

At minimum: keep order, settle disputes, defend the group, and provide the things that benefit everyone but that no one person will pay for — roads, clean water, drains, defence, courts.

Beyond that people disagree, honestly and permanently: schooling, medicine, care of the old and the ill, work, the relief of poverty. **Where the line falls is the main argument of politics**, and it is not a question with a provable answer. A child should be told that plainly, and should be able to state the case on both sides before giving their own.

## The forms it takes

| Form | Who decides | The catch |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct democracy** | everyone votes on everything | only works for small numbers; the majority can still crush a minority |
| **Representative democracy** | elected representatives decide | representatives may not represent you; it depends on real choice and honest counting |
| **Monarchy** | a hereditary ruler | quality is a lottery; today usually ceremonial, as in Britain |
| **Oligarchy** | a small group — wealthy, military, party | rules tend to favour the group |
| **Dictatorship** | one person, unchecked | no way to remove them but force |
| **Theocracy** | religious authority | no appeal against a ruling said to be divine |
| **Anarchy** | no central authority | works in very small, tight groups; tends to be replaced by whoever is strongest |

Most real states are mixtures. **Ask of any of them the same three questions: how does power change hands, what can it not do, and what happens to somebody who says it is wrong?** Those three answers tell you more than any label.

## How Britain's works

- **Constitutional monarchy and parliamentary democracy.** The monarch is head of state with almost no political power; Parliament makes the law.
- **Parliament** has two chambers: the **House of Commons**, elected, which holds the real power; and the **House of Lords**, unelected, which revises and delays.
- **The government** is formed by whoever commands a majority in the Commons; the **[Prime Minister](wiki:Prime_Minister_of_the_United_Kingdom|Prime_minister)** leads it and the **Cabinet** runs the departments.
- **General elections** at least every five years. The country is divided into constituencies, each returning one MP by **first past the post** — the most votes wins, even without a majority. This produces clear results and decisive governments, and it also means a party can win power on well under half the votes cast. That trade-off is a real argument with two sides.
- **The courts** are independent of government, and there is no single written constitution — an unusual arrangement resting on convention, precedent and statute.
- **Local government** runs housing, roads, refuse, planning and schools — and is where an individual can actually be heard.

Britain has no written constitution; the United States has one, with an explicit separation of powers and a supreme court that can strike down laws. Compare the two and the trade-off becomes visible: flexibility against fixed guarantees.

## Law

- **Criminal law** — offences against society; the state prosecutes; the standard is *beyond reasonable doubt*; the outcome is punishment.
- **Civil law** — disputes between people; one party sues; the standard is the *balance of probabilities*; the outcome is compensation or an order.

**The principles that matter**, and each is there because of an abuse it was invented to prevent:

- **The rule of law** — the same law applies to everyone, including the government.
- **Innocent until proven guilty**, with the burden of proof on the accuser.
- **The right to a fair, public hearing** before an independent judge, and to defend yourself.
- **No punishment without a law** that already existed when you acted.
- **Trial by jury** for serious offences — ordinary people, not officials, deciding the facts.
- **Proportionate punishment**, and an appeal.

Ask of each: *what would happen without it?* That question turns a list into an understanding.

## Rights, and their limits

Some rights are **absolute** — nobody may be tortured or enslaved, ever, for any reason. Most are **qualified**: free speech, movement, assembly and religion can be limited where they seriously harm others, and the argument is always about where that line falls.

**A right implies a duty on somebody.** Your right to a fair trial requires courts, judges and taxes to pay for them. Your right to be left alone requires everybody else to leave you alone. A child who grasps that rights are claims on other people, and not merely permissions, argues about them far better.

**Minority rights are what tell you whether a democracy is real.** A vote of 51% that strips the other 49% of their rights is not a triumph of democracy but the failure it was designed to prevent — which is why courts and constitutions exist above voting.

## Money, tax and trade

- **Money** is a way of storing and exchanging value, and it works only because people believe it will be accepted tomorrow. Modern money is not backed by gold; it is backed by confidence and by the state's demand for it in tax. When confidence goes, so does the money — which is what hyperinflation is.
- **Tax** pays for the shared things. **Progressive** taxes take a larger share from the better-off; **flat** taxes take the same share; **regressive** ones, like taxes on necessities, fall hardest on the poor. Which mix is fair is a genuine argument, not a technical question.
- **Public and private.** Some things are run by the state, some by companies, some by neither — charities, mutuals, co-operatives, a village hall committee. Each has its failure mode.
- **Trade** lets places specialise in what they do best, which makes everyone richer on average — and makes them dependent on each other, which is a strength until a supply chain breaks. Both halves are true.
- **Markets do some things very well** — matching supply to demand, and rewarding useful work — and some things badly: anything where the cost lands on somebody who is not party to the deal, like pollution, or where one seller has no competition.

## How power goes wrong, and what checks it

Corruption, capture by a wealthy few, censorship, rigged or cancelled elections, secret police, emergency powers that never end. It rarely arrives announced; it arrives as a reasonable-sounding exception during a crisis.

**The checks that have actually worked:**

- **Separation of powers** — those who make the law, those who carry it out and those who judge it are different people.
- **Elections that can actually remove a government**, which is the real test — not whether a vote is held, but whether power ever changes hands because of one.
- **A free press and free speech**, including speech the government hates.
- **Independent courts**, and the government losing cases in them.
- **Term limits, records that are public, and accounts that are audited.**
- **Distributed power** — local government, unions, churches, clubs, co-operatives. A society thick with small organisations is much harder to dominate than one where everything runs through the centre.

**The warning signs**, worth being able to name: the courts attacked or packed; journalists imprisoned; elections postponed; emergency powers extended; one group blamed for everything. Teach the list without reference to any current argument, and it will be useful for the rest of the child's life.

## Making decisions in a household or village

Here is the practical end, and it works at any scale from four people to four hundred.

**Running a meeting:**

1. **A stated purpose** — what is to be decided today. Circulated beforehand if possible.
2. **Someone in the chair**, whose job is to see that everyone is heard and nobody dominates — not to win the argument.
3. **The question stated and written down** before any discussion.
4. **Facts first, then preferences.** Establish what is actually true, and send someone to check if nobody knows.
5. **Everyone speaks once before anyone speaks twice.** This one rule does more for a meeting than all the others together.
6. **Decide how you will decide**, before you argue about the answer: consensus, a majority vote, or one person's call because it is their responsibility.
7. **Write down what was decided, who does what by when, and the date to review it.**

**On deciding how to decide:** consensus is best for anything everyone must actually co-operate on, because a vote leaves a losing side who may not carry it out. A vote is better when a decision must be made and agreement will not come. One person's call is right when it is their job and their consequences.

**And two rules for afterwards:** the minority who lost should be able to say so and remain in good standing; and a decision that turns out badly gets reviewed, not defended. A group that cannot admit a decision went wrong will make the next one worse.

## Exercises

1. What are the three questions every society must answer? Answer each one for your own household.
2. For each form of government in the table, give its main strength and its main weakness in your own words.
3. What are the three questions to ask about any government, whatever it calls itself? Why do they tell you more than the label?
4. Describe how a law is made in Britain, from proposal to statute.
5. What is first past the post? Give one argument for it and one against, each at its strongest.
6. Explain the difference between criminal and civil law, including the different standards of proof, with an example of each.
7. Take four legal principles from this lesson. For each, describe exactly what could happen to an innocent person without it.
8. Which rights are absolute and which are qualified? Give an example of a limit on a qualified right that you think is justified, and one you think is not.
9. "A right implies a duty on somebody else." Explain with two examples.
10. Why is the treatment of a minority a better test of a democracy than the size of a majority?
11. What actually gives money its value today? What happens when that fails?
12. Progressive, flat and regressive taxes: explain each, and argue for the one you think fairest against the strongest objection to it.
13. Give one thing markets do well and one thing they do badly, with an example of each.
14. List six checks on power. For each, say what happens when it is removed.
15. Name four warning signs that a government is becoming unaccountable.
16. Chair a real meeting in your household about a real decision. Use all seven steps. Write the decision, who does what, and the review date. Afterwards, write half a page on what worked and what did not.
17. When is consensus better than a vote, and when is a vote better than consensus? Give a real example of each from your own life.

## Answers

1. Who decides; how disputes are settled; what happens to those who cannot fend for themselves. Household answers will vary — the value is in noticing that a household is a small government.
2. Look for genuine engagement rather than repetition of the table: e.g. direct democracy gives everyone a say but does not scale and can trample minorities; a dictatorship can act fast but cannot be removed without violence.
3. How does power change hands; what can it not do; what happens to somebody who says it is wrong. The label is what a state calls itself, and any state can call itself a democracy; these three describe what it actually does.
4. A bill is proposed, debated and voted through several readings in the Commons, then the Lords, which may revise or delay but not ultimately block a Commons majority; then it receives royal assent and becomes an act.
5. The candidate with the most votes in each constituency wins the seat. For: simple, gives a clear local representative and usually a decisive government rather than endless coalitions. Against: a party can win a large majority of seats with well under half the votes, and votes for losing candidates count for nothing.
6. Criminal: an offence against society, prosecuted by the state, proved beyond reasonable doubt, punished — e.g. theft. Civil: a dispute between parties, brought by one of them, decided on the balance of probabilities, ending in compensation or an order — e.g. a dispute over a boundary.
7. Look for concrete harms: without innocent-until-proven-guilty, you must prove a negative or be punished; without no-punishment-without-law, you can be punished for something lawful when you did it; without an independent judge, the accuser decides; without an appeal, one error is final.
8. Absolute: freedom from torture and slavery. Qualified: speech, movement, assembly, religion. The two examples and the reasoning are the exercise — mark the argument, not the position.
9. Your right to a fair trial requires other people to fund, staff and run courts; your right to property requires everyone else to refrain from taking it. Rights are claims on others, not just permissions.
10. Because a majority can always protect itself by voting, while a minority cannot. How a system treats those who cannot outvote anyone shows whether it has real limits on power or only counts heads.
11. Confidence that others will accept it, and the state's requirement that taxes be paid in it. When confidence fails, prices spiral and people revert to barter or a foreign currency — hyperinflation.
12. Progressive takes a larger share from higher incomes; flat takes the same share from all; regressive takes a larger share from the poor, as taxes on necessities do. Mark the argument and whether the objection was faced honestly.
13. Well: matching supply to demand quickly without anyone directing it — prices rise, more is produced. Badly: anything where the cost falls on a third party, like a factory polluting a river, or where a single seller has no competition.
14. Separation of powers, real elections, free press, independent courts, term limits and public accounts, distributed local organisations. Removing each: the same people make and judge the law; power never changes hands; wrongdoing is unreported; the government cannot lose a case; officials stay indefinitely and are unaudited; everything runs through a centre that is easy to capture.
15. Courts attacked or packed; journalists imprisoned or outlets closed; elections postponed or manipulated; emergency powers extended past the emergency; one group blamed for all problems; opponents prosecuted for opposing.
16. No fixed answer. The written decision, the named responsibilities and the review date are what to check.
17. Consensus when everyone must co-operate to carry it out, because a vote leaves a losing side who may not. A vote when a decision must be made by a deadline and agreement will not come, or when the group is too large for consensus.
