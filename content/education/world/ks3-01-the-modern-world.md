# The modern world and how it was made

**What they need first:** *Britain from the Stone Age to now* and *The ancient world*, for the timeline this sits on the end of.

Nearly everything that shapes an ordinary life today was decided in the last five hundred years: who owns land, what a country is, what rights a person has, where the food and fuel come from, and what a machine can do instead of a person. This lesson is the story of that, and it is deliberately not only a British story.

**A word on how to teach it.** Much of what follows is genuinely contested, and some of it was terrible. Do not flatten it into a list of achievements, and do not flatten it into a list of crimes either. Give the child the events, the evidence and the arguments, and let them reason. A history lesson that tells you what to conclude is not teaching history; it is teaching obedience.

## Printing and the scientific revolution, 1450–1700

**[The printing press](wiki:Printing_press)**, about 1450. Before it, a book was copied by hand, took months, and cost as much as a house. Within fifty years there were millions of books in Europe. Ideas could now travel faster than the authorities could suppress them — which is precisely why the [Reformation](wiki:Reformation) succeeded in 1517 when earlier attempts at the same argument had failed.

**The scientific revolution.** [Copernicus](wiki:Nicolaus_Copernicus) put the sun at the centre; [Galileo](wiki:Galileo_Galilei) looked through a telescope and reported what he saw rather than what he was supposed to see; [Newton](wiki:Isaac_Newton) showed that the same laws govern a falling apple and an orbiting moon. The lasting invention was not any one discovery but the **method**: propose, test, publish, let others check. It is the best tool for finding out anything that anyone has ever devised, and it is the subject of *How to find things out*.

## Empire, slavery and exchange, 1500–1900

European ships reached the Americas, Africa and Asia; within four centuries a handful of European states claimed most of the world.

**The exchange of crops changed everyone's diet permanently.** Potatoes and maize went east and fed Europe's growing population; wheat, cattle and horses went west. So did diseases: smallpox and measles killed a catastrophic proportion of the peoples of the Americas — in many regions the great majority — and that, more than any battle, is why the conquest succeeded.

**[The Atlantic slave trade](wiki:Atlantic_slave_trade)** carried roughly twelve million people from Africa to the Americas in chains over three centuries. Millions died on the voyage. It was legal, profitable, and defended at the time by respectable people with arguments they believed. Britain's ports and much of its early industrial capital grew from it. It was ended — in law — by a long campaign led in part by formerly enslaved people who wrote and spoke about what had been done to them, and by revolt in the colonies themselves; Britain abolished the trade in 1807 and slavery in most of the empire in 1833, and the United States only after a civil war in 1865.

Teach this plainly and at proper length. It is not a footnote to the history of industry; it is part of the same story. And note what it shows: **that something is legal, normal and profitable is no evidence at all that it is right** — which is a lesson about the present as much as the past.

**Empire** brought railways, law codes and schools to some places; it also brought famine, extraction, drawn borders that ignored the people inside them, and conquest. Both statements are true, and asking which outweighs the other is a real question that historians still argue. Give the child the evidence for each.

## Industry, 1750–1900

Coal, steam, iron and machinery. Britain first, then Europe, America and Japan.

- **What it produced:** more goods than had ever existed; railways; cheaper cloth and tools; more food moved further; medicine; eventually much longer lives.
- **What it cost:** child labour, twelve-hour days, towns without drains, ruined air and rivers, and communities broken up as people left the land. Life expectancy in the worst industrial cities *fell* for a generation.
- **What followed:** trade unions, factory acts, public sanitation, compulsory schooling, and the vote. These were fought for, decade by decade, not granted.

The pattern is worth naming, because it keeps recurring: **a new technology arrives, the gains and the damage land on different people, and the argument that follows is about who pays.**

## Revolutions and the idea of rights

- **America, 1776** — colonies break from Britain, declaring that people have rights a government may not remove. Written while many of its authors held slaves, which is a contradiction worth sitting with rather than explaining away.
- **France, 1789** — a monarchy overthrown in the name of liberty, equality and fraternity, followed by terror and then an emperor. Revolutions do not reliably end where they aimed.
- **Across the 1800s** — the vote extended, slowly and with resistance; unions legalised; schooling made compulsory.
- **Votes for women** — campaigned for over decades, achieved in Britain in 1918 (limited) and 1928 (equal).
- **The 1900s** — independence movements across Asia and Africa, the American civil rights movement, and the idea, new in the twentieth century, of rights that belong to everyone everywhere.

## The wars

**[The First World War](wiki:World_War_I), 1914–18.** A tangle of alliances turned a murder in Sarajevo into a war that killed about seventeen million people. Industrial machinery met nineteenth-century tactics, and the result was trenches, machine guns and gas. Four empires fell. The settlement afterwards punished Germany in ways that helped cause the next war — which is the clearest case in history of a peace being made badly.

**[The Second World War](wiki:World_War_II), 1939–45.** Roughly seventy to eighty-five million dead, most of them civilians. **[The Holocaust](wiki:The_Holocaust):** the deliberate, industrialised murder of six million Jews, and of Roma, disabled people, prisoners of war, gay men and political opponents. It was carried out by an educated, modern state, using ordinary bureaucracy — timetables, forms, clerks. That is the fact that must not be softened: it was not done by monsters in a dark age, and that is exactly why it must be remembered and taught.

The war ended with the first use of nuclear weapons, and with [the United Nations](wiki:United_Nations), [the Universal Declaration of Human Rights](wiki:Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights) and [the Geneva Conventions](wiki:Geneva_Conventions) — attempts to make rules for what states may do, which work imperfectly and are better than nothing.

## Since 1945

- **The end of empire.** Most colonies became independent states within thirty years, some peacefully, some after long wars. Many borders drawn by outsiders are still causing conflict.
- **[The Cold War](wiki:Cold_War)**, 1947–1991 — two armed blocs, proxy wars, and the permanent possibility of nuclear annihilation.
- **In Britain:** [the National Health Service](wiki:National_Health_Service|Health_care) (1948), mass housebuilding, the motor car, television, and a standard of living that would have astonished a Victorian.
- **Technology:** [antibiotics](wiki:Antibiotic), [vaccination](wiki:Vaccination) against diseases that had killed children for all of history, [the contraceptive pill](wiki:Combined_oral_contraceptive_pill|Birth_control), the jet, the satellite, the computer and the internet. More information is now available to an ordinary person than to any king in history.
- **The costs being counted now:** a climate changed by two centuries of burning coal and oil, depleted soils and seas, and a global system in which everybody depends on supply chains almost nobody understands.

That last point is the one to end on, because it is the argument for this vault: **a society can be enormously capable and, at the same time, fragile in ways it does not notice**, because the capability is spread across systems no single person can see or repair.

## How to think about it

- **Judge by evidence, not by side.** The question is what happened and why, not who to cheer for.
- **Beware hindsight.** People decided with what they knew then. Asking what they could reasonably have known is fairer and more interesting than asking why they were not us.
- **Look for who benefited and who paid.** Almost every change in this lesson helped somebody and cost somebody else.
- **Ordinary people are in the story.** Most history is not kings; it is what people ate, what work they did, and what they could and could not say.
- **It is not over.** Everything here is still being argued about, and the argument shapes what is done now.

## Exercises

1. Put in order: [the French Revolution](wiki:French_Revolution), the printing press, the Second World War, the abolition of slavery in the British Empire, the first railways, the Reformation.
2. Explain why the printing press made the Reformation possible when similar arguments a century earlier had failed.
3. What was the lasting invention of the scientific revolution? Explain why the method mattered more than any single discovery.
4. Why did European conquest of the Americas succeed so completely? Give the reason that mattered more than weapons.
5. Name three crops that moved between continents after 1500 and say what each changed.
6. Roughly how many people were carried across the Atlantic in the slave trade, over what period, and how did it end?
7. Slavery was legal, profitable and defended by respectable people. What does that tell you about using legality or normality as evidence that something is right? Give one present-day question where the same caution applies.
8. Give two things empire built and two things it destroyed. Then say what evidence you would want in order to judge a particular colony.
9. List three gains and three costs of industrialisation. Who received the gains and who paid the costs?
10. Name four rights or protections won between 1800 and 1930, and say how each was achieved.
11. Explain how the settlement after the First World War contributed to the Second.
12. The Holocaust was carried out by a modern, educated state using ordinary administration. Why is that fact important, and why is it the part that must not be softened?
13. What was set up after 1945 to limit what states may do to people? Give two examples and say honestly how well they have worked.
14. Name four things available to an ordinary person today that no king had in 1900.
15. Explain the argument that a society can be highly capable and fragile at the same time. Give two examples from your own household of things you depend on but could not repair or replace.
16. Choose one event from this lesson and write a page on it from two opposed points of view, each argued as well as you can. Then say which you find more convincing and why.

## Answers

1. Printing press (about 1450), Reformation (1517), French Revolution (1789), first railways (1820s–30s), abolition in the British Empire (1833), Second World War (1939–45).
2. Printing meant an argument could be copied thousands of times, cheaply and fast, in the language people actually spoke. Authorities could no longer suppress it by seizing a few handwritten copies — the ideas outran them.
3. The method: propose an explanation, test it, publish it, and let others check. Any single discovery can be wrong, but a method that catches its own errors keeps producing new knowledge indefinitely.
4. Disease — smallpox and measles, to which the peoples of the Americas had no prior exposure, killed a catastrophic proportion of the population, in many regions the great majority, before and during the conquest.
5. Potatoes and maize to Europe (far more calories per acre, supporting population growth); wheat, cattle, horses to the Americas (changed farming and warfare); also sugar, which drove the plantation system and the slave trade.
6. Roughly twelve million people over about three centuries. It was ended by a long abolition campaign — including formerly enslaved writers and speakers — and by revolt in the colonies; Britain abolished the trade in 1807 and slavery in most of the empire in 1833, the United States after the civil war in 1865.
7. That legality and normality are facts about a society, not arguments about right and wrong — plenty of legal, normal, profitable things have later been judged monstrous. Accept any thoughtful present-day example; the reasoning matters, not the topic.
8. Built: railways, ports, some schools and hospitals, written law codes. Destroyed: local industries, self-government, lives in famines and wars, and borders that ignored who lived inside them. Evidence wanted: what happened to population, food supply, literacy, income and self-rule, before, during and after — and accounts from the people who lived there.
9. Gains: more goods, cheaper cloth and tools, railways, food moved further, eventually longer lives. Costs: child labour, terrible hours, disease in unsanitary towns, polluted air and water, communities broken. The gains went first to owners and later, unevenly, to everyone; the costs fell first on workers and on the places that were mined and polluted.
10. Any four of: factory acts limiting hours and child labour; legal trade unions; public sanitation; compulsory schooling; the widening male franchise; votes for women (1918, 1928). All were achieved by long campaigning, striking, petitioning and protest, against resistance — none were simply granted.
11. The settlement imposed heavy reparations, territorial losses and blame on Germany, contributing to economic collapse and humiliation that extreme politics exploited. It is the clearest example of a peace made badly.
12. Because it shows that industrial-scale murder does not require a primitive society or obvious monsters — it can be done by an advanced state through ordinary clerks, timetables and forms. Softening it would remove the warning, which is precisely that ordinary institutions and ordinary people can be turned to it.
13. The United Nations, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the Geneva Conventions, later international courts. Honestly: they have constrained some behaviour, given people language and a standard to appeal to, and failed repeatedly to prevent atrocities — better than nothing, and far from sufficient.
14. Antibiotics, vaccination, anaesthetic dentistry, clean piped water, electric light, refrigeration, fast travel, instant communication, recorded music, an encyclopedia in the house.
15. Capability comes from specialisation and long supply chains: each person does one thing well and depends on strangers for everything else. That is enormously productive and it means very few people can repair or replace what they use, so a break anywhere propagates. Accept any honest household examples — most people cannot repair the things they most depend on.
16. Mark it on whether *both* sides are argued at their strongest. A weak version of the side the writer disagrees with is the failure mode; see the steel man in *Speaking and debate*.
