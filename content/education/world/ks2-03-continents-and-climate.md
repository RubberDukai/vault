# The continents and the climate

**What they need first:** *Reading a map* for scale, symbols and grid references. This lesson zooms out from the parish to the planet.

A child should be able to shut their eyes and see the world: where the continents are, which way the wind comes from, why it rains here and not there. That picture is what makes news, weather, food and history make sense — and it is built in an afternoon with a ball, a torch and a bowl of water.

## The globe

**Seven continents:** Africa, Antarctica, Asia, Europe, North America, Oceania (Australia and the Pacific islands), South America.
**Five oceans:** Pacific (by far the biggest), Atlantic, Indian, Southern, Arctic.

Draw the world from memory once a week for a month. It will be dreadful and then it will be good, and a child who can sketch it freehand owns it in a way no amount of looking at an atlas achieves.

**The lines:**

- **[The Equator](wiki:Equator)** — 0° latitude, round the middle.
- **[Latitude](wiki:Latitude)** — how far north or south, from 0° at the Equator to 90° at each pole. The lines run *across*, parallel, like the rungs of a ladder.
- **[Longitude](wiki:Longitude)** — how far east or west, from 0° at Greenwich to 180° on the far side. The lines run *pole to pole*, meeting at the top and bottom.
- **The Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn** at 23.5° north and south — the furthest the sun ever gets overhead.
- **The Arctic and Antarctic Circles** at 66.5° — where there is at least one day a year with no sunset, and one with no sunrise.

**Remembering which is which:** latitude is *flat* — the lines lie flat across the map. That one word settles a confusion that otherwise lasts years.

**Time zones** follow longitude, because the Earth turns 360° in 24 hours — 15° an hour. When it is noon at Greenwich it is early morning in New York and evening in Delhi. The world is not in one day at once, and a child who grasps that has grasped that the Earth turns.

## Why it is hot at the Equator and cold at the poles

This is the key that unlocks the whole lesson, and it is one experiment.

Take a torch and a ball. Shine the torch straight at the middle of the ball: a small, bright, concentrated circle. Now shine it at the top of the ball: the same light is smeared over a much larger, dimmer patch. **The same sunlight, spread over more ground, warms it less.** That is all "climate" means at bottom.

Add the second fact: the Earth is **tilted by 23.5°**, and keeps pointing the same way as it goes round the sun. So for half the year the northern half leans towards the sun — long days, steep light, summer — and for the other half it leans away. The seasons are not about being nearer the sun; they are about the angle and the length of the day. Demonstrate it with the ball tilted, walked round a lamp. Do not skip this; almost every adult gets it wrong.

Three more things that shift a local climate:

- **Height.** It gets colder as you go up, roughly 1 °C for every 150 m. That is why there is snow on the equator's mountains.
- **The sea.** Water heats and cools slowly, so coasts have mild winters and cool summers, and inland places have extremes. Britain is far further north than most people guess — level with Labrador — and is only habitable as it is because of a warm ocean current.
- **Mountains in the way.** Air forced up a mountain cools, drops its rain on the way up, and comes down the other side dry. Wet on one side, a rain shadow on the other. That is why the west of Britain is green and the east is drier.

## Wind and rain

Warm air rises; cool air sinks; air moves from where there is more of it to where there is less, and that movement is wind.

At the Equator the sun's heat lifts wet air, which cools as it rises and dumps its water — so there is rainforest in a belt round the middle of the world. That air, now dry, travels high above the surface and sinks again at about 30° north and south — and sinking dry air makes deserts. Look at the globe: the Sahara, Arabia, the Kalahari, central Australia and the American south-west all sit in that same band. **The deserts are where they are for a reason, and the child can see the reason on the map.**

**The water cycle** drives all of it: evaporation from sea and land → condensation into cloud → precipitation as rain, hail or snow → run-off through streams and rivers → back to the sea. (There is more on this in *Earth, water and weather*.)

## Biomes: the world's main kinds of place

| Biome | Where | What it is like |
|---|---|---|
| **[Tropical rainforest](wiki:Rainforest)** | on the Equator | hot, wet all year, the greatest variety of life anywhere |
| **Desert** | about 30° N and S | very dry, huge day-to-night temperature swings |
| **Savannah/grassland** | between the two | wet season and dry season, big grazing herds |
| **Temperate forest** | 40–60°, incl. Britain | four seasons, rain all year, deciduous trees |
| **Boreal forest (taiga)** | 50–70° N | long hard winters, conifers, the largest forest on Earth |
| **Tundra** | near the Arctic | frozen ground, no trees, brief burst of summer |
| **Polar** | the caps | ice, almost no rain — technically deserts |
| **Mountain** | any latitude, high up | bands of climate stacked up the slope |

**Climate is not weather.** Weather is what happened today; climate is what usually happens over thirty years. A cold week says nothing about a climate, and confusing the two is the commonest mistake in any argument about it.

## The shape of the land

- **Rivers** run from high to low, wearing a V-shaped valley near the source, meandering across a flood plain lower down, and dropping their silt at a delta or estuary. People have always settled on them: water, transport, power, fish, and flood-rich soil — and the price is the flood itself.
- **Mountains** are built where the plates of the Earth's crust push into each other. The Himalayas are still growing, a few millimetres a year, because India is still pushing into Asia.
- **[Volcanoes](wiki:Volcano) and earthquakes** happen mostly where those plates meet — draw them on a world map and the plate edges appear, especially the ring round the Pacific. People still live there, because volcanic soil is superb.
- **[Glaciers](wiki:Glacier)** carved a great deal of northern Britain into U-shaped valleys and left the boulders and gravel your soil is made of.
- **Coasts** are worn away in one place and built up in another, all the time.

## Where people are, and why

Most people live where there is fresh water, workable soil, a tolerable temperature, a flat enough site and a way of getting goods in and out. Very few live in deserts, high mountains, deep forest or on ice. Put a world map of population beside a world map of rainfall and the two are close to the same picture.

And it moves: people follow water, soil and safety. Drought, flood, war and failed harvests move people now exactly as they did in the ancient world.

## Exercises

1. Draw the world from memory. Label seven continents and five oceans. Do it again a week later and compare.
2. Which is which, and how do you remember: latitude or longitude runs across the map?
3. Shine a torch straight at a ball, then at an angle. Describe the two patches of light and explain what it tells you about the Equator and the poles.
4. Why is it summer in Britain when it is winter in Australia? (The answer is not "we are nearer the sun".)
5. It is noon in Greenwich. Roughly what time is it 45° east? 60° west? Show your working.
6. Why is there snow on mountains at the Equator?
7. Explain, using rising and sinking air, why there is rainforest at 0° and desert at about 30°. Then find four deserts on the map that sit in that band.
8. Why is the west of Britain wetter than the east?
9. Why does the sea keep a coast mild in winter and cool in summer?
10. Name the biome: hot and wet all year, enormous variety of life. Frozen ground and no trees. Wet season and dry season with grazing herds. Conifers and long hard winters.
11. What is the difference between weather and climate? Why is a single cold week not evidence about a climate?
12. Describe a river from its source to the sea, naming three things that change along the way.
13. Why do people go on living near volcanoes?
14. Give five things that make a place good to live in, and use them to explain why very few people live in the Sahara or in Antarctica.
15. Using the Maps tab: find your own latitude and longitude. Then name a place at roughly the same latitude on another continent, and say whether you would expect its climate to be similar — and why it might not be.

## Answers

1. No fixed answer — compare the two attempts; the second should be markedly better.
2. Latitude runs across — it is *flat*. Longitude runs pole to pole.
3. Straight on: a small, bright, concentrated patch. At an angle: the same light spread over a bigger, dimmer patch. Sunlight hits the Equator nearly straight on and the poles at a glancing angle, so the same energy is spread thinner and warms less.
4. Because the Earth is tilted 23.5° and keeps pointing the same way as it orbits. When the northern half leans towards the sun it gets steeper light and longer days — summer — while the southern half leans away and has winter.
5. 15° per hour. 45° east is 3 hours ahead: about 3 p.m. 60° west is 4 hours behind: about 8 a.m.
6. Air gets colder with height — roughly 1 °C per 150 m — so a high enough mountain is below freezing whatever its latitude.
7. At the Equator strong heating lifts wet air; as it rises it cools, cannot hold its water, and rains — rainforest. That air, now dry, sinks again around 30° N and S, and sinking dry air gives no rain — desert. Four of: Sahara, Arabian, Kalahari, Australian, Atacama, Mojave/Sonoran.
8. The wet westerly wind off the Atlantic is forced up over the western hills, cools and drops its rain there; by the time it reaches the east it has little left. The east is in the rain shadow.
9. Water heats and cools far more slowly than land, so it stores summer warmth into the winter and stays cool into the summer, moderating the air above it.
10. Tropical rainforest; tundra; savannah/grassland; boreal forest (taiga).
11. Weather is the state of the atmosphere now or today; climate is the pattern over about thirty years. One week is weather — far too short a sample to say anything about the pattern.
12. Source high up, fast, narrow, V-shaped valley → middle course, wider, meandering, slower → lower course, flood plain, silt dropped, delta or estuary. Three changes: it gets wider, slower and siltier; the valley changes shape; it carries finer material.
13. Volcanic soil is extremely fertile, so farming is good; there may also be geothermal heat, minerals, and simply that it is home and eruptions are rare in a human lifetime.
14. Fresh water, workable soil, tolerable temperature, flat enough ground, a way in and out. The Sahara has almost no water and extreme heat; Antarctica has no soil, no agriculture, extreme cold and no easy access.
15. No fixed answer. A good answer names a place at the same latitude and explains that latitude alone does not settle a climate — the sea, height, ocean currents and mountains change it, which is why Britain is far milder than other places on its line.
