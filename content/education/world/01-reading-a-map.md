# Reading a map

A map is a drawing of the ground from above, made smaller. Every skill in reading one follows from that sentence: things are where they are, only shrunk, and the map tells you by how much.

**Use the Maps tab for this lesson.** Zoom to where you live. Everything below can be tried on it, then confirmed by walking outside and looking.

## Scale — how much smaller

**Scale** is how many times smaller the map is than the world. A scale of 1:25,000 means one centimetre on the map is 25,000 cm — 250 metres — on the ground. 1:50,000 means 500 metres.

The Maps tab shows a **scale bar** in the corner: a line with its real length written beside it. Zoom in and the bar represents less distance; zoom out and it represents more. Hold a finger against it, then move that finger across the map — that is how far.

**Exercise:** on the map, find your home and the nearest shop, water source, or school. Using the scale bar, estimate the distance. Then walk it, counting paces (see below). How close were you?

## Direction — which way is up

**North is at the top of the map.** Always, unless the map says otherwise. East is right, south is down, west is left.

Outside, find north with a compass, the sun (due south at midday in Britain), or the Pole Star at night. Then **turn the map** so its north points the same way. Now everything on the map is in the same direction as everything on the ground, and you can look from one to the other. This is called **orienting the map** and it is the single most useful habit in navigation.

**Exercise:** stand somewhere with a view. Orient the map. Pick three things you can see — a hill, a church, a road junction — and find each on the map. Then pick something on the map and find it in the view.

## Symbols — what the marks mean

Maps use colours and small pictures. On the Maps tab: blue is water, green is woods and parks, thin lines are paths, thicker ones roads, orange ones main roads. Buildings are grey blocks. A pin with a label is a point of interest, and the categories can be switched on and off.

There is no universal key — every map has its own, and a printed map has it in the margin. Read the key before the map.

**Exercise:** find, on the map, a river, a wood, a footpath, a main road, a church or place of worship, and a hospital. Switch the point-of-interest categories off one at a time and watch what disappears.

## Grid references — saying exactly where

A map is covered by an invisible grid of squares, numbered along the bottom (**eastings**) and up the side (**northings**). A point is named by its numbers: **along the corridor, then up the stairs** — east first, then north.

The Maps tab shows the position under the cursor as **latitude and longitude** in the corner — the world's grid, in degrees. Latitude is how far north (54.2 is just south of the Lake District); longitude how far east or west (−2.8 is 2.8 degrees west of Greenwich). Two numbers name any point on Earth, which is how a place can be written down, sent by radio, or typed into the "Go to" box.

**Exercise:** hover over your home and write down its latitude and longitude. Type them into "Go to." Did the marker land on your house? Now write down the coordinates of a place a friend would recognise, and give them only the numbers.

## Contours — seeing the hills

On a printed walking map, thin brown lines join points of equal height. **Close together means steep; far apart means flat.** A ring of them is a hill; a ring inside a ring is its top. Lines bending like a V pointing uphill mark a valley; pointing downhill, a ridge.

Read contours and you can see, from a flat piece of paper, where the climb is, where the water runs, where a stream will be, where it is too steep to walk. The Maps tab does not draw contours yet, but the rivers do half the job: water is always at the bottom of the valley, so where the blue lines run, the ground is lowest.

## Pacing — measuring with your legs

Count how many double-steps (left foot each time) you take over a measured 100 metres. Most adults take 60–70; children more. That number is yours. Now you can measure any distance by walking it, and check any map estimate by pacing it.

**Exercise:** measure 100 m with a tape or the map's scale bar. Walk it three times, counting. Take the average. Write it in the back of your notebook. It will change as you grow.

## Finding yourself when lost

1. **Stop.** Moving while lost makes it worse.
2. **Orient the map** to north.
3. **Look for something you can identify** — a river, a road, a hill, a building — and find it on the map. Two such things, and where lines from them cross is roughly where you are. Three, and you are sure.
4. If you can identify nothing: **follow water downhill.** Streams lead to rivers, rivers lead to people.
5. If it is getting dark or cold: **stop and shelter** before finding the way. The shelter chapter says how.

## Exercises

1. Two places are 6 cm apart on a 1:25,000 map. How far apart are they on the ground?
2. On the Maps tab, measure the distance from your home to the nearest river using the Measure tool. Then estimate it from the scale bar. How close were you?
3. Give the latitude and longitude of three places you know. Have someone else find them from the numbers alone.
4. Draw a map of your house and garden, from above, with a scale bar and a north arrow. Give it to someone and ask them to find a hidden object using only the map.
5. Orient the map outside and identify three landmarks. Then, from a different spot, do it again. Could you tell where you had moved to?
6. Explain, in one sentence, why a stream on the map tells you where the low ground is.

## Answers

1. 6 × 25,000 = 150,000 cm = **1.5 km**.
2. No fixed answer. On a 1:25,000 map, an estimate from the scale bar should be within about 10% of the measured figure; a bigger gap usually means the scale bar was read from the wrong end or the route was measured straight-line while the walk is not.
3. No fixed answer. Check they wrote latitude first and longitude second, with north/south and east/west (or a sign), and enough decimal places to be useful — three decimal places is about 100 m.
4. Look for: a scale bar, a north arrow, a plan view from above rather than a picture from the side, and enough fixed features (walls, doors, trees) to locate anything by. The test is whether the other person finds the object.
5. Orienting means turning the map until its north matches real north, so everything on the paper lies in the direction it really lies. From a second spot the same landmarks appear in different directions, and where the lines back from two or three of them cross is where you are — that is a resection, and yes, it tells you where you have moved to.
6. Water runs downhill, so a stream marks the lowest line of its valley — which also means the contours around it point uphill, back upstream.
