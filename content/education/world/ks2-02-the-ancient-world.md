# The ancient world

**What they need first:** *Britain from the Stone Age to now*, so there is already a timeline to hang these on. It helps enormously to notice that Stonehenge was already old when the pyramids were being built.

Britain's story is not the world's. While Britain was raising earth banks, cities of eighty thousand people were doing algebra in Mesopotamia. Teaching both puts the child's own country in its place — one corner of a large, busy world — and shows that almost everything we use was invented by somebody, somewhere, and could have been invented differently.

## The pattern that repeats

Every one of these civilisations grew the same way, and it is worth drawing out before the names start:

1. A **river** that floods and leaves rich silt — the Tigris and Euphrates, the Nile, the Indus, the Yellow River.
2. **Farming** on that silt produces more food than the farmers need.
3. The surplus feeds people who **do not farm**: priests, scribes, soldiers, potters, smiths, builders.
4. Surplus has to be **counted and stored**, so somebody invents **writing** and **number**.
5. Counting and storing need somebody in charge, so there are **rulers, laws, taxes and armies**.
6. What is built gets big, because there are now many hands and a reason.

So: **city, writing, law and army all come out of grain.** If a child takes one idea away from this lesson, that is the one.

## The first cities

**[Mesopotamia](wiki:Mesopotamia), from about 3500 BC** — "between the rivers", in what is now Iraq. The [Sumerians](wiki:Sumer) built the first true cities, and with them the first **writing** ([cuneiform](wiki:Cuneiform), pressed into clay with a reed), the **wheel**, the plough, irrigation canals, the sailing boat, and arithmetic. Their tablets are mostly not poetry but *receipts* — so many jars of oil, so many sheep — because writing was invented to keep accounts. Later came [Babylon](wiki:Babylon), [Hammurabi's law code](wiki:Code_of_Hammurabi) carved on stone for everyone to see, and the counting in sixties that still gives us 60 seconds, 60 minutes and 360 degrees.

**[Ancient Egypt](wiki:Ancient_Egypt), from about 3100 BC** — the Nile flooded on a schedule, so the year could be measured; a 365-day calendar followed, and so did surveying, because the flood wiped out the field boundaries every year and they had to be re-set. [Hieroglyphs](wiki:Egyptian_hieroglyphs), papyrus, medicine, quarrying, and the [pyramids](wiki:Egyptian_pyramids) — built with ramps, levers, water, rope and an enormous organisation of ordinary paid workers, not by magic and not by slaves as the old stories have it.

**The [Indus Valley](wiki:Indus_Valley_Civilisation), from about 2600 BC** — in modern Pakistan and north-west India. Cities on a grid, with brick drains, public baths, standard weights and, apparently, no palaces and no great temples. We still cannot read their writing, which is a good lesson in itself: a whole civilisation whose own account of itself is lost.

**[Ancient China](wiki:History_of_China), from about 1600 BC** — the longest continuous written culture on Earth. Bronze casting of a quality nobody matched, silk, the compass, paper, gunpowder, printing, the crossbow, cast iron a thousand years before Europe, and a system of examinations for entering government.

## Greece and Rome

**[Ancient Greece](wiki:Ancient_Greece), about 800–300 BC** — not one country but many quarrelsome city-states, chiefly [Athens](wiki:Athens) and [Sparta](wiki:Sparta). From them: **[democracy](wiki:Athenian_democracy)** — though only free men could vote, which is worth saying plainly — theatre, history as a discipline, the [Olympic Games](wiki:Ancient_Olympic_Games), and the habit of asking *why* rather than *who says so*. [Geometry](wiki:Euclid) was put on a footing that still stands, the Earth's size was measured from shadows, and [Socrates](wiki:Socrates), [Plato](wiki:Plato) and [Aristotle](wiki:Aristotle) began nearly every argument Western thought has had since.

**[Ancient Rome](wiki:Ancient_Rome), about 500 BC–AD 476** — first a republic, then an empire that ran from Scotland to the Sahara. Romans were the great **engineers and organisers**: concrete, the arch, aqueducts, 80,000 km of roads, sanitation, a professional army, and above all **law** — the idea that the rule is written down and applies to everyone, which is the direct ancestor of most law today. Latin became French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese and Romanian, and left half of English behind it.

## The rest of the world, which is usually left out

- **Africa.** [Nubia and Kush](wiki:Kingdom_of_Kush) built more pyramids than Egypt. [Aksum](wiki:Kingdom_of_Aksum) in Ethiopia traded across the Red Sea and minted its own coin. Later, [Mali](wiki:Mali_Empire) and [Timbuktu](wiki:Timbuktu) were centres of gold and scholarship, and [Great Zimbabwe](wiki:Great_Zimbabwe) was built in stone without mortar.
- **The Americas**, developing entirely separately, with no wheel for transport and no horses: the [Maya](wiki:Maya_civilization) with a written script, astronomy and a place-value number system including zero; the [Aztecs](wiki:Aztecs) with a city built on a lake and floating gardens; the [Inca](wiki:Inca_Empire) with terraced mountain farming, suspension bridges, thousands of miles of road and record-keeping on knotted cords, having never invented writing as we would recognise it.
- **[The Polynesians](wiki:Polynesian_navigation)** crossed thousands of miles of open Pacific in canoes, navigating by stars, swell, cloud and bird, and settled islands from Hawaii to New Zealand. It is probably the greatest feat of navigation in history, done with no instrument at all.

Teach these alongside Greece and Rome, not after them. A child should not grow up thinking civilisation is a thing that happened around the Mediterranean.

## Four inventions that changed everything

- **Writing.** Knowledge stops dying with the person who has it. Everything else compounds from there — and it is exactly what this vault is for.
- **The wheel.** Not obvious; the Americas never used it for transport and did perfectly well. It needs a hard road and a draught animal to be worth having.
- **Metal.** Copper, then bronze (copper plus tin — which means *trade*, because the two are rarely found together), then iron. Iron is common, so once you can smelt it everyone can have a good tool, not just the rich.
- **Farming**, which came first and caused the rest.

## Why they fell, and what survived

Rome did not end in a single year. It was ground down over two centuries by too much to defend, too few taxpayers, plague, civil war, a currency debased until nobody trusted it, and people arriving at the borders in numbers.

**What survives a fall is what somebody keeps using or keeps copying.** Roman law and the Latin language survived because the Church went on using them. Greek mathematics and medicine survived because Arabic scholars translated, corrected and extended them — and came back to Europe through Spain centuries later. The Indus script survived not at all, because nobody kept reading it.

That is the practical lesson of the whole subject, and it is the reason this vault exists: **knowledge is not kept by being written once. It is kept by being copied, used and taught.**

## Exercises

1. Put in order, earliest first: the Roman Empire, the first cities of Sumer, Ancient Greece's democracy, the Egyptian pyramids, the Inca Empire.
2. Give the five steps by which a river leads to a city with a king, an army and a written language.
3. What were the first written documents mostly about, and what does that tell you about why writing was invented?
4. Name three things we still use today that come from Mesopotamian counting in sixties.
5. Why did the Egyptians need surveyors every single year?
6. The Indus Valley cities had drains, standard weights and a grid plan, but no palaces. What might that suggest about how they were run? Why can we not be sure?
7. Name four Chinese inventions that reached Europe centuries later.
8. Athens is called the birthplace of democracy. Who was allowed to vote, and who was not?
9. What is the single most important thing Rome left behind, and why does it matter more than the roads?
10. The Inca ran an empire of millions with no writing and no wheel. How did they keep records and move goods?
11. How did the Polynesians navigate thousands of miles of open ocean without instruments?
12. Give four reasons the Roman Empire declined. Which do you think mattered most, and why?
13. Greek mathematics survived the fall of Rome. By what route, and through whom?
14. The Indus script has never been read. What have we lost, and what does that tell you about how knowledge is kept?
15. Choose one civilisation from this lesson and write half a page on a single day in the life of an ordinary child in it.

## Answers

1. Sumer (about 3500 BC), the Egyptian pyramids (about 2600 BC), Greek democracy (about 500 BC), the Roman Empire (from about 27 BC), the Inca (from about AD 1400).
2. River floods and leaves rich silt → farming produces a surplus → people who do not farm can be fed → the surplus must be counted and stored, so writing and number are invented → rulers, laws, taxes and armies follow to control it.
3. Accounts — lists of grain, oil, sheep, and who owed what. Writing was invented for administration, not for literature; the poetry came later.
4. 60 seconds in a minute, 60 minutes in an hour, 360 degrees in a circle.
5. The Nile flooded every year and washed away the field boundaries, so land had to be re-measured and re-marked. Geometry came out of that need.
6. It may suggest a society with less concentrated power, or wealth not displayed in palaces. We cannot be sure because we cannot read their writing, so we have no account of their society in their own words.
7. Paper, printing, gunpowder, the magnetic compass; also silk, cast iron, the crossbow.
8. Free adult male citizens of Athens. Women, enslaved people and foreigners could not vote — a large majority of those living there.
9. Written law that applies to everyone. The roads wore out; the idea that a rule is recorded and applies alike to rich and poor is still the basis of most legal systems.
10. Records on knotted cords (quipu); goods moved by human porters and llamas along a road network of thousands of miles, with runners carrying messages in relays.
11. By the stars, the direction and shape of the ocean swell, cloud formations over unseen islands, the flight paths of birds, and drifting seeds and wood — all learned by heart and handed down.
12. Any four of: too much frontier to defend, plague, civil war, currency debased so trade broke down, a falling tax base, pressure from people arriving at the borders, and dividing the empire in two. The judgement matters less than the reasoning.
13. Through Arabic scholars, who translated and extended it while Europe had largely lost it, and back into Europe through Spain and Sicily centuries later.
14. Everything they said about themselves — their laws, beliefs, history and names. It tells you that writing alone does not preserve knowledge: it survives only while somebody goes on reading, copying and teaching it.
15. No fixed answer. Mark it on whether the detail is right for that place — what they ate, what work they did, what the house was made of — rather than on length.
