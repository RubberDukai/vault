# Learning to read

Everything else in this vault is locked behind this one skill. Teach it first, teach it patiently, and teach it every day in short sessions.

**Fifteen minutes daily beats two hours on Sunday.** Children learn to read through frequent, short, low-pressure repetition. Frustration is the only real enemy — stop before they get tired, always end on something they can do, and never let it become a battle.

## The principle

English writing is a code for the sounds of speech. Letters represent sounds; sounds blend into words. A child who learns this can decode words nobody has ever taught them.

Teach **sounds, not letter names.** The letter *m* says "mmm", not "em". A child taught names will try to read *cat* as "see-ay-tee" and get nowhere.

## The sequence

**Stage 1 — first sounds.** Start with letters that are common, easy to say, and make many words: **s, a, t, p, i, n**.

With just those six you can build: *sat, pat, tap, tip, sit, nap, pin, pit, tan, nip, span, snap*. That is a genuine reading experience within a week or two, which matters enormously for a child's belief that this is possible.

**Stage 2 — blending.** This is the actual skill, and the moment it clicks is unmistakable. Sound each letter, then push them together, faster each time. "sss-aaa-t... ssat... sat!"

Some children take weeks to blend. Keep going. It arrives.

**Stage 3 — more sounds.** m, d, g, o, c, k, e, u, r, h, b, f, l — then the rest.

**Stage 4 — digraphs.** Two letters, one sound: **sh, ch, th, ng, ai, ee, igh, oa, oo, ar, or, ur, ow, oi, ear, air**. Teach each as a single unit.

**Stage 5 — the tricky words.** English has words that will not decode: *the, said, was, one, two, come, some, are, you, there, where, their*. These are learned by sight. There are not many. Write them on cards and drill them lightly.

## How to run a lesson

1. **Review** yesterday's sounds — two minutes, quick.
2. **Introduce** one new sound. Say it, write it, trace it in dirt or sand or flour with a finger.
3. **Blend** three or four words using it.
4. **Read** a short sentence made of known sounds.
5. **Write** two or three words from dictation.
6. **Read to them** — something well above their own level.

That last step is not optional filler. Being read to builds vocabulary, sentence rhythm and, most importantly, the belief that books contain things worth the effort.

## Materials from nothing

- A stick and smooth dirt, or a tray of sand or ash
- Charcoal on any pale surface
- Stones or bark with letters painted on for building words
- Any printed matter at all — labels, packets, signs
- Their own name, which is almost always the first word a child wants

## Signs it is going well

They sound out a word they have never seen. They notice letters in the world — on a tin, a sign, a tool. They correct themselves mid-word. They ask what something says.

## Signs to slow down

Guessing from the first letter and the picture. Refusing to try. Reversing letters persistently past about age seven. Tears.

If it stalls, go back to a stage they are secure in and rebuild. Never push through distress — a child who decides reading is painful will avoid it for years, and that is a far more expensive problem than a slow start.

## After decoding: fluency and meaning

Decoding is not reading. Once words come out reliably:

- **Re-read the same passage** several times. Fluency comes from repetition, and fluency is what frees the mind to think about meaning.
- **Ask what happened, and why.** Then ask what might happen next.
- **Let them read aloud to a younger child, or to an animal.** Both are wonderfully non-judgemental audiences.
- **Give them things worth reading.** The single strongest predictor of a child who reads well is a child who wants to.
