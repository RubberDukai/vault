# Sentences and stories

**What they need first:** reading simple books, spelling from the first four steps, handwriting that is legible.

## What a sentence is

Say a sentence; clap once at the end. Say a not-sentence ("the big dog") and ask what is missing — *what did the dog do?* A sentence tells you **who** and **what happened**, and it makes sense on its own. Children hear the difference long before they can explain it; work from the ear.

**The two marks that make writing readable:** a **capital letter** to start, a **full stop** to finish. Insist on them from the first sentence written, every time, before anything else is corrected. Then **question marks** (a sentence that asks — say it with the rising voice) and **exclamation marks** (a sentence that shouts). Capitals also for names, days, months, places and *I*.

## Building sentences

Start from the child's own speech. They tell you something that happened; you write it down together; they copy it. Then they write it themselves and you read it back *exactly as written*, missing punctuation and all, so they hear what a reader hears.

**Growing a sentence.** *The dog ran.* → *The big dog ran.* → *The big dog ran to the gate.* → *The big dog ran to the gate because the postman came.* Each addition answers a question: what kind? where? why? when? Teach the words that join: **and, but, so, because, when, if, or**.

**The word families**, by what they do — the names can come later, the idea now:
- **Naming words** (nouns): things you can point to. Play "I spy a noun".
- **Doing words** (verbs): what happens. Act them out.
- **Describing words** (adjectives): what kind. Describe an apple with five.
- The little words that join and place: *and, but, in, on, under, with*.

**Past and present.** *I walk to school. I walked to school yesterday.* Most doing words add *-ed*; the ones that do not (*go/went, see/saw, run/ran, come/came, eat/ate, have/had*) are learned by ear and by reading.

## From sentences to pieces of writing

Once three good sentences come easily, write things that are *for* something:
- **A list** — what we need from the garden; the animals we saw. Real, and used.
- **Instructions** — how to plant a bean, how to make toast: numbered steps, each starting with a doing word. Follow them exactly to see if they work.
- **A letter** — to a grandparent, a neighbour, the person who will read this vault in fifty years: *Dear …, / what happened / a question / From …*.
- **A diary** — one sentence a day, dated. The most valuable writing habit there is, and the record that outlasts everything.
- **A description** — a person, an animal, a place, using the five senses.
- **A report** — what we found out: *We put a bean in a jar. After five days it had a root.*

## Stories

Every story has a **beginning** (who, where), a **middle** (something happens — a problem) and an **end** (it is sorted out). Tell stories aloud first: retell a known one (*Three Little Pigs*), then change one thing (what if the wolf was friendly?), then make one up together, taking turns a sentence at a time. Write them the same way — plan the three parts in three boxes, then write a few sentences in each.

**Story words** to collect on the wall: *Once upon a time, one day, suddenly, then, next, after that, at last, in the end.* **Characters** need a name and one thing they want. **Endings** need to answer the beginning.

Read stories to them every day — well above what they can read alone. That is where the vocabulary, the rhythm of sentences and the shape of a story come from; the writing only follows.

## Checking their work — with them

Read it aloud together. Three questions, in order: *Does every sentence start with a capital and end with a stop? Does it make sense? Is there a word we could make better?* One improvement per piece, not ten. Then keep it: date it, put it in a folder. In a year they will see how far they have come, which is the best encouragement there is.

## Exercises

1. Which of these are sentences? *the red hen. The hen laid an egg. ran to the shop. My dad is tall.*
2. Copy these out and put in the capital letters and full stops: *my cat is black she likes fish we live in a house by the river*
3. Add the right mark: *Where is my hat / Look out / I like apples*
4. Grow this sentence three times, answering *what kind, where, why*: *The boy ran.*
5. Join each pair with *and, but* or *because*: *I was cold. I put on a coat. / She is small. She is strong. / We went in. It rained.*
6. Write the past: *I jump. I go. I see. I eat. I play.*
7. Write instructions, in numbered steps, for making a cup of tea.
8. Write a short story in three parts: a hen, a fox, and how it ends. At least two sentences in each part.
9. Write today's diary sentence, with the date.

## Answers

1. Sentences: *The hen laid an egg. My dad is tall.* The others have no doing word or no who.
2. *My cat is black. She likes fish. We live in a house by the river.*
3. Where is my hat? Look out! I like apples.
4. For example: *The little boy ran. The little boy ran down the lane. The little boy ran down the lane because he was late.* Any sensible growth counts.
5. *I was cold so / and I put on a coat. She is small but she is strong. We went in because it rained.*
6. I jumped. I went. I saw. I ate. I played.
7. Check for numbered steps, each starting with a doing word (Fill, Boil, Put, Pour, Stir), in a sensible order. Then make the tea by them.
8. Check for the three parts, capitals and full stops, and an ending that answers the beginning.
9. Any dated sentence.
