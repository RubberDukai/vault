# Grammar and punctuation

**What they need first:** *Sentences and stories*; reading fluently enough to read their own work back.

Grammar is not a set of rules invented to catch children out. It is the description of how the language already works in their mouths; naming the parts lets them see what they are doing and do it on purpose. Teach every term with the child's own sentences, never with a table first.

## The parts of speech — by job

| Part | Job | Test | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Noun** | names a thing, person, place, idea | can you put *the* in front? | hen, Tom, Lancaster, courage |
| **Pronoun** | stands in for a noun | | he, she, it, they, we, this |
| **Verb** | what is done or what is | can you put *to* in front? change its time? | run, is, thought, will grow |
| **Adjective** | describes a noun | fits between *the* and the noun | red, tall, broken, careful |
| **Adverb** | describes a verb (how, when, where, how much) | often ends *-ly* | quickly, yesterday, here, very |
| **Preposition** | places a noun in space or time | fits before *the box*: in, on, under… | in, under, before, with, through |
| **Conjunction** | joins | | and, but, or, because, although, when |
| **Determiner** | points at a noun | | the, a, this, my, some, three |

The game: take a sentence from the book they are reading and label every word. Then swap one adjective for a better one; swap *said* for a verb that shows how (*whispered, snapped, muttered*); add an adverb; move the adverb to the front and hear what changes.

**Proper nouns** get capitals: names, places, days, months, titles of books. **Plurals**: add *-s*; *-es* after s, x, ch, sh; *y → ies* after a consonant (*babies* but *days*); the old ones — *children, men, women, feet, teeth, mice, sheep*.

## Tenses

**Present** (it happens now, or always): *she walks*. **Past** (it happened): *she walked*. **Future**: *she will walk*. Regular verbs add *-ed* for the past; the irregular ones must be known — *go/went, come/came, see/saw, take/took, give/gave, write/wrote, think/thought, bring/brought, buy/bought, catch/caught, sit/sat, run/ran, begin/began, drink/drank, swim/swam, ring/rang, fly/flew, grow/grew, know/knew, throw/threw, draw/drew, break/broke, speak/spoke, wake/woke, forget/forgot, hide/hid, find/found, hold/held, tell/told, sell/sold, lose/lost, leave/left, feel/felt, keep/kept, sleep/slept, mean/meant, build/built, send/sent, spend/spent, cut/cut, put/put, hit/hit, let/let, read/read.*

**The progressive**: *she was walking* (in the middle of it). **The perfect**: *she has walked* (done, and it matters now). A story stays in one tense; the commonest fault in children's writing is drifting from past to present and back. Read it aloud to hear the drift.

**Subject and verb agree**: *the hen lays; the hens lay; there is one; there are two; I was; you were; we were.*

## Punctuation — what each mark does

- **Full stop, question mark, exclamation mark** end a sentence. One exclamation mark, ever, per piece.
- **Comma** separates: items in a list (*eggs, milk, flour and salt* — no comma before the final *and* in British style), a clause at the front (*When the rain stopped, we went out*), extra information that could be lifted out (*My uncle, who keeps bees, gave us honey*), and a direct address (*Come here, Tom*). A comma **cannot** join two sentences: *It rained, we went in* is wrong — use a full stop, a semicolon or *so*.
- **Apostrophe** — two jobs and no others. **Missing letters**: *don't, can't, it's* (= it is), *we're, they've, o'clock*. **Belonging**: *the dog's bone* (one dog), *the dogs' bones* (many dogs), *the children's coats* (an irregular plural). **Never for plurals**: *apples* not *apple's*. **Its** (belonging) has no apostrophe; *it's* means *it is* — the single most common error in English, so drill it.
- **Speech marks** ("…" or '…' — choose one and keep to it): around the exact words spoken; the punctuation inside; a new line for each new speaker; a comma before or after the *said*: *"Come in," said Mum. Tom asked, "Is it ready?"*
- **Colon** introduces: a list, an explanation, a quotation. *We need three things: water, fire and shelter.*
- **Semicolon** joins two sentences that belong together, and separates long items in a list. *The rain fell all night; by morning the river was over the road.*
- **Dash** — for an aside, or a sudden turn. **Brackets** (for an aside that matters less). **Hyphen** joins words into one: *well-known, twenty-one, mother-in-law*.
- **Ellipsis** … for a trailing off. Rarely.

## Sentences that do things

**Simple**: one idea. *The fox came.* **Compound**: two joined with *and, but, or, so*. *The fox came but the hens were shut in.* **Complex**: a main idea with a dependent clause hung on it by *because, although, when, if, while, until, since, after*. *Although the fox came, the hens were shut in.* Move the clause to the end and drop the comma: *The hens were shut in although the fox came.* Vary the three: a piece of all simple sentences reads like a list; all complex reads like a lawyer.

**Statements, questions, commands, exclamations**: *The gate is open. Is the gate open? Shut the gate. What a mess!*

**Active and passive**: *The fox took the hen* / *The hen was taken by the fox*. The passive hides who did it; that is sometimes the point, and worth noticing in what other people write.

## Paragraphs

A paragraph is one idea, or one step in time, or one speaker. Start a new one when the idea, the time, the place or the speaker changes. Indent or leave a line. Plan a piece as a list of paragraph ideas before writing it: three to five for a page.

## Marking their writing

Look for one thing at a time, in this order: sentences ending properly; tense steady; *its/it's* and the other apostrophes; commas doing a job; paragraphs; then the vocabulary. Write the corrected form in the margin and have them copy it once. Keep the personal spelling book going.

## Exercises

1. Label every word: *The old dog slept quietly under the table.*
2. Rewrite with a better verb than *said* and add an adverb: *"Get out," said the farmer.*
3. Put into the past: *I go to the market and buy bread. She takes the bucket and brings water. They begin work when the sun rises.*
4. Add commas: *We need string nails glue and a hammer. After the storm passed we checked the roof. My sister who is nine can milk the goat.*
5. Apostrophes — fix or leave: *The hens eggs. Its raining. The dog wagged its tail. Two apple's for a penny. The childrens boots. Dont go.*
6. Punctuate the speech: *where are you going asked Tom / to the well she said its dry again*
7. Join into one complex sentence in two different ways: *The river rose. We moved the animals.*
8. Turn active to passive and back: *The fox ate the hen. / The bridge was built by the villagers.*
9. Here is a page with no paragraphs (use a page from the child's own writing or a book with the breaks removed): mark where each new paragraph should start and say why.
10. Write four sentences about the same event: a statement, a question, a command, an exclamation.

## Answers

1. The (determiner) old (adjective) dog (noun) slept (verb) quietly (adverb) under (preposition) the (determiner) table (noun).
2. E.g. *"Get out," roared the farmer furiously.*
3. I went to the market and bought bread. She took the bucket and brought water. They began work when the sun rose.
4. *We need string, nails, glue and a hammer. After the storm passed, we checked the roof. My sister, who is nine, can milk the goat.*
5. The hens' eggs (or hen's, if one hen). It's raining. The dog wagged its tail (correct). Two apples for a penny. The children's boots. Don't go.
6. *"Where are you going?" asked Tom.* / *"To the well," she said. "It's dry again."*
7. *When the river rose, we moved the animals. / We moved the animals because the river rose.* (Any two correct joinings.)
8. *The hen was eaten by the fox. / The villagers built the bridge.*
9. New paragraphs at each change of idea, time, place or speaker.
10. E.g. *The gate is open. Who left the gate open? Shut the gate. What a mess the goats have made!*
