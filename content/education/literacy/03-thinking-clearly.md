# Thinking clearly

A person who can read but cannot judge what they read is at the mercy of whoever wrote it. In a world with no fact-checkers, no second opinions and no way to look things up, the ability to weigh a claim is not an academic nicety. It is what stops a family drinking the wrong water because a confident stranger said it was fine.

This can be taught. It is a set of questions, asked by habit.

## The four questions

Whenever someone tells you something matters — a rumour, a remedy, a warning, a plan — ask:

1. **How do they know?** Did they see it, or hear it? From whom? Is there any way to check?
2. **What would it look like if they were wrong?** If nothing could count as evidence against it, it is not a claim about the world; it is a belief.
3. **Who benefits if I believe it?** Not because everyone lies, but because people find it easy to believe what suits them, and to pass it on.
4. **What is the cost of being wrong each way?** Believing a false warning costs a wasted day. Ignoring a true one costs more. The decision depends on both the odds and the stakes.

## Fact, claim, opinion

- **A fact** can be checked: "The river rose a metre last night." Go and look.
- **A claim** is presented as fact but not yet checked: "The water upstream is poisoned." Maybe. How would we know?
- **An opinion** is a judgement: "We should leave." Reasonable people can differ; the question is what reasons are behind it.

People blur these constantly, usually without meaning to. The habit of sorting them is most of clear thinking.

## Bad arguments that sound good

Learn these by name so you notice them in the moment.

**"Everyone knows…"** — Popularity is not evidence. Everyone once knew the earth was flat.

**"He's a doctor / he's old / he's in charge, so…"** — Authority is a reason to listen, not a reason to stop thinking. Experts are right more often than not; that is all. Ask *how* they know.

**"It worked for my cousin."** — One case. Most things get better on their own; the remedy may have done nothing. See the science lesson on controls.

**"If we let them do X, next it'll be Y, then Z."** — Maybe. Each step needs its own argument. A chain of maybes is not a certainty.

**"Either we do this or we all die."** — Almost never true. There are nearly always more than two options; when someone offers exactly two, look for the third.

**"You only say that because you're…"** — Attacking the person instead of the argument. A bad person can say a true thing.

**"It's natural / traditional / the way it's always been done."** — Hemlock is natural. Traditions encode a great deal of hard-won wisdom *and* a great deal of nonsense, and the only way to tell them apart is to check.

**"After we did X, Y happened, so X caused Y."** — The rooster crows and the sun rises. Things that follow are not always caused. Look for the mechanism.

**"Nobody has proved it's safe."** — Nobody has proved most things. The question is what the evidence actually says, and how much of it there is.

## What good evidence looks like

- **More than one source**, independent of each other — not three people repeating what one person said.
- **Direct observation** beats hearsay. Written at the time beats remembered later.
- **A mechanism** — a reason *why* it would work — makes a claim far more believable. "This plant cures fevers" is weak. "This plant contains a compound that lowers temperature, here is how, here is who tested it" is strong.
- **Numbers**, with what they are numbers *of*. "Most" and "many" hide a lot.
- **Someone who says what would change their mind.** The person who can tell you what evidence would prove them wrong is the person to trust.

## Changing your mind

Is not weakness. It is the whole point. A mind that cannot be changed by evidence is a mind that learns nothing, and in a hard world that is fatal.

Two habits:

- **Say out loud what you expect to happen.** Then see. If you were wrong, you know it, and you have learned something real. If you never predict, you can never be wrong, and you never learn.
- **Steel-man the other side.** Before dismissing a view, state it in its strongest form — so well that someone who holds it would say "yes, that's it." Only then reply. Most disagreements dissolve at this step, because it turns out you disagreed about something else.

## Deciding under uncertainty

You will rarely have enough information. Decide anyway, well:

1. What are the options — all of them, including doing nothing?
2. For each, what is the best case, the likely case, the worst case?
3. **Can we survive the worst case?** If not, that option is out, however good its likely case. (This is the expected-value lesson in the maths module, in words.)
4. Which option is easiest to reverse if it turns out wrong? Prefer it.
5. Decide, write down why, act, and check later whether the reasoning held.

Writing down *why* matters most. Memory rewrites decisions to make them look wiser than they were. The note does not.

## Exercises

1. Sort these into fact, claim, opinion: "The bridge is down." "The bridge is unsafe." "We shouldn't use the bridge." "Someone said the bridge is down." For each claim, how would you check?
2. Find the flaw: "Three families who drank from the well got ill, so the well is poisoned." What else could explain it? What would you check?
3. "Either we ration now or we starve in March." Name three other options.
4. A stranger arrives with news of a safe town two days' walk away. List the four questions and how you would answer each.
5. Someone you trust says a herb cures infection. Steel-man their view. Then say what evidence would make you believe it, and what would make you doubt it.
6. Make a decision this week using the five steps, and write the note. Next week, read the note and judge your own reasoning.
7. Find a "bad argument" from the list in something someone said today. Which one was it?

## Answers

1. *Fact:* "The bridge is down" — checkable by going to look. *Claim:* "The bridge is unsafe" — checkable, but needs a standard for "safe" and someone able to judge it. *Opinion:* "We shouldn't use the bridge" — a judgement about what to do, which depends on the facts but is not itself one. *Hearsay:* "Someone said the bridge is down" — evidence about what someone said, not about the bridge. Check by looking, by asking who said it and how they knew, and by asking what "unsafe" would mean in practice.
2. It confuses correlation with cause, and the sample is small and self-selected. Other explanations: the three families ate together, shared something else, caught it from one another, or were simply the ones who reported it. Check whether others using the well are well, what else the three shared, and test the water itself.
3. A false choice. Other options: ration partially; ration now and review in a month; find or trade for more food; reduce the number of mouths sharing (guests, livestock); eat the stores in a different order so what spoils first goes first; forage or fish; or accept a smaller ration rather than either extreme.
4. How do you know? How confident are you? What would change your mind? What are you not saying? For the stranger: how did they see it — in person or heard from another? Would they go back? What would make them doubt it? And what are they not saying — why did they leave, what do they want from you?
5. A good steel man states their case with its best evidence: that the herb has been used for generations, that people did recover, and that plant compounds really are the origin of many medicines. Evidence that would convince: a comparison where similar cases were treated and not treated, with someone recording the results who did not know which was which. Evidence that would cause doubt: recoveries just as fast without it, or harm in those who took it.
6. and 7. No fixed answers. The value of 6 is reading your own note a week later, when you know how it turned out — that is the only reliable way to find out whether you reason well. For 7, mark on whether the fallacy is named accurately and politely.
