# Writing that lasts

In a world with no internet, writing stops being schoolwork and becomes infrastructure. It is how a repair gets done twice, how a harvest is remembered, how a message crosses a valley, and how a person who never met you learns what you knew.

Write for **someone who is not there, does not know what you know, and cannot ask you a question.**

## The kinds worth teaching

**Instructions.** The hardest and most useful. Numbered steps, one action each, in order. State what is needed before you begin. Say what it should look like when it is right — and what it looks like when it is wrong.

> Test: hand your instructions to someone who has never done the task. Do not speak. Watch them fail, and fix the step where they failed. Repeat. This is the whole discipline of technical writing and it is genuinely humbling.

**Records.** Date, what happened, what was measured, who was there. Write it at the time. Memory rearranges itself with astonishing confidence.

**Messages.** Who it is for, who it is from, when it was written, what you need, by when. A message that arrives without a date is nearly useless.

**Explanations.** Start with what the reader already knows and build one step at a time. The commonest failure in explaining is forgetting how much you know.

**Letters and journals.** These are how a person survives in the memory of people who never met them. Encourage this. It matters more than it seems.

## Rules that carry most of the weight

1. **One idea per sentence.** Long sentences are where meaning goes to hide.
2. **Concrete over abstract.** "Boil for one minute" not "ensure adequate thermal treatment".
3. **Numbers with units, always.** "2 drops per litre", not "a couple of drops".
4. **Say who does what.** "Check the fuse" beats "the fuse should be checked" — the second leaves it unclear whose job it is.
5. **Cut every word that carries nothing.** Read it aloud; you will hear them.
6. **Put the important thing first.** Readers in a hurry stop early.
7. **Date everything.** Including the year. Especially the year.

## Structure

- **Title** that says what it is.
- **A first line** that says what the reader will get.
- **Headings** so a reader can find the part they need without reading the rest.
- **Short paragraphs**, one idea each.
- **Lists** for anything sequential or enumerable.
- **A warning before the step it applies to**, never after.

That last one is a safety rule, not a style rule. A warning that appears after the dangerous step has already failed at its only job.

## Exercises

1. Write instructions for making a cup of tea. Give them to someone and have them follow it *exactly*, without inferring anything. Rewrite. Repeat until it works.
2. Write a half-page record of today: weather, what was done, what was used, what worked, what did not.
3. Take a paragraph from the handbook and rewrite it for a six-year-old. Then in exactly twenty words.
4. Write a message asking a neighbour for help with a specific job on a specific day. Include everything they need to answer without asking you anything.
5. Write instructions for something you know well and someone else does not. Have them do it while you stay silent.
6. Write a letter to be read in twenty years by someone who does not know you. Explain one thing you think is worth knowing.

## Answers

These are all pieces of writing rather than questions with one right answer, so mark them against what the writing had to *do*.

1. It works when the other person makes the tea without asking you anything and without guessing. Every hesitation is a fault in the instructions, not in them. Expect three or four rewrites — that is the lesson.
2. Look for: the date, the weather, what was actually done (not "the usual"), quantities with units, and one honest line about what did not work. A record that only lists successes is worth much less in a year.
3. For the six-year-old: short sentences, no technical words, one idea at a time. For the twenty words: count them exactly. The discipline of an exact count is what teaches compression.
4. Check it contains the job, the day, roughly how long it will take, what they would need to bring, and a way to say no. If the neighbour would have to write back to ask a question, it has failed.
5. As with 1 — staying silent is the whole exercise. Note where they stopped, and fix those places.
6. No fixed answer. Look for something concrete and true rather than general advice, and for enough context that a stranger in twenty years would understand it. Date it and keep it.

## Handwriting, and why it still matters

Legible handwriting is a survival skill when there is no printer. Teach a plain, joined hand — speed comes from joining, legibility from consistent size and spacing.

Practise on anything: dirt, slates, the backs of packets, charcoal on wood. Paper is precious; write small, write on both sides, and keep the important things in one durable book rather than scattered on loose sheets.

## Making it last

- **Pencil outlasts most ink.** Graphite does not fade, run or bleed. A pencil-written note from a century ago is usually perfectly legible; the ink one may not be.
- **Acid-free paper** if you have any choice, kept dry and dark.
- **Write the date and the author on every page**, not just the first — pages get separated.
- **Copy the important things more than once, and keep the copies apart.** Fire and water take whole shelves at a time.
- **Bind or file loose sheets.** Nothing is lost as reliably as a single loose page.
