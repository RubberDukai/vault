# The report and the letter

**What they need first:** *Grammar and punctuation*; *The essay* for structure, though this is writing of a different kind.

An essay persuades. This lesson is about writing that has a **job**: to be followed, to be acted on, to be found and understood by somebody who is not in the room — possibly not in this decade. It is the most useful writing there is and almost nobody is taught it.

**The test of every piece of writing here is not "is it good?" but "did it work?"** Could the reader do the thing, find the thing, or decide the thing? Wherever possible, test it: hand the instructions to somebody and watch.

## Instructions

Someone must be able to do the thing from your page, without you.

- **Say what it is for and what it needs first.** Materials, tools, time, and any warning — before the steps, not in the middle of them.
- **Number the steps**, one action each, in the order they happen.
- **Start each step with the verb.** *Cut the board to 600 mm.* Not *the board should then be cut*.
- **Be exact about quantity, size, time and temperature.** "A while" and "a bit" are not instructions. If it must be exact, say so; if it need not be, say that too — *about a handful* is honest, *some* is not.
- **Say how to tell it is working**, and what it looks like when it has gone wrong. This is what separates a real instruction from a copied one.
- **Put the warnings where the danger is** — immediately before the dangerous step, not only at the top.
- **Then test it.** Hand it to somebody and watch without speaking. Every place they hesitate is a fault in your writing, not in them.

## Reports: what happened

A report is an account of something that happened or was found, written so that somebody who was not there can rely on it.

**Structure:** what was done → what was observed → what it means → what should happen next. Keep those four separate, and label them if it helps. The single most important discipline is to **keep observation and interpretation apart**: *the ewe would not stand, and her gums were pale* is an observation; *she was anaemic* is a conclusion, and it may be wrong.

Always include: **the date, the time, the place, and who wrote it.** A report without a date is half a report.

Use plain past tense and the active voice where you can. Write numbers with units. If you do not know something, write that you do not know it — an admitted gap is useful; a guess dressed as a fact is dangerous.

## The log, which may be the most valuable thing you write

A log is a record kept as it happens, in a bound book, in ink, never rewritten. Weather, harvest, stores, medicine given, repairs, births and deaths, what was tried and whether it worked.

- **Date every entry** and write it the same way every time.
- **Write it at the time**, not from memory that evening.
- **Do not erase.** Rule one line through a mistake so the original is still readable, write the correction beside it, and initial it. A log that has been tidied up cannot be trusted, and a log that cannot be trusted is waste paper.
- **Record failures as carefully as successes.** *Tried X, it did not work, here is what happened* saves somebody a season.
- **Write for a stranger.** Not "did the usual with the top field" but what was actually done.

In fifty years the log will be the only thing that can tell your grandchildren when the frosts come here, what grows on that soil and what killed the pigs in the bad year. That is not sentiment; it is the single cheapest piece of insurance in the house. (The tables to keep are listed in *Graphs, tables and keeping records*.)

## Letters

A letter is a piece of writing aimed at one person to get one thing. Decide the thing before you start.

**The shape:** your address and the date; their name; why you are writing, in the first sentence; the detail; what you want them to do; how to reach you; a sign-off.

*Dear Mr Hale / Yours sincerely* when you know the name; *Dear Sir or Madam / Yours faithfully* when you do not. It is an old convention and still the one people notice.

Four kinds worth practising:

- **Asking.** Say who you are and what you want in the first two sentences. Give the reason. Make it easy to say yes — say exactly what you are asking for, by when.
- **Complaining.** Facts, dates and what was promised; then what you want done. **Anger loses.** A calm letter with dates attached is far harder to dismiss than a furious one, and the child who learns this at twelve will be listened to all their life.
- **Thanking.** Short, specific and soon. *Thank you for the seed potatoes; we planted them on the 3rd and they are already through* beats a page of general gratitude.
- **The difficult one** — condolence, apology, bad news. Plain, short, no excuses, no explanation of how hard it was for you to write. Say the thing; say what you will do; stop.

Always: **read it once as the person receiving it.** How does this land? Then send it.

## Notices, labels and the writing nobody signs

- **A notice** has to work from three metres away: biggest words first, few words, one instruction. *Deep water — do not swim.*
- **A label** says what it is, how strong or how much, and **when it was made**. An unlabelled jar is a mystery in two months and a hazard in two years. Label the medicine, the seed, the preserve, the fuel and the chemical, with the date, every time.
- **A sign on a path, a mark on a map, a note on a door** — these are writing too, and the same rule holds: could a stranger act on it correctly?

## Summarising somebody else's writing

A skill this lesson needs and the rest of life needs more: read it, put it down, and write it in a quarter of the words without looking. Then check nothing essential is lost and nothing has been added. Practise on a page of the Handbook, a Wikipedia article, a letter. This is also the best possible check on whether you understood it at all.

## Exercises

1. Write instructions for lighting a fire from cold, for someone who has never done it. Then hand them to somebody and watch them try without saying a word. Note every place they hesitated, and rewrite.
2. Rewrite these as proper steps: *You want to get the water on to boil and then you should put in the grain, stirring it a bit, and cook it until it seems done.*
3. Take one of your instructions and mark where the warning belongs. Explain why it goes there and not at the top.
4. Write a report of something that actually happened this week — an animal ill, a tool broken, a crop failure. Use the four parts, and label which sentences are observation and which are conclusion.
5. Find the conclusion hiding in this "observation": *The hen was egg-bound and miserable.* Rewrite it as what was actually seen.
6. Set up a log book. Rule the columns, write the first week's entries, and rule through a deliberate mistake correctly.
7. Explain why a log entry should never be rubbed out and rewritten, even to make it neater.
8. Write a letter asking a neighbour to lend a tool for three days. Under 150 words.
9. Rewrite this complaint so it will work: *I am absolutely furious about the state of the cart you sold me, it is a disgrace and you should be ashamed of yourself.* You may invent dates and details.
10. Write a thank-you letter of four sentences that could only have been written to that one person about that one thing.
11. Design a notice warning of a deep ditch, to be read from three metres. Draw it at the size you would make it.
12. Label six jars: a preserve, a medicine, a seed, a fuel, a disinfectant and something unknown found in a shed. What does each need on it, and how do they differ?
13. Summarise a page of the Handbook in a quarter of the words, from memory, then check what you left out. Was anything essential lost?
14. Write the same event twice: once as a log entry, once as a letter to a relative. What changed, and why?

## Answers

1. No fixed answer — the watching is the mark. Hesitation means a fault in the writing.
2. E.g. *1. Bring 500 ml of water to a rolling boil. 2. Add 100 g of grain. 3. Stir once. 4. Simmer for 20 minutes. 5. It is done when the grain is soft through and the water is taken up.* Verb first, one action per step, exact quantities, and a test for "done".
3. Immediately before the dangerous step, because that is where it will be read at the moment it matters. A warning only at the top has usually been forgotten by step nine.
4. Look for the four parts kept apart, a date and a name, and honest labelling of which sentences are conclusions.
5. "Egg-bound" and "miserable" are both conclusions. Observed: *the hen sat in the corner, would not move when approached, strained repeatedly, and laid nothing for two days.*
6. Check: consistent date format, entries written at the time, and a correction made by a single ruled line with the new value beside it and initials — not scribbled out.
7. Because a record that can be altered cannot be trusted, and an untrustworthy record is worth nothing. The original entry may also turn out to have been right.
8. Look for: what is wanted, why, exactly when it would be returned, and an easy way to say no.
9. Look for dates, what was promised, what was actually received, what is wanted now, and a calm tone. The facts do the work; the anger only gives the reader something to object to instead of the complaint.
10. Mark it on specificity. If the letter could be sent to anybody, it has failed.
11. Biggest word first, very few words, an instruction not a description, high contrast, and mounted where it is seen before the danger.
12. All six need: what it is, and the date. The preserve needs what and when made; the medicine needs strength, dose and date; the seed needs variety, year and where saved from; the fuel needs what kind and a warning; the disinfectant needs strength, dilution and a hazard warning. The unknown one should be labelled *unknown, found in shed, dated today* — and treated as dangerous until identified.
13. No fixed answer. The check is the exercise: summarising from memory shows what was understood.
14. The log entry is short, dated, factual, impersonal. The letter has context, feeling and explanation for someone who does not know the background. The facts must not change between them — only what is included and how it is said.
