# The essay

**What they need first:** *Grammar and punctuation*; *Reading for meaning*; *Thinking clearly*, which is where the logic behind all of this lives.

An essay is not a collection of everything you know about a subject. It is **an answer to a question, in order, with reasons**. A child who learns that is learning to think on paper, which is a different and harder skill than thinking in their head — and it is the one that lasts, because a written argument can be checked, kept, and handed to somebody else.

## Start from the question

Read the question and find the **command word**, because it tells you what shape the answer must have:

| Command | What it asks for |
|---|---|
| **Describe** | what it is like — no argument needed |
| **Explain** | why or how — reasons and mechanisms |
| **Compare** | similarities *and* differences, side by side |
| **Discuss** | both sides, then a judgement |
| **Argue / To what extent** | take a position and defend it against the other side |
| **Evaluate** | weigh it and say how good, how true, how successful |

Underline the command word and the topic words. Then write the question out again in your own words. Half of all bad essays are answers to a question nobody asked.

## The thesis

**The thesis is your answer to the question, in one sentence.** Write it before you write anything else. If you cannot say it in a sentence, you do not yet know what you think, and more reading — or five minutes of staring out of the window — is the right next step, not more writing.

Weak: *This essay will look at whether the enclosures were a good thing.*
Strong: *The enclosures raised the country's food output, but they did it by taking a living from the poorest people, and both halves of that are true at once.*

The strong one tells the reader where the essay goes and commits the writer to defending something. It also gives you your plan: you now know you need a section on output, a section on cost, and a conclusion that holds both.

## Plan before writing — five minutes, always

1. Write the thesis.
2. List three or four **points** that support it, one line each.
3. Under each, note the **evidence** you have.
4. Decide the **order**. Strongest last is usual; chronological works for history; clearest-first works when the reader needs building up.
5. Note the **counter-argument** and where you will deal with it.

That is the whole plan, and it fits on a postcard. An essay written from a plan takes less time overall than one written without, because you are not thinking and writing at once.

## The shape

**Introduction** — what the question is, what you say about it (the thesis), and the route you will take. Three or four sentences. No throat-clearing: *Since the dawn of time, humans have* is a sentence that should be struck out on sight.

**The body** — one paragraph per point. Each paragraph does four things, in this order:

1. **Point** — a topic sentence saying what this paragraph argues. One sentence, at the start, always.
2. **Evidence** — the fact, quotation, figure or example.
3. **Explanation** — *why* that evidence supports the point. This is the part that gets skipped and it is the part that is actually the essay.
4. **Link** — back to the question, or on to the next point.

Point, evidence, explanation, link. Teach it as a habit until it is invisible. A paragraph with a topic sentence and no explanation is a note; a paragraph with explanation and no evidence is an opinion.

**Counter-argument** — say what someone who disagrees would argue, at its strongest, and then answer it. This is not a weakness in your case. An essay that never admits an objection reads as though the writer has not thought about it, and a reader who thinks of the objection you ignored stops trusting you.

**Conclusion** — answer the question. Do not introduce new evidence. Do not simply repeat the introduction in other words. Say what follows from what you have shown, and what it means. One good final sentence is worth a paragraph of summary.

## Evidence and quotation

- **Quote short and quote exactly.** A few words inside the sentence is better than four lines set apart.
- **Never let a quotation stand alone.** Introduce it, give it, explain it. *Kipling calls the jungle "the Law", which turns a wild place into something governed — and the whole story follows from that.*
- **Say where it came from**, at least in passing: which book, whose figures, what year.
- **Weigh a source before using it:** who made it, when, for whom, and what did they want the reader to believe?
- **Distinguish** what you *know* from what you *infer* from it. "The rent book shows arrears in 1847" is different from "the family was starving".

## Sentences that carry argument

- **Vary the length.** Long, worked-out sentences for reasoning; a short one to land the point. Like that.
- **Use the connectives that show the logic**, and mean them: *because, therefore, however, although, whereas, moreover, nevertheless, consequently*. Each makes a different claim about how two ideas relate, and using the wrong one is a logical error, not a style error.
- **Prefer the active voice** — *Parliament passed the act*, not *the act was passed* — unless who did it is genuinely unknown or beside the point.
- **Cut the padding.** *It is important to note that, in terms of, basically, very, really, at the end of the day.* Strike them and the sentence is sharper every time.
- **Hedge honestly.** *Suggests, indicates, may* when the evidence is partial; *shows, proves* only when it does. Overclaiming is the commonest fault in a confident writer and it costs you the reader.

## Redrafting, which is where essays are actually made

Nobody writes a good essay once. Leave it overnight if you can, then read it **aloud** — the ear catches what the eye slides over. Then go through it four times, looking for one thing each time:

1. **Does it answer the question?** Put the question at the top and check every paragraph against it. Cut anything that does not earn its place, however good it is.
2. **Is every claim supported?** Any sentence with no evidence behind it is either obvious or unproven.
3. **Do the paragraphs each make one point, and is it in the first sentence?**
4. **Sentences and words.** Length, connectives, padding, spelling.

Then count the words you cut. A good redraft usually gets shorter.

## A worked skeleton

> **Question:** To what extent did the railways change ordinary life in Britain?
>
> **Thesis:** The railways changed ordinary life more than any invention before them, not mainly by moving people but by moving food, work and time itself.
>
> 1. **Food** — fresh milk, fish and vegetables reached cities within a day; diets changed and local famine became far less likely. *Evidence:* the collapse of local food prices; fish in inland towns.
> 2. **Work** — people could live apart from where they worked, so towns grew outward and labour could move to where the wages were.
> 3. **Time** — before the railways every town kept its own clock by the sun. Timetables forced a single national time, and the working day became something measured rather than something felt. *(Strongest point, so it goes last.)*
> 4. **Counter-argument** — for the very poorest, and in places the line missed, little changed for decades; and the change came at a cost in danger, noise and destroyed housing. *Answer:* the reach was uneven but the direction was one way, and within two generations almost nobody was untouched.
> 5. **Conclusion** — the deepest change was the least visible: a country that had lived by the sun began to live by the clock.

Have the child write the essay from that skeleton, then write their own skeleton for a different question, then swap skeletons with somebody and write from theirs. Planning is a separate skill from writing and is worth practising on its own.

## Exercises

1. For each, name the command word and say what shape the answer needs: *Describe a Norman castle. Explain why the Black Death raised wages. Discuss whether the Romans were good for Britain. To what extent is a wind pump better than a water wheel here?*
2. Turn each into a one-sentence thesis: *Was the enclosure of common land justified? Should children be taught Latin? Is it better to keep goats or sheep on poor ground?*
3. Here is a weak opening: *In this essay I am going to talk about lots of different things about rivers and why they are important to people in many ways.* Rewrite it as a proper introduction with a thesis.
4. Write a single paragraph using point, evidence, explanation, link, on: *Why a household should keep written records.*
5. Take this paragraph and find what is missing: *The Romans were good engineers. They built roads and aqueducts and the Pantheon. This shows they were advanced.* Rewrite it properly.
6. Write the strongest possible counter-argument to a position you hold, then answer it in three sentences.
7. Choose the right connective and say why: *The harvest failed ____ we had grain stored.* (*because / although / therefore*)
8. Cut these to half their length without losing meaning: *It is important to note that, at the end of the day, the basic fundamental reason why the crop actually failed was essentially due to the fact that there was a lack of rainfall.*
9. Plan — do not write — an essay on: *To what extent should a household grow its own food?* Thesis, three points, evidence, counter-argument, order.
10. Write that essay, in about 500 words, from your plan.
11. Redraft yesterday's essay using the four passes. Count the words before and after, and list three things you cut and why.
12. Swap essays with somebody. Mark theirs against three questions only: does it answer the question, is every claim supported, does each paragraph make one point? Write two sentences of praise and one thing to change.

## Answers

1. *Describe* — what it was like, features, no argument. *Explain* — causes and mechanism (fewer workers, so labour was scarce, so wages rose). *Discuss* — both sides then a judgement. *To what extent* — take a position and defend it against the other side, with a measured answer rather than all-or-nothing.
2. Look for a sentence that takes a position and could be disagreed with. E.g. *Enclosure was justified by the food it produced but not by the way it was done.*
3. Look for: the question restated, a thesis in one sentence, and a hint of the route — and no "since the dawn of time".
4. Check the four parts are present and in order, especially the explanation.
5. The explanation is missing, and the evidence is a list rather than a worked example. Better: *The Romans were engineers before they were anything else. The aqueduct at Segovia carried water 16 km on stone arches built without mortar, and it still stands. That is not a display of wealth but a solution to a problem — a city too big for its wells — and it is why Roman remains are so often waterworks and roads rather than palaces.*
6. Mark it on whether the counter-argument is the *strongest* version. A weak counter-argument set up to be knocked down is worth nothing, and the child should be told so by name: it is a straw man.
7. *although* — the two halves are in tension, and *although* is the connective that signals a concession.
8. E.g. *The crop failed because it did not rain.* From 29 words to seven.
9. Mark the plan, not the prose. Look for a real thesis, three distinct points, evidence noted under each, a counter-argument (cost, time, land, skill, what you cannot grow) and a decided order.
10. Mark against the plan. If the essay drifts from the plan, that is worth discussing — sometimes the drift is the writer discovering what they think, which is fine, but then the plan should have been rewritten.
11. No fixed answer. The three cut things and the reasons are the exercise; a shorter second draft is a good sign.
12. No fixed answer. Marking someone else's work teaches more than being marked.
