# Poetry

**What they need first:** reading aloud without struggling; *Reading for meaning*, which is where inference is taught — a poem is inference in a small space.

Poetry is the oldest way of keeping words. Before writing, everything worth remembering was in verse, because rhythm and rhyme make words stick; that is why nobody forgets a nursery rhyme and everybody forgets a paragraph. A child who knows twenty poems by heart carries a library that cannot be lost, damaged or taken away. Teach it for that reason if for no other.

It is also the cheapest lesson in this Vault. It needs no equipment and, once the poems are learned, no book.

## The daily five minutes

- **Read one poem aloud, every day.** Do not explain it first. Read it twice — the second time is when they hear it.
- **Ask two questions only.** *What picture came into your head?* and *which line did you like best?* Not "what does it mean". A poem is allowed to be enjoyed before it is understood.
- **Learn one poem a fortnight by heart.** Four lines a day, said aloud together, then said back. Say it at the same moment each day — while washing up, while walking. In a year that is twenty-five poems.
- **Say them, don't just read them.** Standing up, out loud, to somebody. Performance is half of poetry and it builds the confidence that carries into *Speaking and debate* at KS3.

**A starting list to learn by heart**, from the shelf and from what every household knows: nursery rhymes first, then Edward Lear's *The Owl and the Pussy-cat* and *The Jumblies*; Stevenson's *The Swing*, *From a Railway Carriage* and *Windy Nights* from *A Child's Garden of Verses*; Lewis Carroll's *Jabberwocky* and *The Walrus and the Carpenter*; Kipling's *If—*; Blake's *The Tyger*; Wordsworth's *Daffodils*; Tennyson's *The Charge of the Light Brigade*; Longfellow's *Paul Revere's Ride*; a psalm or two, whatever your household's beliefs — they are the root of a great deal of English. All are public domain and on the reading shelves or in the Library.

## How a poem sounds

**Rhythm** is the beat. Clap it. Most English verse is a pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables — *da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM* is the commonest and the closest to a heartbeat, which is why it sounds natural. Do not teach the names of the metres at KS2; teach the child to *hear* the beat and to notice when the poet breaks it, because the broken beat is always the important line.

**Rhyme** is the chime at the ends of lines. Mark the pattern with letters: *AABB* (couplets), *ABAB* (alternating), *ABCB* (the ballad, the commonest of all). Half-rhyme is a rhyme that nearly fits — *stone/moon* — and is worth pointing out so that they do not think poetry must jingle.

**The sound of the words themselves:**

| Device | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Alliteration** | the same sound starting nearby words | *the silver stream slid* |
| **Assonance** | the same vowel inside words | *the lone road home* |
| **Onomatopoeia** | the word sounds like the thing | *crack, hiss, thud, splash* |
| **Repetition** | the same word or line again | a refrain, a chorus |
| **Rhythm broken** | the beat stops or stumbles | usually the line that matters |

Hunt for these in the poem of the day. One device, one minute — not a checklist.

## How a poem sees

This is the part that transfers to everything else they write.

- **Simile** — says one thing is *like* another. *The field was like a sheet of glass.* Signalled by *like* or *as*.
- **Metaphor** — says one thing *is* another. *The field was a sheet of glass.* Stronger, because it does not apologise.
- **Personification** — gives a thing a person's life. *The wind shouldered the door.*
- **Imagery** — language aimed at the senses. Push beyond sight: what could you hear, smell, feel underfoot?

The test of any of them: *is it exact?* "As white as snow" is dead because everyone has said it. "As white as the inside of a bone" is alive because someone looked. Hunt down the tired ones — *cold as ice*, *good as gold* — and make them invent a better one. That is the single most useful poetry exercise there is.

## Kinds of poem worth knowing

- **Nursery rhyme and nonsense.** Lear and Carroll. Nonsense is not a lesser thing; it teaches that sound carries meaning on its own — every child knows the Jabberwock is dangerous without knowing one word of the description.
- **The list poem.** No rhyme, no metre, just a list with a shape. The best first thing to write.
- **Haiku.** Three lines, 5–7–5 syllables, one moment, usually in nature, usually with a turn in the last line. Teaches compression better than anything.
- **The ballad.** A story in four-line verses rhyming ABCB, often with a refrain. *The Charge of the Light Brigade*, *Paul Revere's Ride*, the old border ballads. This is how news and history travelled for a thousand years.
- **The sonnet.** Fourteen lines with a turn in the argument. Look at one, learn its name, and leave it for KS3.
- **Free verse.** No rhyme and no fixed beat — but still shaped: the line breaks are the craft. Late KS2.

## Writing poems

Rules that make it work, in order:

1. **Write about something small and real.** Not "the sea" — this puddle, that dead wasp, the smell of the shed. Big subjects produce vague poems.
2. **Use the five senses.** Make them list what they could hear and smell before they write a line.
3. **Cut every word that is not working.** A poem is a piece of writing with nothing spare in it. Take out *very*, *really*, *nice*, *beautiful*.
4. **Line breaks are decisions.** The last word of a line is the loudest word. Read it aloud to find where to break.
5. **Do not chase a rhyme off a cliff.** If the rhyme is forcing a silly line, drop the rhyme. Meaning first.
6. **Say it aloud, then fix it.** Every poem is finished by ear.

Copy out a poem they love in their best handwriting, keep it in a folder with their own poems, and by the end of KS2 they will have a book that is entirely theirs.

## Exercises

1. Learn *The Owl and the Pussy-cat* — or any poem of at least twelve lines — by heart and say it to somebody, standing up.
2. Clap the beat of a nursery rhyme, then clap the beat of this line and say how many strong beats it has: *The wind was a torrent of darkness among the gusty trees.*
3. Mark the rhyme pattern with letters: *I saw a ship a-sailing, / A-sailing on the sea, / And oh but it was laden / With pretty things for thee.*
4. Simile or metaphor? *(a)* The moon is a white coin. *(b)* Her coat was like a sack. *(c)* The fog crept in on its belly. *(d)* He ran as fast as the dog.
5. These are worn out. Invent a better ending for each: *as cold as ___, as quiet as ___, as dark as ___, as busy as ___.*
6. Personify three things: the kettle, the river, the old gate. One sentence each.
7. Find the alliteration and the onomatopoeia: *The black beck clattered and chuckled below the bridge.*
8. Write a list poem of eight lines: *Things in my grandmother's kitchen* or *Sounds of the morning*. Every line must be a thing you could actually see, hear or smell.
9. Write two haiku — one about the weather today, one about an animal. Count the syllables: 5, 7, 5.
10. Write a ballad of four verses (ABCB) telling a true thing that happened to your family. Give it a refrain if you can.
11. Take this flat sentence and make one line of poetry from it, using an image: *It rained a lot and the street got wet.*
12. Copy out your favourite poem in your best handwriting and write two sentences underneath saying why you chose it.

## Answers

1. No answer — but hear it said, not recited into a book.
2. Four strong beats: *wind, torrent, darkness, gusty* (the line is Noyes's *The Highwayman*, and it gallops on purpose).
3. ABCB — only *sea* and *thee* rhyme. This is the ballad pattern, and it is everywhere.
4. *(a)* metaphor *(b)* simile *(c)* personification (also a metaphor — the fog is given an animal's body) *(d)* simile.
5. Anything exact and their own. Good ones from real children: *as cold as a church floor*, *as quiet as snow landing*, *as dark as the inside of a coat*, *as busy as a station*. The mark of a good answer is that nobody has said it before.
6. E.g. *The kettle grumbled itself awake. The river shouldered the bank aside. The old gate complained every time anyone touched it.* Look for a verb that only a person could do.
7. Alliteration: *black, beck, below, bridge* — and the hard *c* of *clattered, chuckled*. Onomatopoeia: *clattered*, *chuckled*.
8. Judge on concreteness: eight real things, not eight feelings. If a line could belong to anybody's kitchen, it is not finished.
9. Count the syllables together, out loud, on fingers. Hold to 5–7–5 at this stage — the discipline is the lesson.
10. Check the shape first (four lines a verse, second and fourth rhyming), then the story. A refrain is a bonus, not a requirement.
11. E.g. *The rain came down all afternoon and the street turned to a black mirror.* Anything with one exact image counts; anything with *very wet* does not.
12. No fixed answer. Keep the folder.
