# Speaking and debate

**What they need first:** *Thinking clearly* for the logic; *The essay* for building a case. This lesson puts both in the mouth.

Most of what anybody decides is decided out loud. A person who can explain a thing clearly, listen properly and disagree without making an enemy has more influence over their own life than one who cannot — and that is true in a family, a workshop or a village meeting. It is also the most teachable of all the English skills, because it can only be learned by doing it, and doing it is free.

**A note for the teacher.** This lesson needs an audience, even an audience of two. Make it routine rather than an event: one short talk a week, at the table, and everyone takes a turn including you. A child who is allowed to stay silent for three years will find it far harder at twenty.

## Preparing a talk

1. **One idea.** Not six. What is the single thing they should remember tomorrow?
2. **Three points** that build it. Three is what an audience can hold.
3. **An opening that earns attention** — a question, a surprising fact, a short story, an object held up. Never *Today I am going to talk about*.
4. **An ending they will remember** — the one idea again, in different words, and then stop. Do not trail off with *so, yeah, that's it*.
5. **Notes, not a script.** Six words a point on a small card. A read-out talk is dead, and a memorised one collapses the moment a line is lost, whereas notes let you talk to people.
6. **Say it aloud twice** before saying it to anybody. Time it.

Hold something up if you can. An object, a drawing, a tool, the thing itself. People remember what they saw.

## Voice, body and nerves

- **Slower than feels natural.** Nerves speed everybody up. Deliberately slow.
- **Louder than feels natural**, aimed at the person furthest away.
- **Pause.** At a full stop, and before something important. A silence feels endless to the speaker and about right to the audience; it is the single most effective thing an inexperienced speaker can add.
- **Look at people** — a few seconds each, moving round. Eyes on the floor lose a room in thirty seconds.
- **Stand still, hands out of pockets.** Fidgeting is what the audience will watch instead of listening.
- **Nerves are normal and useful.** The shake is the body getting ready. Breathe out slowly before starting — out, not in — and begin with your first sentence already decided, because the first sentence is the hardest.
- **If you lose your place**, stop, look at your card, and carry on. The audience minds far less than you do.

## Listening, which is the other half

Being listened to is rare enough that it is persuasive in itself.

- **Do not plan your reply while they speak.** This is the commonest failure, and everybody can tell.
- **Look at the speaker. Do not interrupt.** Let a silence sit before you answer.
- **Play it back:** *So you are saying that…* — and let them correct you. Half of all arguments end here, because they turn out to be misunderstandings.
- **Ask a real question**, not a disguised statement. *Have you considered that you are wrong about the drainage?* is not a question.
- **Open questions get you somewhere**: *what happened next? how did you decide? what would change your mind?* Closed questions only confirm.

## Debate, and its one rule

The rule: **answer the argument, not the person.** Everything else follows from it.

A formal debate is worth running properly a few times, because the form teaches the habit:

- A **motion** — one clear sentence that can be agreed or disagreed with. *This house would keep goats rather than sheep.*
- **Proposition** speaks, then **opposition**, alternating; each has a fixed time.
- **Points of information** — a brief challenge offered during another's speech, which the speaker may accept or wave away.
- **Summaries** at the end from each side: no new argument, just the case at its strongest.
- A **vote**, taken before and after. What moved is the interesting number.

**The steel man.** Before you argue against a position, state it so well that somebody who holds it would say *yes, that is exactly what I think*. Only then answer it. This is the opposite of the straw man, it is the mark of a serious person, and it is a rule worth making absolute in your household.

**Things that are never allowed:** attacking the person rather than the point; misrepresenting what they said; shouting the other down; inventing facts. Name them when they happen, calmly, and carry on.

**Changing your mind is winning, not losing.** Say it out loud when it happens — *you have convinced me* — and praise it in a child louder than you praise a clever argument. A household where changing your mind is admitted freely makes far better decisions than one where it is a humiliation.

## Fallacies to catch, in speech

*Thinking clearly* covers these properly; these are the ones that come up out loud:

- **Straw man** — answering a weaker version of what was said.
- **Ad hominem** — attacking the speaker instead of the point.
- **False choice** — "either we do this or we starve", when there are five other options.
- **Appeal to authority** — "the book says", when the book may be wrong or about somewhere else.
- **Anecdote as proof** — "my grandfather did it this way and was fine".
- **Moving the goalposts** — changing what would count as being convinced, after being shown it.

Teach the child to name them politely: *I think that is a false choice — is there a third option?* Naming it without sneering is the skill.

## Settling a real disagreement

The practical end of the lesson, for a household or a group that has to live together afterwards:

1. **Agree what the question actually is.** Write it down. Often this alone resolves it.
2. **Each side states the other's case** until the other side agrees it is fair.
3. **Separate facts from preferences.** Facts can be checked; preferences can only be traded. Go and check the facts if you can.
4. **List the options** — usually more than two.
5. **Decide how you will decide** — consensus, a vote, or one person's call because it is their job. Agree that *before* you argue about the answer.
6. **Decide, write down what was decided and why, and set a date to review it.** The written note stops the same argument recurring in March.

## Exercises

1. Prepare and give a two-minute talk on something you know how to do. One idea, three points, an object to hold up. Notes on one card only.
2. Give the same talk again a day later, half a step slower, with a deliberate three-second pause before your most important sentence. Ask the audience which version they preferred.
3. Practise an opening: write three different first sentences for a talk on keeping bees — a question, a surprising fact, a short story.
4. Listen to somebody describe something for two minutes without speaking at all, then play their point back in one sentence beginning *So you are saying…* Ask them to correct you.
5. Turn each into an open question: *Did you like it? Was that hard? Do you agree?*
6. Take a view you disagree with and write it out so well that somebody who holds it would say "yes, that is exactly it". Then, and only then, answer it.
7. Hold a debate: *This house would spend a week's work on a better roof rather than on a bigger garden.* Two speakers a side, three minutes each, votes before and after.
8. Name the fallacy: "You would say that, you have always liked sheep." / "Either we cut the wood now or we freeze." / "My grandfather never boiled water and he lived to ninety." / "The book says to sow in March, so March it is."
9. Somebody says "we should not bother saving seed". Answer the argument, not the person, in three sentences.
10. Take a real disagreement in your household and run the six steps. Write down the question, the options, how you decided, and the review date.
11. Speak for one minute, with no preparation, on a subject someone gives you on the spot. Then again on another. The aim is not brilliance; it is not stopping.
12. Watch or listen to somebody arguing in earnest — a neighbour, a reading of a speech, an argument in a book. List what they did well and what they did badly, using this lesson's terms.

## Answers

1. Mark against: one idea, three points, an opening that is not *today I am going to talk about*, an ending that stops, and notes rather than a script.
2. No fixed answer. Audiences nearly always prefer the slower version, which is the lesson.
3. Look for variety and for an opening that would make somebody look up. E.g. *How many bees does it take to make one spoonful of honey?*
4. The correction is the point. If they say "no, what I meant was…", the exercise has worked.
5. *What did you think of it? What was the hard part? Where do you disagree?*
6. Mark it on whether the holder of the view would accept it. If they would not, it is still a straw man.
7. No fixed answer. The interesting result is the movement between the two votes, not who won.
8. Ad hominem. False choice. Anecdote as proof (and survivorship — you do not hear from the ones who died). Appeal to authority.
9. Look for engagement with the actual reason (cost, effort, whether saved seed comes true) and no attack on the person. E.g. *Saving seed costs a morning and a dry jar, and it means next year's crop does not depend on anyone else. It is true that some varieties do not come true from saved seed — so save from the ones that do, and buy only those that will not.*
10. No fixed answer. Look for the written question and the review date; those are the two parts everyone skips.
11. No fixed answer. Praise carrying on over being clever.
12. Look for the vocabulary being used accurately — straw man, false choice, pausing, eye contact — rather than for a harsh verdict.
