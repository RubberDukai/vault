# Reading for meaning

**What they need first:** reading a simple book aloud without stumbling over most words. If they are still sounding out every word, go back to *Learning to read* — comprehension cannot be taught on top of a struggle with decoding, because all of the child's attention is going into the decoding.

At KS1 the question was *can you read this word?* At KS2 it becomes *what is this book telling you, and how do you know?* The skill has three parts, and they are taught in this order: read it fluently, understand what it says, then work out what it does not say outright.

## The daily habit

Three things every day, and they are not the same thing:

- **Reading aloud to them, above their own level.** Twenty minutes. A book harder than they can read alone, read well by you. This is where vocabulary, sentence rhythm and the appetite for stories come from. Keep it up long after they can read alone — through KS3 if they will let you.
- **Reading aloud by them.** Ten minutes, a book they can manage with about one word in twenty unfamiliar. Too easy and nothing grows; too hard and it becomes a battle. Do not correct every slip — only the ones that break the sense.
- **Reading silently, alone.** Twenty minutes, anything they choose, including the same book for the fifth time. Comics count. Re-reading is not laziness; it is how fluency is built.

Then, once a day, *talk about it*. Four or five minutes, no writing. The talking is the lesson.

## Fluency: the mechanics

Fluent reading is accurate, at a sensible pace, and *expressive* — the voice shows the meaning. A child who reads in a flat monotone usually is not following the sense.

- **Punctuation is stage directions.** Practise a page: stop at full stops, lift at question marks, pause at commas, change voice at speech marks.
- **Echo reading.** You read a sentence the way it should sound; they read the same sentence back.
- **Reader's theatre.** Take a scene with dialogue — Toad in *The Wind in the Willows*, Long John Silver — give out parts and perform it. Nothing improves expression faster.
- **Re-read for speed, once.** The same short passage on three days. They hear themselves get better.

## Unfamiliar words

The most important habit of all: *do not skip the word, and do not stop dead*. Teach the four-step move and make it a reflex.

1. **Read to the end of the sentence.** Very often the sentence explains its own word.
2. **Look inside the word.** Prefixes and roots: *un-* (not), *re-* (again), *dis-* (opposite), *pre-* (before), *sub-* (under), *tele-* (far), *port* (carry), *scrib/script* (write), *aqua* (water), *terr* (earth), *vis* (see), *audi* (hear). *Inaudible* is *in + audi + ible*: cannot be heard.
3. **Try a substitute.** Say a word that would fit and carry on. *The path was strewn with leaves* — "covered" fits; near enough to keep going.
4. **Look it up, later.** Keep a slip of paper in the book. At the end of the session, look up two or three words in the dictionary or search the Vault, and write them in a vocabulary book: the word, what it means in this sentence, and the sentence it came from. Two or three a day is six hundred a year.

## The four questions

Every comprehension exercise ever set is one of these four. Teach them by name, so the child knows which kind of thinking is being asked for.

| Question | What it asks | Signal words | Where the answer is |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Retrieval** | What does it say? | who, what, where, when, how many | on the page, in the words |
| **Inference** | What does it mean without saying? | why, how do you know, what does this suggest | between the lines — a clue plus what you know |
| **Prediction** | What will happen? | what next, what would happen if | ahead of the page, built on clues |
| **Summary** | What was that about? | in a few words, the main idea | the whole page, held in your head |

**Retrieval** is the easy one, and the one to insist on: *point to the words that tell you*. A child who answers from memory rather than from the page will guess wrong all through KS3.

**Inference** is the heart of it, and the answer always has two halves. Teach the sentence frame: **"I think ____ because the text says ____."** *I think Mary is lonely because the text says she played by herself and no one came.* Never accept the first half alone.

**Prediction** is inference pointed forwards, and being wrong is fine as long as the reason was good. Ask *what made you think that?* and a wrong prediction is still a good lesson.

**Summarising** is the hardest. Build it in three sizes: one sentence for the chapter, then one sentence for the whole book, then — the test of real understanding — the book in ten words.

## Digging in: five more moves

- **Character.** *What kind of person is this? Which line shows it?* People are shown by what they do and say, not by the author announcing it.
- **The author's choice of words.** *Why "crept" and not "walked"?* Swap the word for a plain one and see what is lost.
- **Fact or opinion.** *The castle is on a hill* / *The castle is the finest in England*. Hunt for both in the same paragraph.
- **The writer's purpose.** To tell a story, to explain, to persuade, to instruct. Ask it of adverts and notices as well as of books — this is where reading turns into *Thinking clearly* at KS3.
- **Themes.** Late KS2: *what is this book really about, underneath the plot?* Courage, home, fairness, growing up.

## Non-fiction is a different skill

Story books are read start to finish. Information books are *used*, and that has to be taught.

- **Find your way about.** Contents, index, chapter headings, glossary, captions, diagrams, the key on a map. Set timed hunts: *find the page that tells you about roof timbers — twenty seconds.*
- **Skim** for the gist (read headings and first lines, look at the pictures), **scan** for one fact (run the eye down the page for the word), **read closely** when it matters.
- **Note-making.** Read a paragraph, cover it, write it in one line in your own words. Copying out is not note-making and teaches nothing.
- **Check it.** Who wrote this, when, and how would they know? A survival manual and a stranger's opinion are not the same kind of evidence.

Practise on the real thing: the *Handbook* in this Vault, a Wikipedia article, the OpenStax textbooks, a seed packet, a set of instructions.

## The shelf

Three **Reading shelf** packs are on the Setup page — public-domain books from [Project Gutenberg](wiki:Project_Gutenberg) with their original illustrations. They are small (the whole of the first shelf is about 12 MB) and they are the spine of this lesson.

**Ages 4–7 (10 books).** [*The Tale of Peter Rabbit*](wiki:The_Tale_of_Peter_Rabbit), [*Aesop's Fables*](wiki:Aesop's_Fables), [*Grimms' Fairy Tales*](wiki:Brothers_Grimm), [Hans Christian Andersen](wiki:Hans_Christian_Andersen), [*Just So Stories*](wiki:Just_So_Stories), *A Child's Garden of Verses*, [Edward Lear's nonsense](wiki:Edward_Lear), [*Alice's Adventures in Wonderland*](wiki:Alice's_Adventures_in_Wonderland), [*The Wonderful Wizard of Oz*](wiki:The_Wonderful_Wizard_of_Oz), [*Pinocchio*](wiki:The_Adventures_of_Pinocchio|Pinocchio). Mostly read aloud by you.

**Ages 7–11 (26 books).** The core of KS2. A rough order of difficulty, easiest first:

1. *The Wind in the Willows* — [Kenneth Grahame](wiki:Kenneth_Grahame). Character and comedy; Toad is unforgettable, and every child can tell you what kind of creature he is.
2. *The Railway Children* and *Five Children and It* — [E. Nesbit](wiki:E._Nesbit|Edith_Nesbit). Ordinary children, clear prose, good for inferring what the grown-ups are not saying.
3. [*Black Beauty*](wiki:Black_Beauty) — [Anna Sewell](wiki:Anna_Sewell). Told by the horse: a first lesson in point of view, and in a book written to change people's minds.
4. [*The Secret Garden*](wiki:The_Secret_Garden) — [Frances Hodgson Burnett](wiki:Frances_Hodgson_Burnett). Character change across a whole book — Mary at the start and Mary at the end.
5. *Heidi*, *The Story of Doctor Dolittle*, [*Peter Pan*](wiki:Peter_Pan), *Little Lord Fauntleroy*, *The Water-Babies*.
6. [*The Jungle Book*](wiki:The_Jungle_Book) — [Rudyard Kipling](wiki:Rudyard_Kipling). Harder language; an excellent read-aloud.
7. *Tales from Shakespeare* — [Charles and Mary Lamb](wiki:Tales_from_Shakespeare|Charles_Lamb|William_Shakespeare). The plots in prose, so that the plays are not strangers later.
8. [*The Arabian Nights*](wiki:One_Thousand_and_One_Nights|Arabian_Nights), *Tanglewood Tales* and *A Wonder Book* — the [Greek myths](wiki:Greek_mythology) and the story within a story.
9. [*Treasure Island*](wiki:Treasure_Island) and *Kidnapped* — [Robert Louis Stevenson](wiki:Robert_Louis_Stevenson); *The Merry Adventures of Robin Hood* — [Howard Pyle](wiki:Howard_Pyle|Robin_Hood); *The Coral Island*; [*Swiss Family Robinson*](wiki:The_Swiss_Family_Robinson).
10. *Tom Sawyer*, [*Anne of Green Gables*](wiki:Anne_of_Green_Gables), [*Little Women*](wiki:Little_Women), *Through the Looking-Glass*, [*The Call of the Wild*](wiki:The_Call_of_the_Wild), [*Around the World in Eighty Days*](wiki:Around_the_World_in_Eighty_Days).

**Ages 11 and up (25 books).** Waiting for KS3 — Dickens, the Brontës, Austen, Verne, Wells, Sherlock Holmes, Homer, the complete Shakespeare. A strong reader can start on *A Christmas Carol*, *The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes* or *The Time Machine* at the top of KS2.

**How to run a book.** One chapter a day, read by them or to them. Four or five minutes of talk afterwards — one retrieval question, one inference question, one prediction. At the end of the book: a one-sentence summary, a favourite line copied out with a note on why, and a drawing or a map of the place. Four or five books a year done properly beats thirty skimmed.

## Exercises

Use a chapter the child has just read; these work with any book on the shelf. The passage below is for the questions that need a fixed text.

> The door of the cottage stood open, though the morning was bitter. Tom stopped at the gate with the milk pail in both hands and looked at the empty step. Every day for six years Mrs Hale had been sitting there when he came, with her shawl round her and a word for him. He set the pail down very quietly, and ran.

1. **Retrieval.** What was Tom carrying? What was the weather like? How long had Tom been coming to the cottage?
2. **Inference.** How does Tom feel at the end? Quote the words that tell you.
3. **Inference.** Why did he set the pail down *quietly*?
4. **Prediction.** What do you think he does next, and what in the passage made you think so?
5. **Word choice.** The writer says the morning was *bitter*, not *cold*. What does *bitter* add?
6. **Summary.** The passage in one sentence. Then in five words.
7. **Vocabulary in context.** Work this out without a dictionary: *The old ewe was so decrepit that she could no longer climb the bank to the top field.* What does *decrepit* mean, and which words in the sentence told you?
8. **Fact or opinion.** Label each: *The river is eleven miles long. / The river is the most beautiful in the county. / Three houses were flooded. / The council should have dredged it.*
9. **Character.** Take any character from the chapter you have read. Say what kind of person they are and give two lines from the book that show it — not lines where the author tells you, lines where the character does or says something.
10. **Non-fiction.** Open the Handbook in this Vault at the chapter on lighting a fire. Without reading it all: how many methods does it list? Now close it and write, in your own words, the steps of one of them.
11. **Purpose.** Find a page of instructions, a page of a story, and a notice or advertisement. For each: what is the writer trying to make you do or feel, and what did they do with the words to manage it?
12. **The whole book.** When you finish your current book: one sentence on what it was about, one sentence on what the writer wanted you to think, and your favourite line copied out with a sentence on why you chose it.

## Answers

1. A milk pail, in both hands. Bitter — very cold. Six years.
2. Alarmed, or frightened — something is wrong. *"He set the pail down very quietly, and ran."* The running is the clue; a person does not run from an ordinary morning.
3. Accept anything reasoned: so as not to spill it, or so as not to make a noise in case something is wrong inside. The good answer notices that *quietly* is an odd way to put down a pail, and that it tells you Tom is already afraid of what he will find.
4. Any prediction with a reason from the text — he goes into the cottage, or fetches help. The reason matters more than the prediction.
5. *Bitter* is colder than cold, and it carries harshness — a cold that hurts. It also sets a bleak mood before anything has happened, which is the real reason the writer chose it.
6. E.g. *Tom arrives with the milk, finds that the old woman who always waits for him is not there, and runs.* Five words: *Tom finds the step empty.*
7. Worn out by age, falling apart. The clues: *old*, and *could no longer climb* — she used to be able to.
8. Fact / opinion / fact / opinion.
9. Judge it on the evidence: two quotations of action or speech, not description. *Toad promising he will never touch a motor-car again* — and then touching one — shows him far better than being told he is vain.
10. Check against the Handbook. The second half is the point: their own words, not copied.
11. Instructions — to make you *do* something: numbered steps, imperative verbs (*cut*, *fold*), no description. A story — to make you *feel*, and keep reading: description, dialogue, suspense. An advertisement — to make you *want*: superlatives, promises, and an instruction at the end.
12. No fixed answer. Keep these; a shelf of one-sentence summaries at the end of a year is the record of a reader growing up.
