# Graphs, tables and keeping records

**What they need first:** *Decimals and percentages*; the ability to measure and write down what was measured.

A graph is a picture of numbers, drawn so that the eye can do work the head would find slow. That is all it is, and a child who understands it that way will never be impressed by a graph that is merely decorative.

This lesson matters more than it looks. A household that keeps records knows when the potatoes really go in, how much fuel a winter takes and whether the goat's milk is falling off — and a household that does not keeps guessing. The maths is easy; the habit is the lesson.

## Collecting: tally and table

A **tally** counts things as they happen, in fives: four strokes and one across. It is quick, it does not need a number written for every item, and it can be added up afterwards.

A **table** is where the counts land. Every table needs three things or it is worthless in a year's time:

1. A **title** saying what was counted.
2. **Headings with units** — kg, mm, °C, litres, eggs.
3. The **date**, and where, and who measured.

Teach that as a rule. A column of numbers with no units is a column of nothing; a page of readings with no date cannot be compared with next year's.

## The four graphs, and when each is honest

| Graph | Shows | Use it when |
|---|---|---|
| **Pictogram** | counts, with a symbol for each unit | young children; small counts; the key must say what one symbol means |
| **Bar chart** | comparing separate categories | eggs per hen, rainfall per month, tools per type — **bars have gaps** |
| **Line graph** | change over time | temperature through a day, weight of a lamb, water in the butt — the line means "in between, it was somewhere along here" |
| **Pie chart** | parts of one whole | how the store is divided, where the day went — only when the parts add to 100% |

**The rule that decides which:** if the thing along the bottom is *separate things*, use bars with gaps between them. If it is *a continuous run of time or measurement*, use a line. A line graph joining Monday's eggs to Tuesday's is a small lie — there is no half-past-Monday egg count — but a line joining this morning's temperature to noon's is true, because the temperature really did pass through every value between.

**Drawing one properly:**

- Both axes labelled, with units.
- The scale even — every square worth the same amount, all the way along.
- A title that says what it shows.
- A key, if there is more than one line.
- Choose a scale that fills the paper; a graph squashed into a corner wastes the eye's work.

## Reading a graph somebody else drew

This is the half that adults need most. Four questions, in this order:

1. **What is on each axis, and in what units?**
2. **Where does the vertical axis start?** If it does not start at zero, a small difference has been made to look enormous. This is the commonest way an honest-looking graph misleads — so say it out loud: *does that axis start at nought?*
3. **What is the trend?** Rising, falling, flat, or up and down in a cycle. Point at it with a finger.
4. **What does it not tell you?** How many were measured, over how long, by whom, and what happened just off the edge of the paper.

Teach them to find the **steepest part** of a line and say what was happening there, and to find where it **crosses** another line and say what that means. Those two questions answer most of what a graph is for.

## The mean, and the two it hides

The **mean** — add them up, divide by how many — is the everyday average. Nine eggs over three days is a mean of three a day.

But it can mislead, and a child should know how:

- **One extreme value drags it.** Five hens lay 4, 5, 5, 6 and 0 eggs a week. The mean is 4, but four of them are laying fine and one has stopped — and the mean tells you neither fact.
- Look also at the **range** (biggest minus smallest) and at the **middle value when they are lined up in order**. When the mean and the middle value are far apart, something odd is going on and it is worth finding out what.

So: quote the mean, but *also* look at the spread. That habit is the beginning of honest statistics, and it is continued at KS3 in *Chance, data and deciding*.

## The records a household should actually keep

Set these up as real, ruled books, and make keeping them a daily job for the child. The maths is the least of it — this is how a family stops being surprised.

- **Weather.** Rainfall (a straight-sided jar and a ruler), highest and lowest temperature, wind, frost. A year of it tells you your own last frost date, which no book can tell you.
- **The garden.** Date sown, date up, date first picked, weight cropped per bed. Within two seasons you know what pays for its space.
- **Animals.** Milk per day, eggs per day, weight gain, dates of service and birth.
- **Stores.** What came in, what went out, what is left — and the date. A running total is the difference between rationing early and running out suddenly.
- **Fuel and water.** How much used per week in summer and in winter. This is the number that sizes everything else.
- **Health.** Temperature, pulse, symptoms, and what was given and when. The Handbook's medical chapters are far more use when there is a record to read them against.

Each one is a table, and each one becomes a graph at the end of the month. When the graph of stores crosses the line of what you need, you have found out something in time to act — and that is the entire point of the lesson.

## Exercises

1. Tally these, then put them in a table with a title and units — eggs collected: 3, 5, 4, 6, 2, 5, 5.
2. For each, say which graph you would draw and why: (a) how many of each kind of tool you own; (b) the temperature every hour through one day; (c) how a day's work was divided between four jobs; (d) rainfall in each month of the year.
3. Draw a bar chart of the rainfall: Jan 88 mm, Feb 65, Mar 52, Apr 48, May 55, Jun 60.
4. Draw a line graph: a lamb weighed 4 kg at birth, then 7, 11, 15, 18, 20 kg at the end of each of the next five weeks. In which week did it grow fastest? What might explain the slowing?
5. Find the mean of 12, 15, 9, 14, 20. Then the range.
6. Five hens lay 4, 5, 5, 6 and 0 eggs in a week. What is the mean? Why is the mean a poor description here, and what would you say instead?
7. A graph of milk yield has a vertical axis starting at 3.4 litres and ending at 3.6, and the line looks like a cliff. What has happened, and how would you redraw it fairly?
8. From a line graph of water in a butt: full at 200 litres on the 1st, 150 on the 8th, 95 on the 15th, 40 on the 22nd. About how much is used a week? On what date will it run dry if nothing changes, and what should you do about it before then?
9. A pie chart of a store shows grain 50%, roots 25%, beans 15%, other 10%. The store holds 400 kg altogether. How many kg of each?
10. Design, on paper, the weather record you will keep for the next month: the columns, the units, and how often you will read it. Then keep it.

## Answers

1. Tally in fives; total 30 eggs over 7 days. The table needs a title ("Eggs collected"), a date column, and the unit (eggs).
2. (a) bar chart — separate categories; (b) line graph — continuous time; (c) pie chart — parts of one whole day; (d) bar chart — the months are separate categories, though a line is often used to show the shape of a year and is accepted if labelled clearly.
3. Check the axes are labelled with units (mm), the scale is even, the bars have gaps, and it has a title.
4. Fastest in the second week (4 kg gained: 7 → 11). The slowing is normal as a lamb grows and begins to take less milk — or a sign of less grass, or illness, which is exactly the kind of question the record makes askable.
5. Mean 14; range 11.
6. Mean 4. It is poor because one hen laying nothing is hidden inside it — four hens are laying 4–6, one has stopped. Better: "four hens are laying about five a week, one has stopped."
7. The axis does not start at zero, so a difference of a fraction of a litre fills the whole page. Redraw with the axis from 0, and the line is almost flat — which is the truth.
8. About 52–55 litres a week. From 40 litres on the 22nd it runs dry around the 27th or 28th. Act before then: refill, catch rain, or cut use now rather than on the day it empties.
9. Grain 200 kg, roots 100 kg, beans 60 kg, other 40 kg.
10. No fixed answer. Look for: units on every column, a date column, a stated reading time (the same time each day), and somewhere to note anything unusual.
