# Equations and formulae

**What they need first:** *Negative numbers and the start of algebra*; *Decimals and percentages*; fluent times tables, because algebra exposes shaky arithmetic immediately.

Algebra is arithmetic with a letter standing in for a number you do not know yet. It is worth the effort because a **formula solves a whole family of problems at once**: work out the area of one field with arithmetic, and you have one answer; write A = l × w and you have every field there will ever be.

## An equation is a balance

The two sides are equal, and they must stay equal. **Whatever you do to one side, do to the other.** Every method in this lesson is that one sentence.

Draw a balance scale for the first week and put objects on it. Take three off the left, take three off the right, it stays level. Halve the left, halve the right, it stays level. Once the picture is in the child's head, the symbols are a shorthand for something they already believe.

**Solving** means getting the letter alone on one side, by undoing what has been done to it — in reverse order, and with the opposite operation.

```
  3x + 7 = 22
  3x     = 15     (take 7 from both sides)
   x     = 5      (divide both sides by 3)
```

**Always check.** Put 5 back in: 3 × 5 + 7 = 22. ✓ Checking takes ten seconds, catches nearly every mistake, and a child who does it every time never has to wonder whether they are right.

**The letter on both sides:** collect them on the side where there are more of them, to avoid negatives.

```
  5x − 4 = 2x + 11
  3x − 4 = 11        (take 2x from both sides)
  3x     = 15        (add 4 to both sides)
   x     = 5
```

**Common slips**, each worth naming out loud: doing it to one side only; forgetting that −3x means *minus* three x; dividing only part of a side; and "moving it over and changing the sign", which is a trick that works right up until it doesn't. Teach the balance, not the trick.

## Brackets

**Expanding**: multiply everything inside by what is outside. 3(x + 4) = 3x + 12. The commonest error in all of school maths is forgetting the second term.

Watch a minus outside: −2(x − 5) = −2x + 10. Both signs change.

**Two brackets**: every term in the first meets every term in the second.
(x + 3)(x + 2) = x² + 2x + 3x + 6 = x² + 5x + 6.

**Factorising** is the reverse — putting the brackets back by taking out what is common. 6x + 9 = 3(2x + 3). Check by expanding it again; the check is instant and free.

## Formulae, and rearranging them

A formula is a rule in symbols. Some to know and use:

| Formula | What it gives | |
|---|---|---|
| A = l × w | area of a rectangle | |
| A = ½bh | area of a triangle | |
| C = πd, A = πr² | circle | |
| V = lwh | volume of a box | |
| P = 2(l + w) | perimeter | |
| s = d ÷ t | speed | distance ÷ time |
| °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9 | temperature | |

**Substituting** is putting the numbers in. Watch the order of operations — brackets, then powers, then × and ÷, then + and −.

**Rearranging (changing the subject)** is the skill that makes formulae genuinely useful, and it is the same balance rule again. You have s = d ÷ t, but you know the speed and the time and want the distance:

```
  s = d ÷ t
  s × t = d       (multiply both sides by t)
  so d = s × t
```

One formula now answers three questions instead of one. Practise it until a child never again needs to remember three versions of the same rule — which is the whole reason the triangle-of-formulae memory tricks exist, and why they are unnecessary.

## Powers and roots

- **x²** is x × x; **x³** is x × x × x. *Squared* and *cubed* are named after area and volume, which is worth pointing out.
- **Square root** undoes squaring: √49 = 7. Know the squares to 15 × 15 by heart; they come up constantly.
- **The rules:** x² × x³ = x⁵ (add the powers); x⁵ ÷ x² = x³ (subtract them); x⁰ = 1, which surprises people and follows straight from the division rule.
- **Standard form** for very big and very small numbers: 3,400,000 = 3.4 × 10⁶; 0.00072 = 7.2 × 10⁻⁴. Astronomy and chemistry are unusable without it.
- **Pythagoras' theorem**: in a right-angled triangle, a² + b² = c², where c is the longest side. This is the 3-4-5 corner from *Area, perimeter, angles and shape*, now general: 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5². Use it to find the length of a brace, a rafter or a diagonal — any diagonal you can measure two sides of.

## Sequences

A **sequence** is a list with a rule. 2, 5, 8, 11… goes up by 3 each time.

The **nth term** is the formula that gives any term you like without working through the whole list. For that sequence: the step is 3, so it is built on 3n; 3 × 1 = 3, but the first term is 2, so subtract 1. **3n − 1**. Check it: the 4th term is 3 × 4 − 1 = 11 ✓, and the 50th is 149 without writing out the list.

Worth knowing besides: the square numbers (1, 4, 9, 16), the triangular numbers (1, 3, 6, 10 — stacked rows), and the [Fibonacci](wiki:Fibonacci_number) sequence (1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13), where each term is the sum of the two before it and which turns up in the spirals of a pine cone and a sunflower head.

## Straight-line graphs

**y = mx + c** is a rule drawn as a picture. **m** is the gradient — how steep, and up or down. **c** is where it crosses the vertical axis — the starting amount.

Plot y = 2x + 1 by making a small table of x and y, marking the points and joining them. Then plot y = 2x + 3 beside it — parallel, higher. Then y = −x + 4 — sloping the other way. Three graphs and the roles of m and c are clear without being told.

**It is worth something in practice.** Water in a butt falling by 50 litres a week from 200: y = 200 − 50x. Draw it, and where the line meets zero is the week it runs dry. Two costs drawn as two lines cross at the point where one becomes cheaper than the other — which is a real decision made by drawing rather than by arguing.

## Exercises

1. Solve: x + 9 = 17; 5x = 45; x ÷ 4 = 7; 3x − 8 = 16; 2(x + 5) = 24.
2. Solve, checking each: 7x + 4 = 3x + 20; 5 − 2x = x − 7; 4(x − 3) = 2(x + 5).
3. Expand: 4(x + 6); −3(2x − 5); x(x + 7); (x + 4)(x + 3); (x − 2)(x + 6).
4. Factorise: 8x + 12; 15x − 25; x² + 5x; 6x² + 9x.
5. Substitute x = 3, y = −2: 4x + y; x² − y; 3(x + y); xy².
6. Rearrange to make the letter in brackets the subject: A = lw (w); v = u + at (a); C = πd (d); A = ½bh (h); P = 2(l + w) (l).
7. °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9. Convert 68 °F. Then rearrange to make °F the subject and convert 25 °C.
8. Work out: 6²; 2⁵; √81; √144; 3² × 3⁴; 5⁶ ÷ 5³; 7⁰.
9. Write in standard form: 45,000; 0.0038; 6,200,000. Write as ordinary numbers: 2.5 × 10³; 9 × 10⁻²·
10. A ladder reaches 4 m up a wall with its foot 3 m out. How long is the ladder? A gate is 1.2 m by 0.9 m — how long is the diagonal brace?
11. A rafter rises 2.5 m over a horizontal run of 6 m. How long is the rafter? Add 300 mm at each end for the overhang and eaves cut.
12. Give the next two terms and the nth term: 4, 7, 10, 13… ; 5, 10, 15, 20… ; 1, 4, 9, 16… ; 20, 17, 14, 11…
13. What is the 100th term of 3n + 2? Which term of 5n − 3 is equal to 97?
14. Plot y = 3x − 2 for x from −2 to 4. What is the gradient, and where does it cross the vertical axis?
15. A water butt holds 240 litres and loses 40 a week. Write the formula, draw the graph, and say in which week it empties.
16. Two ways of getting fuel: A costs 30 plus 5 a week; B costs nothing up front and 9 a week. Write both formulae, draw both lines, and say after how many weeks A becomes the cheaper.

## Answers

1. 8; 9; 28; 8; 7.
2. x = 4; x = 4; x = 11.
3. 4x + 24; −6x + 15; x² + 7x; x² + 7x + 12; x² + 4x − 12.
4. 4(2x + 3); 5(3x − 5); x(x + 5); 3x(2x + 3).
5. 10; 11; 3; 12.
6. w = A ÷ l; a = (v − u) ÷ t; d = C ÷ π; h = 2A ÷ b; l = (P ÷ 2) − w.
7. 20 °C. Rearranged: °F = °C × 9/5 + 32, so 25 °C = 77 °F.
8. 36; 32; 9; 12; 3⁶ = 729; 5³ = 125; 1.
9. 4.5 × 10⁴; 3.8 × 10⁻³; 6.2 × 10⁶. 2,500; 0.09.
10. √(16 + 9) = √25 = **5 m**. Gate: √(1.44 + 0.81) = √2.25 = **1.5 m**.
11. √(6² + 2.5²) = √(36 + 6.25) = √42.25 = **6.5 m**; plus 0.6 m = **7.1 m** to cut.
12. 16, 19 — 3n + 1. 25, 30 — 5n. 25, 36 — n². 8, 5 — 23 − 3n.
13. 302. 5n − 3 = 97 → 5n = 100 → n = **20**.
14. Gradient 3; crosses at −2.
15. y = 240 − 40x. It reaches zero at x = 6, so it empties during the sixth week.
16. A: 30 + 5x. B: 9x. They are equal when 30 + 5x = 9x → 4x = 30 → x = 7.5, so from **week 8** onwards A is cheaper.
