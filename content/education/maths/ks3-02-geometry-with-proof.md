# Geometry with proof

**What they need first:** *Area, perimeter, angles and shape* at KS2; *Equations and formulae*.

At KS2 the child learned that the angles of a triangle add to 180°. Here they learn **why that must be true, everywhere, for ever, for every triangle that could ever be drawn**. That is a different kind of knowing, and it is the most important idea in mathematics.

**What a proof is.** Start from things everyone agrees on. Move by steps everyone can check. Arrive somewhere nobody can now deny. A proof is not evidence and it is not a strong argument; measuring a thousand triangles is evidence, and it would still leave open the possibility of a strange one somewhere. A proof closes the question. Nothing else in human knowledge works quite like it, and a child should be told so.

## The starting facts

These are the agreed ground. Everything else is built from them.

- Angles on a straight line add to **180°**.
- Angles round a point add to **360°**.
- Vertically opposite angles (where two lines cross) are **equal**.
- Where a line crosses two parallel lines: **corresponding** angles are equal (an F shape), **alternate** angles are equal (a Z shape), and **co-interior** angles add to 180° (a C or U shape).

Learn the three parallel-line rules by their shapes, then stop using the shapes and use the names.

## The first proof: the angles of a triangle

Draw any triangle ABC. Through the vertex A draw a line parallel to the opposite side BC.

- The angle between that new line and AB equals angle B — **alternate angles**, because AB crosses two parallel lines.
- The angle between the new line and AC equals angle C — alternate angles again.
- Those two angles and angle A together lie along the straight line through A, so they add to **180°**.
- Therefore **A + B + C = 180°**. ∎

Have the child talk it through aloud, then write it out with a reason on every line. Notice what has happened: this is now true for *every* triangle, including ones nobody has drawn, because nothing in the argument depended on which triangle it was.

**Two more that follow immediately:**

- **A quadrilateral adds to 360°**, because a diagonal cuts it into two triangles.
- **Any polygon with n sides** adds to (n − 2) × 180°, because it cuts into n − 2 triangles. Test it on a pentagon: 540°.
- **The exterior angle of a triangle** equals the sum of the two opposite interior angles. (Proof: it sits on a straight line with the third angle, and all three angles add to 180°.)

## Congruence and similarity

- **Congruent** — identical in shape *and* size; one could be laid exactly on the other, allowing for turning or flipping.
- **Similar** — the same shape, different size; all angles equal and all sides in the same ratio.

Two triangles are **congruent** if any one of these holds: **SSS** (three sides), **SAS** (two sides and the angle between them), **ASA** (two angles and a side), **RHS** (right angle, hypotenuse and one other side).

**Why this matters practically:** SSS is the reason a triangle cannot be pushed out of shape — fix three lengths and the shape is fixed, with nothing left to vary. A four-sided frame has slack, which is why everything from a gate to a roof truss to a shelf bracket gets a diagonal. The bracing rule that was a fact at KS2 is a theorem here.

**Similarity is how you measure what you cannot reach.** A stick 1 m tall casts a shadow 1.5 m long; a tree's shadow is 18 m. The two triangles are similar, so the tree is 18 ÷ 1.5 = 12 m. No instrument, no trigonometry, one stick. Do it on a real tree.

## Pythagoras, proved

In a right-angled triangle, a² + b² = c².

**The proof by rearrangement**, which can be cut out of paper in ten minutes and is worth the time:

Take a large square of side (a + b). Place four copies of the right-angled triangle inside it, each in a corner, arranged so the space left in the middle is a square on the hypotenuse, c².
Now slide the same four triangles into a different arrangement in the same big square, so the space left is two squares: one of side a and one of side b.
The big square has not changed and the four triangles have not changed. **So the leftover space must be the same both times: c² = a² + b².** ∎

That argument would have convinced anybody in any century, and a child who has cut the pieces out and moved them owns it.

## Constructions

Straight edge and compasses only — no measuring. These are the oldest practical geometry there is, and each one is used in setting out real work.

- **Perpendicular bisector** of a line: compasses set to more than half the length, arc from each end above and below, join the crossings. Gives both the midpoint and a right angle.
- **Bisect an angle**: arc from the vertex crossing both arms, then equal arcs from those two points, join the crossing to the vertex.
- **A perpendicular from a point** to a line, and **at a point** on a line.
- **An equilateral triangle**, and from it a **60° angle**; bisect it for 30°. Bisect a right angle for 45°.
- **A hexagon in a circle**: keep the compasses at the radius and step round the circumference — it goes exactly six times, which is not a coincidence and is worth proving.

**Loci** — all the points obeying a rule: a fixed distance from a point (a circle); equidistant from two points (the perpendicular bisector); equidistant from two lines (the angle bisector). Used in earnest for siting a well between two dwellings, or a path that keeps clear of a boundary.

## Trigonometry: the height you cannot reach

In a right-angled triangle, the ratio of two sides depends only on the angle. That is the whole idea, and it follows from similarity — all right-angled triangles with the same angle are similar, so their sides are in the same ratio whatever their size.

Label the sides from the angle you know: **opposite**, **adjacent**, **hypotenuse** (always the longest, opposite the right angle).

- **sin** = opposite ÷ hypotenuse
- **cos** = adjacent ÷ hypotenuse
- **tan** = opposite ÷ adjacent

**SOH-CAH-TOA**, and it is worth the silly mnemonic.

**Tan is the workhorse.** Stand back a measured distance from a tree, measure the angle up to the top, and the height is distance × tan(angle). Make the instrument: a protractor, a straw taped along the flat edge to sight through, and a weighted thread hanging from the centre to read the angle. That, a tape and this formula will give you the height of any tree, wall or building you can stand back from.

Worth knowing without any table: **tan 45° = 1**. So when the angle up to the top is 45°, the height above your eye equals your distance from it — pace back until the angle is 45° and you have measured the tree by walking. Keep a written table of sin, cos and tan every 5° in the back of a notebook; it is a page, and it does not need a battery.

## Circles

- **Radius, diameter, chord, arc, sector** (a slice), **segment** (cut off by a chord), **tangent** (a line touching at one point).
- A **tangent is at right angles to the radius** at the point of contact.
- The **perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects it** — which is how you find the centre of a circle you did not draw: take two chords, bisect both, and the bisectors cross at the centre. Useful for a wheel, a lid or a millstone.
- The **angle in a semicircle is a right angle** — a fine, surprising result, and a way of setting out a right angle with a length of string.
- Arc length and sector area are just fractions of the whole: (angle ÷ 360) × circumference, and (angle ÷ 360) × area.

## Exercises

1. Write out the proof that the angles of a triangle add to 180°, with a reason on every line.
2. Prove that a quadrilateral's angles add to 360°. Then find the total for a pentagon, a hexagon and an octagon.
3. Find the missing angles, giving a reason for each: two straight lines cross and one angle is 130° — find the other three. A line crosses two parallel lines and one angle is 65° — find the corresponding, alternate and co-interior angles.
4. Explain the difference between congruent and similar, with an example of each from around you.
5. Why does a diagonal brace stop a gate sagging? Answer using SSS.
6. A stick 1.2 m tall casts a shadow 2 m long. A barn's shadow is 15 m. How tall is the barn? Draw the two triangles and say why the method works.
7. Cut out the four triangles and the big square and do the Pythagoras rearrangement proof. Explain in your own words why it proves the theorem.
8. A rectangular plot is 12 m by 9 m. How long is the diagonal? How would you use that number to check the plot is truly rectangular?
9. Construct, with straight edge and compasses only: the perpendicular bisector of a 10 cm line; a bisected angle; an equilateral triangle; a 30° angle; a hexagon inside a circle.
10. Why does a compass set to the radius step exactly six times round its own circle? (Hint: what kind of triangle is the centre and two neighbouring points?)
11. Two dwellings are 400 m apart. Mark all the points where a well would be the same distance from both. What is that line called?
12. Make the angle-measuring instrument described above. Use it to find the height of a tree, then check it by the shadow method. Do the two agree? If not, why might that be?
13. Pace back from a building until the angle to the top is 45°. What is its height above your eye, and why? Add your eye height for the total.
14. A ladder 5 m long leans with its foot 1.5 m from a wall. What angle does it make with the ground, and how far up does it reach?
15. Find the centre of a circle drawn round a plate, using only a straight edge and compasses. Describe every step.
16. A sector has an angle of 60° in a circle of radius 6 m. What is its area, and the length of its arc?

## Answers

1. Look for: a line drawn parallel to one side through the opposite vertex; alternate angles used twice, named; angles on a straight line summing to 180°; and a conclusion. A reason on every line is the mark of the exercise.
2. A diagonal divides it into two triangles, each 180°, so 360°. Pentagon 540°, hexagon 720°, octagon 1080° — (n − 2) × 180.
3. Crossing lines: 130° (vertically opposite), 50° and 50° (angles on a straight line). Parallel lines: corresponding 65°, alternate 65°, co-interior 115°.
4. Congruent: same shape and size — two identical fence pickets. Similar: same shape, different size — a plan of a room and the room.
5. The brace turns a four-sided frame into two triangles. Three fixed sides fix a triangle completely (SSS), leaving nothing free to move, whereas a four-sided frame with fixed sides can still change its angles and collapse into a parallelogram.
6. 15 ÷ 2 × 1.2 = **9 m**. The sun's rays arrive at the same angle for both, so the two triangles have the same angles and are therefore similar, which means their sides are in the same ratio.
7. Look for: the big square and the four triangles are the same in both arrangements, so the uncovered area must be equal — and that area is c² in one and a² + b² in the other.
8. √(144 + 81) = √225 = **15 m**. Measure both diagonals: if both are 15 m the plot is a true rectangle, because equal diagonals plus equal opposite sides force right angles.
9. Judge on method: arcs left visible, no measuring used, and accuracy within a millimetre or two.
10. The centre and two neighbouring points form a triangle with two sides equal to the radius and the third — the step — also equal to the radius. That is equilateral, so its angle at the centre is 60°, and 360 ÷ 60 = 6 exactly.
11. The perpendicular bisector of the line joining them.
12. No fixed answer. The two methods usually agree within a few per cent; differences come from an uneven ground, an unclear shadow tip, a tree leaning, or a misread angle. Discussing the disagreement is more valuable than getting them to match.
13. The height above eye level equals the distance you paced, because tan 45° = 1, so opposite = adjacent. Add your eye height for the full height.
14. cos(angle) = 1.5 ÷ 5 = 0.3, so the angle is about **72.5°**. Height up the wall = √(25 − 2.25) = √22.75 ≈ **4.77 m**.
15. Draw any two chords across the circle. Construct the perpendicular bisector of each. They cross at the centre — because the perpendicular bisector of any chord passes through the centre.
16. Area = (60 ÷ 360) × π × 36 = 6π ≈ **18.85 m²**. Arc = (60 ÷ 360) × π × 12 = 2π ≈ **6.28 m**.
