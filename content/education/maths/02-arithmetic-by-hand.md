# Arithmetic by hand

There will be no calculator. This is not a hardship — hand arithmetic is a skill people held for thousands of years and it is entirely learnable. It just needs practice, daily, in small amounts.

## Addition with carrying

Line the numbers up **by place value**, right-hand edges aligned. Add each column from the right. When a column totals ten or more, write the ones digit and carry the ten into the next column.

```
   1 (carry)
   3 4 7
 + 1 8 5
 ─────────
   5 3 2
```

7 + 5 = 12: write 2, carry 1. Then 4 + 8 + 1 = 13: write 3, carry 1. Then 3 + 1 + 1 = 5.

The carry is just "ten ones became one ten". Say that aloud while doing it and it stops being a rule to memorise.

## Subtraction with borrowing

```
   2 13 (borrowed)
   3 4 7
 - 1 8 5
 ─────────
   1 6 2
```

7 − 5 = 2. Then 4 − 8 does not work, so borrow: take one hundred from the 3, making it 2, and add it to the tens as ten tens, making 14. Now 14 − 8 = 6. Then 2 − 1 = 1.

**Check by adding back:** 162 + 185 should equal 347. Teach this check from the beginning — it makes the child self-correcting.

## Multiplication

Learn the tables to 12 × 12. There is no way around it and it is the highest-return memorisation in all of school mathematics. Chant them, sing them, test at meals.

Some are free: **×10** adds a zero. **×5** is half of ×10. **×9** — hold up ten fingers and fold down the *n*th; the fingers left either side are the tens and the ones. **×11** for two digits: 3 × 11 = 33, and 12 × 11 = 132 (split the digits, add them in the middle).

Long multiplication:

```
     3 4 7
 ×      2 6
 ───────────
   2 0 8 2      (347 × 6)
 6 9 4 0        (347 × 20 — note the shift)
 ───────────
 9 0 2 2
```

The shift on the second line is not a trick — you are multiplying by *twenty*, so the answer is ten times 347 × 2.

## Division

Long division, one digit at a time from the left. 902 ÷ 4:

- 9 ÷ 4 = 2, remainder 1. Write 2.
- Bring down the 0 to make 10. 10 ÷ 4 = 2, remainder 2. Write 2.
- Bring down the 2 to make 22. 22 ÷ 4 = 5, remainder 2. Write 5.
- Answer: **225 remainder 2**.

Check by multiplying back: 225 × 4 = 900, plus 2 = 902.

## Estimating first — the most practical habit

Before any calculation, **round and estimate.** 347 × 26 is roughly 350 × 25 = 8,750. So an answer of 9,022 is plausible; an answer of 902 or 90,220 means a mistake.

This habit catches almost every serious arithmetic error, and it is more useful in real life than exact calculation. Teach it as an inseparable part of every sum.

## Exercises

1. 486 + 297, 1,205 + 899, 67 + 458 + 12
2. 803 − 476, 1,000 − 347, 512 − 89
3. 47 × 8, 236 × 14, 108 × 25
4. 738 ÷ 6, 945 ÷ 7, 1,000 ÷ 8 (give the remainder)
5. Estimate first, then calculate: 612 × 39
6. Real problem: 4 people share 27 potatoes equally. How many each, and how many left over?
7. Real problem: a bag of grain weighs 25 kg and feeds one person for 40 days. How many days does it feed a family of 5? How many bags for a year?

## Answers

1. 783; 2,104; 537.
2. 327; 653; 423.
3. 376; 3,304; 2,700.
4. 123; 135; 125 (no remainder in any — 738 = 6 × 123, 945 = 7 × 135, 1,000 = 8 × 125).
5. Estimate 600 × 40 = 24,000; exact 23,868.
6. 6 each, 3 left over.
7. 8 days (40 ÷ 5); 46 bags for a year (365 ÷ 8 = 45.6, so round up).

## Going further

- **Doubling and halving** for multiplication: 16 × 25 = 8 × 50 = 4 × 100 = 400.
- **Times tables patterns** — the 9s always have digits summing to 9. The 3s do too, for every multiple. This is the divisibility rule and it works for any number.
- **Casting out nines**, a medieval checking method that catches most arithmetic errors quickly. Worth teaching once the basics are solid.
- **The abacus** or a counting board. Fast, physical, and it needs no paper — which matters when paper runs short.
