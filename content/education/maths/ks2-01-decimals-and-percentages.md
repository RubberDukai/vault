# Decimals and percentages

**What they need first:** *Arithmetic by hand* — the four operations with whole numbers, done reliably. *Fractions, ratio and measuring* for the idea of a fraction; this lesson and that one are two ways of writing the same thing, and the child should end up able to move between them without thinking.

A decimal is not a new kind of number. It is place value carried on past the units column, and everything already learned about columns still holds. Say that out loud at the start, because children who are told decimals are a separate topic spend years believing 0.7 is smaller than 0.25 — "because 25 is bigger than 7".

## The places

| 100 | 10 | 1 | **·** | 1/10 | 1/100 | 1/1000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| hundreds | tens | units | point | tenths | hundredths | thousandths |

Each step to the right divides by ten, exactly as each step to the left multiplies by ten. The point is not a place — it is the mark that shows where the units column is.

**Read them properly.** 3.47 is *three point four seven*, never "three point forty-seven". The second habit tells the child there is a number called "forty-seven" after the point, and that is the misunderstanding at the root of most decimal errors.

**Money is the way in.** £3.47 is three pounds, four tens of pence, seven pence. Every child who can handle coins already understands hundredths; the job is to show them they do.

**Compare by lining up the points**, never by length. Which is bigger, 0.7 or 0.25? Write them one above the other:

```
0.70
0.25
```

Filling the gap with a zero changes nothing — seven tenths is seventy hundredths — and now it is plain. **Zeros on the end after the point are free; zeros in the middle are not.** 0.07 and 0.7 are ten times apart.

**On the number line.** Draw a line from 0 to 1 and mark tenths. Put 0.5, 0.25, 0.9, 0.05 on it. Then zoom in: draw the stretch from 0.2 to 0.3 as a whole line and mark hundredths on it. This zooming is the whole idea, and it is worth doing on paper several times.

## The three that must be automatic

| Fraction | Decimal | Percentage |
|---|---|---|
| ½ | 0.5 | 50% |
| ¼ | 0.25 | 25% |
| ¾ | 0.75 | 75% |
| ⅕ | 0.2 | 20% |
| ⅒ | 0.1 | 10% |
| ⅓ | 0.333… | 33⅓% |

Learn that table by heart. It does more work in adult life than any other six lines of maths.

**Fraction to decimal:** divide the top by the bottom. ⅜ is 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375. Some do not stop — ⅓ is 0.3333… for ever, which is not a failure of the method but a fact about thirds.

**Decimal to fraction:** say it and write it. 0.6 is "six tenths" = 6/10 = ⅗. 0.04 is "four hundredths" = 4/100 = 1/25.

## Adding and subtracting

**Line up the points.** That is the whole rule, and it is the same rule as lining up the columns, because it *is* lining up the columns. Fill the short one with zeros so both have the same number of places.

```
  12.40        8.05
+  3.75      − 2.60
-------      ------
  16.15        5.45
```

The commonest error is ragged right-hand ends — a child lines up 12.4 and 3.75 by their last digits and gets nonsense. Insist on the zero.

## Multiplying and dividing by 10, 100, 1000

The digits move; the point stays still. This is worth being fussy about, because "move the point" works until it doesn't.

- 3.6 × 10 = 36 — every digit shifts one place to the left.
- 3.6 ÷ 10 = 0.36 — every digit shifts one place to the right.
- 0.045 × 1000 = 45.

This is also all that converting metric units is: mm → cm → m → km is ÷10, ÷100, ÷1000. A child fluent here never has to memorise a conversion table.

## Multiplying decimals

Ignore the point, multiply as whole numbers, then put the point back by counting decimal places.

**2.4 × 1.3.** 24 × 13 = 312. There is one decimal place in 2.4 and one in 1.3, so two altogether: **3.12**.

**Check it is sensible first.** 2.4 is about 2, 1.3 is about 1, so the answer is about 3 — and 3.12 is about 3. Teach the estimate *before* the calculation, every time. It catches the misplaced point, which is the only error that matters.

Two things that surprise children and are worth showing on purpose:

- Multiplying by a number less than one makes it **smaller**: 8 × 0.5 = 4. "Multiplying makes bigger" is a KS1 rule that expires here.
- Dividing by a number less than one makes it **bigger**: 8 ÷ 0.5 = 16, because you are asking *how many halves are in eight?*

## Dividing decimals

**By a whole number:** divide as usual, and keep the point in the answer directly above the point in the number. Add zeros on the end and carry on until it stops or you have enough places. 7.2 ÷ 5: bring down as far as needed — 1.44.

**By a decimal:** move both points the same number of places until the *divider* is a whole number, then divide. 4.8 ÷ 0.6 becomes 48 ÷ 6 = 8. This works because you have multiplied both numbers by ten, and a division is a fraction — multiply top and bottom by the same thing and it is unchanged.

## Rounding

- To the nearest whole number, tenth, hundredth: look at the **next digit only**. 5 or more rounds up, 4 or less stays.
- 3.47 to one decimal place is 3.5. 3.44 is 3.4. 2.5 to the nearest whole is 3.
- To a number of **significant figures**: start counting from the first non-zero digit. 0.00427 to two significant figures is 0.0043.

**Round at the end, not in the middle.** Rounding early and then calculating is how a roof comes out short.

And know what rounding is *for*: a measurement is never exact, so carrying eight digits from a tape measure that reads to the nearest centimetre is pretending to a precision you do not have.

## Percentages

*Per cent* means *per hundred*. 40% is 40 out of every 100, which is 40/100, which is 0.4. Those three are one thing wearing three hats, and the child should be able to swap hats instantly.

**To find a percentage of an amount**, build it from the easy ones and add:

- **10%** — divide by 10.
- **1%** — divide by 100.
- **50%** — halve. **25%** — halve twice. **5%** — half of 10%.

So 35% of 240: 10% is 24, so 30% is 72; 5% is 12; total **84**. No calculator, no formula, and it works in a market or a shop.

**As a decimal**, for bigger jobs: 35% of 240 = 0.35 × 240 = 84. Same answer, and this is the method that scales.

**Increase and decrease.** A 20% rise means you end with 120% of what you had, so multiply by 1.2. A 20% fall means 80%, so multiply by 0.8. One step, not two.

**Careful:** a 20% rise followed by a 20% fall does not bring you back. 100 → 120 → 96. The second percentage is of a different amount. This trips up adults as much as children, and it is why "50% off, then 20% more off" is not 70% off.

**One amount as a percentage of another:** divide, then multiply by 100. 18 out of 25 = 18 ÷ 25 = 0.72 = 72%.

## Where this is actually used

Interest on a debt; tax; a discount; the strength of a disinfectant or a feed mix; the proof of a spirit; a battery at 40%; germination rate on a seed packet (85% means 85 of 100 come up, so sow accordingly); the loss from a harvest; a slope expressed as a gradient; a doctor's dose per kilogram of body weight. Set problems from these and not from imaginary shops — the child is learning the arithmetic of running a household that has to account for itself.

## Exercises

1. Write in order, smallest first: 0.7, 0.25, 0.09, 0.5, 0.125, 0.4.
2. Write as decimals: ⅖, ⅜, 7/10, 3/100, ⅞.
3. Write as fractions in their simplest form: 0.8, 0.35, 0.04, 0.125.
4. 14.6 + 3.85; 20 − 7.4; 6.35 + 0.9 + 12; 100 − 0.75.
5. 0.078 × 1000; 45.2 ÷ 100; 3.4 × 10 ÷ 1000.
6. Estimate first, then work out: 3.2 × 4.5; 0.6 × 0.4; 12 × 0.25; 7.5 ÷ 0.5.
7. 9.6 ÷ 4; 2.4 ÷ 0.8; 15 ÷ 0.25.
8. Round 47.382 to: the nearest whole number, one decimal place, two decimal places, two significant figures.
9. Convert: 2.5 m to cm; 340 g to kg; 1.75 km to m; 65 mm to cm.
10. Find, in your head, building from 10% and 1%: 10% of 350; 5% of 80; 15% of 60; 35% of 240; 2% of 450.
11. A sack of seed is 25 kg. The packet says 85% germination. About how many of 200 sown seeds should come up?
12. A tool costs £48 and is reduced by 25%. What do you pay? The next week the reduced price rises by 25%. What is it now, and why is it not £48?
13. Out of 40 hens, 34 are laying. What percentage is that? What percentage is not?
14. A water butt holds 210 litres and is 30% full. How many litres is that, and how many more will fill it?
15. You borrow 200 units of grain and must return it with 12% added. How much do you return?
16. A field yielded 1,250 kg last year and 1,400 kg this year. What was the percentage increase, to the nearest whole per cent?

4. 18.45; 12.6; 19.25; 99.25.
5. 78; 0.452; 0.034.
6. About 12 → 14.4; about 0.24 (0.6 × 0.4 = 0.24 — note it is smaller than both); 3; 15.
7. 2.4; 3; 60.
8. 47; 47.4; 47.38; 47.
9. 250 cm; 0.34 kg; 1,750 m; 6.5 cm.
10. 35; 4; 9; 84; 9.
11. About 170.

## Going further

*Fractions, ratio and measuring* is the same ground in the other notation — move between them until it is one topic, not two. *Area, perimeter, angles and shape* uses decimals in every measurement. *Negative numbers and the start of algebra* at KS3 turns "multiply by 1.2" into a formula you can use on anything. For drill, the multiplication tables still underpin all of it: a child who is shaky on 7 × 8 will be shaky on 0.7 × 0.8.
