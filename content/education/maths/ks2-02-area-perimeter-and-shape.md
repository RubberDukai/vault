# Area, perimeter, angles and shape

**What they need first:** times tables; *Decimals and percentages* for the measurements; a ruler, string, pegs and somewhere outside to work.

This is the most practical maths in the vault. Everything here is used the day you set out a garden bed, order timber, thatch a roof, size a tank or check that a wall is square. Teach it outside with a tape measure wherever you can, and on paper only to record what was found.

## Perimeter and area are different questions

- **Perimeter** is the distance round the edge. It is a length, measured in m. *How much fence?*
- **Area** is the amount of surface inside. It is measured in m², and m² are squares, not metres. *How much turf, paint, seed?*

Two shapes can have the same perimeter and very different areas — a 1 m × 9 m strip and a 5 m × 5 m square both have a 20 m perimeter, but the strip is 9 m² and the square is 25 m². Show this with a loop of string and pegs before any formula: **for a given length of fence, the squarer the plot, the more land you enclose.** That single fact is worth a term of arithmetic to somebody fencing a garden.

## Area, built not memorised

Draw everything on squared paper and count squares first. The formulae should arrive as shortcuts for counting, and every one of them can be shown.

| Shape | Area | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Rectangle** | length × width | rows of squares, so many in each row |
| **Triangle** | ½ × base × height | it is half of the rectangle round it — cut one out and see |
| **Parallelogram** | base × height | slice the triangle off one end, slide it to the other: a rectangle |
| **Trapezium** | ½ × (a + b) × height | two of them make a parallelogram |
| **Circle** | π × r × r | see below |
| **Any awkward shape** | cut it into rectangles and triangles | and add them up |

**The height is the perpendicular height** — straight up from the base, not the length of a sloping side. This is the single most common mistake, so draw the little right-angle square on the height every time.

**Compound shapes.** An L-shaped room is two rectangles. Cut it, work out each, add. Or box it in and subtract the missing corner. Both work; let the child choose and check one against the other.

## The circle

- **Diameter** all the way across through the middle; **radius** half of that, centre to edge.
- **Circumference** is the distance round: **C = π × d**, and π is a little over 3 — use 3.14, or 22/7 for mental work.
- **Area** is **π × r²**, which is *r squared*, not 2 × r. A great many wrong answers come from that confusion alone.

**Find π yourself.** Wrap string round a bucket, a plate, a tree; measure the string and the diameter; divide. Do it for five objects of different sizes and write the results in a column. They all come to about 3.14, and the child has discovered something that is true everywhere in the universe. That lesson is worth more than the formula.

## Volume and capacity

- **Volume** of a box = length × width × height, in m³ or cm³.
- **1 litre = 1000 cm³**, and **1 m³ = 1000 litres**. That pair of facts sizes any tank, trough or cistern.
- **Volume of a cylinder** = area of the circle × height = π × r² × h. That is your water butt, your drum, your silo.
- **1 litre of water weighs 1 kg** — so a full 210-litre butt is 210 kg standing on whatever you put it on. Worth knowing before you build the stand.

## Angles

An angle is an amount of **turn**, not a distance. Stand the child up and turn them: a quarter turn is 90°, half a turn 180°, all the way round 360°.

| Name | Size |
|---|---|
| Acute | less than 90° |
| Right | exactly 90° |
| Obtuse | between 90° and 180° |
| Straight | 180° |
| Reflex | more than 180° |

The facts to know cold:

- Angles on a **straight line** add to 180°.
- Angles round a **point** add to 360°.
- Angles in a **triangle** add to 180°. *Prove it:* tear the three corners off a paper triangle and put them together — they make a straight line, every time.
- Angles in a **quadrilateral** add to 360°. (It is two triangles.)
- **Vertically opposite** angles, where two lines cross, are equal.

**Measuring without a protractor.** A protractor is a thing you may not have. A square corner can be got from any sheet of paper folded twice; fold that corner in half for 45°, in half again for 22.5°. Better still, use the triangle below.

## The 3-4-5 triangle: how to set out a building

If a triangle's sides are 3, 4 and 5 — in any unit, any multiple — the angle between the 3 and the 4 is **exactly a right angle**. This is the practical face of Pythagoras, and it is how corners have been set out for four thousand years.

To square a corner for a shed, a bed or a foundation:

1. Peg the first line and mark a point 3 m along it from the corner.
2. From the corner, swing a 4 m line roughly at right angles.
3. Adjust the 4 m line until the distance between the two marks is exactly 5 m.
4. That corner is now true. Use 6-8-10 or 9-12-15 for a bigger job — more accurate over distance.

**Check a rectangle by its diagonals.** Measure corner to opposite corner both ways. If the two diagonals are equal, the shape is a true rectangle. One piece of string and no maths at all — teach it, and have the child check a door, a gate and a table.

## Shapes and their properties

- **Triangles:** equilateral (three equal sides, three 60° angles), isosceles (two equal), scalene (none equal), right-angled.
- **Quadrilaterals:** square, rectangle, rhombus (a pushed-over square), parallelogram, trapezium (one pair of parallel sides), kite.
- **Regular polygons:** pentagon 5, hexagon 6, heptagon 7, octagon 8. A hexagon tiles a floor with no gaps, which is why bees use it and why paving does.
- **Solids:** cube, cuboid, cylinder, cone, sphere, triangular prism, pyramid. Count faces, edges and vertices; build them from folded card nets.

**Why a triangle is the shape of every roof, gate and pylon:** it is the only shape that cannot be pushed out of square without breaking a side. Make a square and a triangle from strips and split pins and let the child push both. That is the whole of structural bracing in one minute, and it explains the diagonal on a field gate.

## Symmetry, co-ordinates and scale

- **Line symmetry:** where a mirror could stand. Fold paper to find it. A square has four lines, a rectangle two, a circle infinitely many.
- **Rotational symmetry:** how many times it looks the same in one full turn.
- **Co-ordinates:** (across, up) — along the corridor, then up the stairs. Always in that order. Plot shapes, then translate and reflect them. This is the same reading as a grid reference in *Reading a map*.
- **Scale:** a plan at 1:50 means 1 cm on paper is 50 cm in the world. Have them draw a room to scale and then a whole dwelling — this is where maths meets the Handbook's building chapters.

## Exercises

Work as many as possible with a real tape measure outside.

1. A garden bed is 4.5 m by 2 m. Find its perimeter and its area.
2. You have 24 m of fencing. Give the dimensions and areas of three different rectangles you could enclose. Which encloses the most?
3. A triangle has a base of 6 m and a perpendicular height of 4 m. What is its area?
4. An L-shaped room: 6 m × 4 m with a 2 m × 2 m corner missing. What is its floor area? How many 30 cm × 30 cm tiles would cover it?
5. A circular pen has a radius of 3 m. What is its circumference and its area? (Use π = 3.14.)
6. A water butt is 0.5 m across and 1 m tall. How many litres does it hold, and what does it weigh full?
7. Angles on a straight line: one is 115°. What is the other?
8. A triangle has angles of 40° and 75°. What is the third?
9. A quadrilateral has angles 90°, 90° and 115°. What is the fourth?
10. You are setting out a shed base. Describe, step by step, how to make the first corner a true right angle using only a tape measure and pegs.
11. Two people measure a rectangle's diagonals as 5.20 m and 5.35 m. What does that tell them, and what should they do?
12. How many faces, edges and vertices has a cuboid? A triangular prism?
13. How many lines of symmetry has: an equilateral triangle, a rectangle, a regular hexagon, the letter H?
14. Plot (1,1), (5,1), (5,4) and (1,4). What shape is it? What is its area in squares? Now reflect it in the line x = 5 and give the new co-ordinates.
15. A plan is drawn at 1:50. A wall measures 8 cm on the plan. How long is the real wall? A door is 0.9 m wide — how wide on the plan?
16. A roof is a triangle 8 m across the bottom and 3 m to the ridge. What is the area of that triangular end? If you must clad both ends, how much material do you need, plus 10% for waste?

4. 24 − 4 = 20 m². A tile is 0.09 m², so 20 ÷ 0.09 ≈ 223 tiles — order about 240 to allow for cuts and breakage.
5. Circumference = 3.14 × 6 = 18.84 m; area = 3.14 × 9 = 28.26 m².
6. r = 0.25 m, so area = 3.14 × 0.0625 = 0.196 m²; × 1 m = 0.196 m³ = about **196 litres**, weighing about **196 kg**.
7. 65°.
8. 65°.
9. 65°.
10. Peg the corner and run a line; mark 3 m along it. From the corner run a second line roughly square and swing it until the mark 4 m along it is exactly 5 m from the 3 m mark. Larger multiples (6-8-10) are more accurate.
