# Ratio, proportion and rates

**What they need first:** *Fractions, ratio and measuring*; *Decimals and percentages*; *Equations and formulae*.

This is the maths of running things. Mixing mortar, sizing a dose, feeding animals, working out whether the big sack is really better value, knowing how a debt grows and how long the fuel will last — all of it is one idea handled from several directions: **how one quantity changes when another does.**

## Ratio

A ratio compares parts. Mortar at 4:1 means four of sand to one of cement.

**Simplify** like a fraction: 20:15 = 4:3.

**Sharing in a ratio** — the method that never fails: add the parts, divide, multiply back.
Share 350 kg in the ratio 3:4. Parts: 3 + 4 = 7. One part: 350 ÷ 7 = 50. So **150 kg and 200 kg**. Check they add back to 350 — always check.

**Ratio and fraction are not the same.** In 3:4, the first share is 3/7 of the whole, not 3/4. This confusion costs more marks and more mortar than any other on the page.

**Unitary form.** 5:2 is the same as 2.5:1, which is often the most useful way to say it — *two and a half of sand to one of cement* is something you can do with one bucket.

## Direct and inverse proportion

**Direct:** double one, double the other. Twice the wall needs twice the mortar; three times the people need three times the food. Plotted, it is a straight line through the origin.

**Inverse:** double one, halve the other. Twice as many people build the wall in half the time; twice the speed takes half the time. Plotted, it is a curve that never reaches either axis.

**Work out which before calculating.** Ask: *if I make this bigger, does the other get bigger or smaller?* Getting that backwards is the only real mistake available here, and it is a mistake of sense, not of arithmetic.

**The unit method solves nearly all of it.** Find the value of one, then multiply.

> Six hens eat 1.8 kg of feed a day. How much for ten?
> One hen: 1.8 ÷ 6 = 0.3 kg. Ten hens: 0.3 × 10 = **3 kg**.

> Four people build a wall in 6 days. How long for three?
> Total work: 4 × 6 = 24 person-days. Three people: 24 ÷ 3 = **8 days**.

Notice the two are handled differently — that is the direct/inverse distinction doing real work. And notice the second is an idealisation: three people are not always ¾ of four, and the child should be asked where the model stops being true.

## Rates

A rate is one quantity per another, and the word **per** is the signal.

| Rate | Formula | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Speed** | distance ÷ time | km/h, m/s |
| **Density** | mass ÷ volume | kg/m³ — will it float, what will it weigh |
| **Yield** | crop ÷ area | kg/m², planning what to plant |
| **Rate of use** | amount ÷ time | litres of fuel a week, kg of grain a month |
| **Dose** | amount ÷ body mass | mg/kg — the one that must never be wrong |
| **Unit price** | cost ÷ amount | per kg, per litre — for comparing |

**Rearrange rather than remember.** From s = d ÷ t you get d = s × t and t = d ÷ s. One formula, three questions — see *Equations and formulae*.

**Units are half the answer.** Write them at every step and they will catch your errors for you: if you are working out a weight and the units come out in metres, you have gone wrong somewhere and the units have told you so. Insist on this; it is the single most valuable habit in applied arithmetic.

**Converting a rate:** 5 m/s into km/h — 5 metres becomes 0.005 km, one second becomes 1/3600 hour, so 0.005 × 3600 = **18 km/h**. Slowly, in steps, writing the units.

## Best value

Reduce both to the same unit and compare. A 2.5 kg bag at £3.20 is £1.28/kg; a 4 kg bag at £4.80 is £1.20/kg. The big bag is better **per kilogram** — but only if you will use it all before it spoils, and only if you can afford the larger sum today. Teach the arithmetic and then teach the two questions that go with it, because value is not the same as price.

## Compound change, and why debt grows

A percentage applied again and again to a growing amount is **compound** change, and it behaves in a way that surprises almost everybody.

Multiply by (1 + rate) each time. 200 kg of stored grain losing 5% a month: × 0.95 each month, so after 6 months 200 × 0.95⁶ = **147 kg**, not 200 − 60 = 140. Each month's 5% is of a smaller amount.

The other direction is the one that matters most in life: a debt of 100 at 10% a year owes 110, then 121, then 133, then 146 — and doubles in about seven years untouched.

**The rule of 72:** divide 72 by the percentage rate and you get roughly the number of years to double. At 10%, about 7 years; at 6%, about 12. It is arithmetic anybody can do in their head, and it is the most useful single thing a young person can know about borrowing money.

The same rule runs forwards: a population, a herd or a stock of seed growing at a steady percentage doubles on the same schedule. Growth and debt are the same mathematics wearing different clothes.

## Scale

A scale is a ratio between the drawing and the world.

- **1:50** — 1 cm on the paper is 50 cm real. **1:25,000** — 1 cm is 250 m, which is the Ordnance Survey Explorer map in the Maps tab.
- **On paper to real:** multiply. **Real to paper:** divide.
- **Area does not scale the same way.** Double the lengths and the area goes up **four** times; treble them and it is **nine** times. Volume goes up by the cube — eight times, twenty-seven times. This catches everybody: a tank twice as long, wide and deep holds eight times as much, not twice, and a pot twice the size needs eight times the filling and takes far longer to heat through.

Have the child draw a real room to 1:50 and then a plan of the whole dwelling. It is the point where *Area, perimeter, angles and shape*, *Reading a map* and this lesson meet.

## Exercises

1. Simplify: 24:36; 50:75; 8:12:20.
2. Share 480 in the ratio 5:3. Share 1,260 kg in the ratio 2:3:4. Check each by adding back.
3. Mortar is 4:1 sand to cement. You have 30 buckets of sand. How much cement? You have only 5 of cement — how much sand can you use, and how much mortar is that altogether?
4. A recipe for 4 people uses 600 g of flour and 350 ml of milk. Rewrite it for 6 and for 15.
5. In the ratio 3:5, what fraction of the whole is the first share? Explain why it is not 3/5.
6. Direct or inverse? Say which, then solve. (a) Five hens eat 1.5 kg a day; how much for eight? (b) Six people dig a ditch in 4 days; how long for eight people? (c) A car uses 6 litres per 100 km; how much for 250 km? (d) A tank empties in 12 hours through one tap; how long through three?
7. Convert: 72 km/h to m/s; 5 m/s to km/h; 1.5 litres per minute to litres per hour.
8. A block 0.5 m × 0.3 m × 0.2 m weighs 66 kg. What is its density? Will it float? (Water is 1,000 kg/m³.)
9. A bed of 12 m² yielded 42 kg of potatoes. What is the yield per m²? How big a bed is needed for 200 kg?
10. Medicine is dosed at 15 mg per kg of body weight. What dose for a 24 kg child? For a 70 kg adult? Why must the units be written at every step here above all?
11. Which is better value: 3 kg for £4.50, or 5 kg for £7.00? Give two reasons the better-value option might still be the wrong buy.
12. 500 kg of grain loses 4% a month. How much is left after 3 months? After a year? Why is it not 500 − (12 × 20)?
13. A debt of 250 grows at 8% a year and nothing is paid. What is owed after 1, 2 and 3 years? Using the rule of 72, roughly when does it double?
14. A wall is 8 cm long on a plan at 1:50. How long is it really? A door 0.8 m wide — how wide on the plan?
15. A tank 1 m × 1 m × 1 m holds 1,000 litres. How much does one twice as long, wide and deep hold? Explain why the answer is not 2,000.
16. Your household uses 35 litres of fuel a month. You have 400 litres. How long will it last? If you cut use by 20%, how long then?

## Answers

1. 2:3; 2:3; 2:3:5.
2. 300 and 180 (8 parts of 60). 280, 420, 560 (9 parts of 140).
3. 7.5 buckets of cement. With 5 of cement you can use 20 of sand, making 25 buckets of mortar.
4. For 6: 900 g and 525 ml. For 15: 2,250 g and 1,312.5 ml.
5. 3/8. The ratio names the parts, not the whole — there are 3 + 5 = 8 parts altogether, and the first share is three of them.
6. (a) Direct — 2.4 kg. (b) Inverse — 24 person-days ÷ 8 = 3 days. (c) Direct — 15 litres. (d) Inverse — 4 hours.
7. 20 m/s; 18 km/h; 90 litres per hour.
8. Volume 0.03 m³; density 66 ÷ 0.03 = 2,200 kg/m³. It sinks, being more than twice as dense as water.
9. 3.5 kg/m². For 200 kg you need 200 ÷ 3.5 ≈ **57 m²**.
10. 360 mg; 1,050 mg (1.05 g). Because a dosing error is measured in lives, and a slip between mg and g, or between the weight of the child and the weight of the dose, is exactly the kind of mistake that writing units at every step prevents.
11. £1.50/kg against £1.40/kg — the 5 kg is better per kilogram. It might still be wrong if it will spoil before you use it, or if you cannot spare the larger sum now, or if you have nowhere dry to store it.
12. 500 × 0.96³ = **442.4 kg**. After a year, 500 × 0.96¹² = **307.7 kg**. It is not a flat 20 kg a month because each month's 4% is taken from a smaller amount than the month before.
13. 270; 291.60; 314.93. 72 ÷ 8 = about **9 years** to double.
14. 8 × 50 = 400 cm = **4 m**. The door: 80 ÷ 50 = **1.6 cm**.
15. 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 m³ = **8,000 litres**. Volume grows with the cube of the lengths: each of the three dimensions doubled, so the capacity went up eight times.
16. 400 ÷ 35 ≈ **11.4 months**. Cutting use by 20% gives 28 litres a month, so 400 ÷ 28 ≈ **14.3 months** — nearly three months more for a fifth less use.
