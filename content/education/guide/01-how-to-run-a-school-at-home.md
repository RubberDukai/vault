# How to run a school at home

Children learned to read, count and reason at home for most of human history, from adults who had never been trained. What those adults had was time, attention, patience, and a rough idea of what came next. This vault gives you the last part. The rest is you.

## The daily rhythm

**Little and often, every day, at the same time.** Twenty minutes for a five-year-old; forty for an eight-year-old; an hour to ninety minutes for a twelve-year-old, split into two sittings. Mornings, before the day's work, when minds are fresh. A short session done daily beats a long one done weekly by a mile.

A workable morning for a seven-year-old:
1. **Ten minutes of reading** aloud to you (their book), then five minutes of you reading to them (a harder book).
2. **Fifteen minutes of maths** — five minutes of quick-fire practice on what they already know (tables, number bonds), ten on the new thing.
3. **Ten minutes of writing** — a sentence or two about yesterday, copied or composed.
4. **One other thing** — science, the map, history, a song, a poem — twenty minutes, different each day.

Then let them go. The afternoon is for the garden, the animals, the workshop and the kitchen, which are also school.

## How children actually learn

- **New things stick to old things.** Every lesson here starts from what the child already knows. If the start is not solid, go back; there is no shame in it and no hurry.
- **Doing beats watching.** Count real beans. Measure the real table. Weigh the real flour. The abstraction (the number on paper) comes *after* the hands have done it, not before.
- **Practice makes permanent.** A skill needs to be used, spaced out, dozens of times before it is owned. The five-minute warm-up each morning is the most important part of the day.
- **Mistakes are the lesson.** A wrong answer tells you exactly what to teach next. Never punish one; ask "how did you get that?" and listen.
- **Short and finished beats long and abandoned.** Stop while it is going well.
- **They learn from you.** If you read, they read. If you measure twice before cutting, so will they.

## How to use these lessons

Each subject is laid out in **key stages**, the same ones English schools use:

| Stage | Ages | School years | In a sentence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Key Stage 1** | 5–7 | Years 1–2 | Read, write, count to 100, add and subtract, tell the time, name the world |
| **Key Stage 2** | 7–11 | Years 3–6 | Fluent reading and writing, the four operations, fractions and decimals, real science, history and geography with dates and maps |
| **Key Stage 3** | 11–14 | Years 7–9 | Algebra and geometry, essays and argument, physics, chemistry and biology as subjects, the world's history |

Within a stage, take the lessons **in order**; each assumes the one before. Each lesson has:
- **What they need first** — check it before starting.
- **The teaching** — written to the adult, with the words to say and the things to do.
- **Exercises** — for the child, on paper.
- **Answers** — folded away at the bottom, for you. Mark together; let them find their own errors first.
- **Going further** — for the child who wants more, and links to the encyclopedia and the textbooks on the Library shelf for depth.

**Mark lessons complete** (the box at the bottom) per child — switch profile in the sidebar. The School page shows each child's progress.

## What "ready for the next stage" looks like

Do not move on because of age. Move on because of this:

**Ready to leave KS1 (about 7):** reads a simple book alone and can tell you what happened; writes a few sentences with capital letters and full stops that another person can read; counts to 100 and back, knows the number bonds to 20 without counting on fingers, adds and subtracts two-digit numbers on paper, knows the 2, 5 and 10 times tables, tells the time to the quarter hour, names and describes common shapes, measures with a ruler and reads a scale; knows the days, months and seasons, the parts of a plant and the body, what living things need, and the difference between a map and a picture.

**Ready to leave KS2 (about 11):** reads anything and reads for pleasure; writes a page — a story, a letter, an explanation — with paragraphs, punctuation and spelling mostly right; all tables to 12 instantly, long multiplication and division, fractions, decimals and percentages and how they relate, area and perimeter, angles, negative numbers, reads and draws a graph; can plan a fair test and explain what the evidence showed; knows the main events of British and world history in order and can find any country on a map; can cook a meal, keep a diary and give a short talk.

**Ready to leave KS3 (about 14):** reads adult books and argues about them; writes a structured essay and a clear report; solves equations, uses formulae, does geometry with proof, handles ratio and proportion and probability; knows the physics of forces, energy and electricity, the chemistry of atoms and reactions, and the biology of cells, bodies and ecosystems well enough to apply them; knows how the world came to be as it is; can teach a younger child.

## Checking progress without exams

Once a month, take a lesson from a few weeks back and set three of its exercises cold. If they are done easily, it is owned. If not, that is the next week's warm-up. Keep a page per child in the Notebook: the date, what was covered, what was easy, what was not. Reading age is the single best measure of everything else: keep a list of books read, in order of difficulty.

## When it goes wrong

- **They won't sit still.** The session is too long. Halve it. Go outside and count fence posts instead.
- **They are stuck and upset.** Stop. Go back two lessons, do something they can do, and end on that. Come back tomorrow from one step earlier.
- **You do not understand it yourself.** Learn it the night before from the lesson and the textbook on the Library shelf; say "let's work it out together" — it is a good lesson in itself.
- **They are ahead of you.** Wonderful. The OpenStax textbooks, Wikibooks and Wikiversity on the Library shelf go to university level. Your job becomes finding the next book and asking what they learned.
- **More than one child.** Teach the youngest the new thing while the older one does practice; then swap. Older children teaching younger ones learn twice.

## The road map

The full sequence, subject by subject, as the lessons stand and as they are meant to grow. A lesson in *italics* is not written yet — the Library textbooks cover the ground until it is.

**Mathematics.** KS1: counting and place value → number bonds and adding/subtracting to 20 → adding and subtracting to 100 → times tables 2, 5, 10 and sharing → shapes, measures, time and money → halves and quarters. KS2: arithmetic by hand → fractions, ratio and measuring → *decimals and percentages* → *area, perimeter, angles and geometry* → *graphs and data*. KS3: negative numbers and algebra → chance and data → *equations and formulae* → *geometry with proof* → *ratio, proportion and rates*.

**English.** KS1: learning to read → handwriting and spelling → sentences and stories. KS2: writing that lasts → *grammar and punctuation* → *reading for meaning: the classics on the shelf* → *poetry*. KS3: thinking clearly → *the essay* → *the report and the letter* → *speaking and debate*.

**Science.** KS1: living things and their needs → materials → seasons, weather and the sky. KS2: how to find things out → living things → earth and weather → *forces, light and sound* → *the human body*. KS3: energy, heat and electricity → *matter and chemical reactions* → *cells, genes and evolution* → *the Earth in space*.

**The world (history and geography).** KS2: reading a map → *Britain from the Stone Age to today* → *the ancient world* → *the continents and the climate*. KS3: *the modern world and how it was made* → *how societies are run*.

**Practical.** Woven through everything: the garden (see *Starting a food garden*), the kitchen (*Artisan foods*), the workshop (*Repair & Making*), first aid (*Medicine*), the map (*Maps*), music (*Music*) and the languages (*Languages*). A child who can grow, cook, mend, navigate, bandage and sing has an education, whatever their tables are like.
