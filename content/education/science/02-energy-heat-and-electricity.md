# Energy, heat and electricity

## Energy cannot be made or destroyed

It only changes form. This is the most reliable law in physics and it is a practical tool, not an abstraction: when you cannot find where the energy went, you have not looked hard enough.

Burning wood: chemical energy → heat and light.
A solar panel: light → electrical energy.
A person digging: chemical energy from food → movement and a great deal of heat.
A waterwheel: gravitational energy → movement.

**Follow the energy** and you understand any system. When a machine gets hot, that heat is energy that was supposed to be doing something else. That is why an inefficient device is a warm one.

## Heat only flows one way

Heat moves from hotter to colder, always, on its own. Making it go the other way — a fridge, a heat pump — takes work.

Three ways it travels, and they map exactly onto staying warm:

- **Conduction** — through touching materials. Metal conducts fast, air very slowly. This is why insulation is trapped air, and why a metal spoon in soup burns your fingers.
- **Convection** — hot fluid rises and carries heat with it. This is why a room is warmer at the ceiling, why a chimney draws, and why a rocket stove works.
- **Radiation** — infrared travelling through space, needing no medium. This is how the sun heats the earth and how a fire warms your face across a gap.

**Temperature is not heat.** Temperature is how hot something is; heat is how much energy it holds. A spark is at a very high temperature and carries almost no heat, which is why it does not hurt. A bath at 40 °C holds enormously more energy than that spark.

**Specific heat capacity** is why water is so useful. It takes a great deal of energy to warm water, and it gives that energy back slowly. That makes water the best cheap thermal store there is — a barrel of water near a stove releases heat all night.

**Latent heat** is the hidden one. Turning water into steam takes about five times more energy than heating it from freezing to boiling. That is why evaporation cools so powerfully — and why sweating works, why wet clothes are so dangerous in the cold, and why steam burns far worse than boiling water.

## Electricity: three quantities

Everything electrical follows from three numbers and one equation.

- **Voltage (V), in volts** — the push. Think of it as pressure in a pipe.
- **Current (I), in amps** — the flow. How much is moving.
- **Resistance (R), in ohms** — how much the path fights the flow.

**Ohm's law: V = I × R**

Rearranged: I = V ÷ R, and R = V ÷ I.

**Power (P), in watts, is V × I.** Watts is the rate energy is used. **Watt-hours** is the amount — a 10 W light for 5 hours uses 50 Wh.

A worked example. A 12 V battery running a 24 W lamp:
current = P ÷ V = 24 ÷ 12 = **2 amps**.
The lamp's resistance = V ÷ I = 12 ÷ 2 = **6 ohms**.

## Why this matters in practice

**Low voltage means high current for the same power.** 400 W at 12 V is 33 A; at 240 V it is 1.7 A. Current is what heats wires, so low-voltage systems need much thicker cable. This is the single most common and most dangerous mistake in home-built off-grid systems.

**Series and parallel:**

- **Series** (end to end) adds voltages. Four 1.5 V cells in series give 6 V.
- **Parallel** (side by side) keeps the voltage and adds the capacity and available current.
- Christmas-light logic: in series, one break stops everything. In parallel, the others carry on.

**Short circuits.** If resistance drops near zero, current becomes enormous. A car battery across a spanner will melt the spanner and set fire to whatever is nearby. This is why every positive line gets a fuse, sized to blow before the wire heats up.

## Experiments

1. **Make a battery.** A copper coin and a galvanised nail in a lemon, or in salty water. Measure it with a multimeter — around 0.5–1 V. Put several in series and light an LED. Electricity from chemistry, in your hand.
2. **Build a circuit.** Battery, wire, small bulb. Add a second bulb in series (both dimmer), then in parallel (both bright). Measure and record.
3. **Verify Ohm's law.** With a variable resistor and a multimeter, measure current at several voltages, plot V against I. The straight line is the law.
4. **Electromagnet.** Wind wire round a nail, connect a battery, pick up paperclips. Count the turns, count the paperclips. More turns is more magnetism — and this is the principle behind every motor and generator ever built.
5. **Generator.** Spin a magnet near a coil and measure the voltage. A motor spun by hand *is* a generator. This is how essentially all the world's electricity is made — including nuclear, which is an elaborate way of boiling water to spin one of these.
6. **Heat loss.** Identical jars of hot water, different coverings, temperature every ten minutes, plotted on a graph. Then apply the result to your actual house.

## Exercises

1. A 5 W radio runs on 12 V. What current does it draw? What is its resistance?
2. Your loads total 380 Wh a day. Your battery is 12 V, 100 Ah. How many watt-hours does it hold, and how many days of autonomy is that at 90% usable?
3. Why does a 2 kW electric heater make off-grid living so difficult? Work out the watt-hours for three hours of use and compare it to the 380 Wh above.
4. Three 1.5 V cells in series: what voltage? In parallel?
5. Explain, using the three modes of heat transfer, why a vacuum flask works.
6. A rocket stove burns clean and an open fire smokes. In terms of energy, what is the smoke?

## Answers

1. I = P ÷ V = 5 ÷ 12 = **0.42 A**. R = V ÷ I = 12 ÷ 0.42 = **about 29 Ω**.
2. 12 × 100 = **1,200 Wh**; 90% usable is 1,080 Wh; 1,080 ÷ 380 = **about 2.8 days** of autonomy.
3. 2,000 W × 3 h = **6,000 Wh** — nearly sixteen times the whole day's other use, and more than five times what the battery holds. Resistance heating is the one thing a small off-grid system cannot do; heat with fuel, and keep the electricity for light, radio and tools.
4. In series: 4.5 V (the voltages add, the capacity does not). In parallel: 1.5 V, with three times the capacity.
5. **Conduction** is stopped by the vacuum, which has no particles to pass heat along, and by the thin glass neck. **Convection** is stopped by the vacuum for the same reason — no air to circulate. **Radiation** is stopped by the silvered surfaces, which reflect heat back. The stopper stops the fourth route, which is the liquid itself evaporating away.
6. Unburnt fuel. Smoke is fuel that left up the chimney without releasing its energy — so a smoky fire is both wasting wood and coating the flue in tar. A clean flame means the fuel is burning fully, which is why a rocket stove gets more heat from less wood.
