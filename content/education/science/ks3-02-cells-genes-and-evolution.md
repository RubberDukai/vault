# Cells, genes and evolution

**What they need first:** *Living things* at KS2, and *Matter and chemical reactions* for the chemistry.

This is the lesson that explains why a seed saved from the best plant is worth saving, why a family of pigs kept too close together goes wrong, why antibiotics stop working, and why the world is full of creatures so well fitted to their lives that they look designed. It is the single most useful piece of biology anybody can own.

## The cell

Every living thing is made of cells: one, if you are a bacterium or an amoeba; something like thirty trillion if you are a person.

**In a plant and an animal cell alike:**

- **Cell membrane** — the skin, deciding what comes in and goes out.
- **Cytoplasm** — the jelly where the chemistry happens.
- **Nucleus** — the control room, holding the instructions.
- **Mitochondria** — where food and oxygen are turned into usable energy.

**In a plant cell as well:**

- **Cell wall** of cellulose — rigid, which is why plants stand up and wood is wood.
- **Chloroplasts** — green, where light is turned into food.
- **A large vacuole** of sap — full, it holds the plant firm; empty, the plant wilts, which is exactly what wilting is.

**Bacteria** are smaller and simpler, with no nucleus — the instructions float loose. **Viruses** are not cells at all and are not really alive on their own: a packet of instructions in a coat, which can do nothing until it gets inside one of your cells and makes it build more viruses. That difference is why antibiotics work on bacteria and do nothing whatever to a virus, and it is worth knowing before anybody is ill.

**Look at some.** A sliver of onion skin, a scraping of pond weed, a drop of pond water under any lens you can find — even a strong magnifying glass or a bead of water held in a wire loop will show plant cells in onion.

**Cells → tissues → organs → systems → organism.** Muscle cells make muscle tissue, which makes the heart, which is part of the circulatory system. Naming that ladder makes the body comprehensible.

## Growing and making more

**Cells divide.** One becomes two, each with a full copy of the instructions. That is how you grow from a single fertilised cell, how a cut heals, and how a bacterium becomes a million overnight. When the copying goes wrong and a cell divides without stopping, that is cancer.

**Two ways of making a new organism:**

| | Asexual | Sexual |
|---|---|---|
| Parents | one | two |
| Offspring | identical copies | a mixture of both |
| Speed | fast | slower |
| Variation | almost none | plenty |
| Examples | potato tubers, runners, cuttings, most bacteria | seed, eggs, most animals |

**Both are used on purpose in a garden.** Take a cutting or plant a tuber and you get exactly the plant you already have — useful when you have something good. Sow seed and you get variation — useful when you want something new, and a nuisance when you wanted the same again. A child who understands this stops being surprised when apple pips do not grow the apple they came from.

**Variation matters because conditions change.** A field of identical plants is a field that one disease can take entirely, which is what a monoculture is and why it is a risk.

## DNA and inheritance

Inside the nucleus, **chromosomes** are long molecules of **DNA**. A **gene** is a length of DNA carrying the instruction for one thing. DNA is a code written in four letters, and the same code is used by every living thing on Earth — bacteria, oak trees and people — which is itself powerful evidence that all of it is related.

**You get one set from each parent.** So you have two versions of most genes, and they need not agree. A **dominant** version shows when present at all; a **recessive** one shows only when both copies are the recessive kind. That is why two brown-eyed parents can have a blue-eyed child, and why a trait can skip a generation and reappear.

**Not everything is inherited.** Height, weight and health come from genes **and** food, work, illness and accident. A well-bred animal that is starved is a poor animal. Nature and nurture both, always — and asking "which one?" is usually the wrong question.

**Mutation** is a copying error. Most do nothing; some are harmful; just occasionally one is useful. Mutation is the only original source of new variation, and everything else merely shuffles what already exists.

**Inbreeding.** Close relatives carry the same recessive versions, so breeding them together brings the bad ones together too — weakness, deformity, poor fertility. This is not superstition; it is arithmetic, and it applies to your goats, your chickens and your family alike. **Bring in a new male from outside every few generations.** For a small population, keep a written breeding record — which is where *The report and the letter* earns its keep.

## Evolution by natural selection

Four observations, and the conclusion follows whether you like it or not:

1. More are born than can survive.
2. They vary, and some of that variation is inherited.
3. Some variations make survival and breeding more likely *in these conditions*.
4. So those variations become commoner in the next generation.

That is it. Nothing is trying to improve anything; there is no plan and no goal. **"Fittest" means best fitted to these conditions now** — not strongest — and when the conditions change, what was fittest may become a liability.

Given enough time and enough separation, populations diverge until they can no longer breed together, and that is a new species. The evidence is fossils in datable layers, the shared anatomy of a bat's wing and a human hand and a whale's flipper, the shared chemistry of DNA in everything alive, leftovers like the human appendix, and change we watch happening now.

**Watch it happen, in two places you cannot ignore:**

- **Antibiotic resistance.** A few bacteria in a population happen to survive the drug. They are the ones that breed. Finish the course and take antibiotics only when they are needed — otherwise you are running a breeding programme for the ones that survive you.
- **Pesticide resistance** and weeds that shrug off what killed them ten years ago. Same mechanism exactly.

**Artificial selection** is the same process with a human doing the choosing, and it is how every crop and domestic animal was made. Wild grass into wheat; wolf into every dog; a scrawny bitter cabbage ancestor into cabbage, kale, broccoli, sprouts and kohlrabi, all the same species. It is also the most practical thing in this lesson: **save seed from the best plants, breed from the best animals, keep a record, and in ten generations you have a strain fitted to your ground.** That is a real and permanent gain available to anyone with patience and a notebook.

## Ecology: living things in their place

- **Habitat** where it lives; **population** all of one kind there; **community** all the populations together; **ecosystem** the community plus the soil, water, air and weather.
- **Food chains** start with a producer (a plant, catching sunlight), then a herbivore, then a carnivore. Real ones are **webs**, not chains.
- **Energy is lost at every step** — most of it as heat — which is why there are far fewer wolves than rabbits, and why more people can be fed from an acre of grain than from an acre of meat.
- **Decomposers** — fungi, bacteria, worms — return everything to the soil. Without them, nothing would grow, because everything would still be locked up in what died.
- **Cycles.** Water, carbon and nitrogen go round and round. Nitrogen is the one to know for growing: legumes fix it from the air into the soil, which is why clover, beans and peas belong in a rotation.
- **Change one thing and the rest moves.** Remove a predator and the grazers boom and strip the ground. That is the argument for thinking twice before clearing anything.

## Exercises

1. Name four parts found in both plant and animal cells and three found only in plant cells, and say what each does.
2. Why do plants wilt, in terms of one specific part of the cell?
3. Why do antibiotics do nothing against a cold? Answer using the difference between a bacterium and a virus.
4. Look at onion skin under any lens you have. Draw what you see and label anything you recognise.
5. Put in order and give an example of each: organ, cell, system, tissue, organism.
6. Give two advantages and two disadvantages of asexual reproduction for a gardener.
7. Why does an apple pip not grow the apple it came from, and how do orchards get round it?
8. Two brown-eyed parents have a blue-eyed child. Explain how, using *dominant* and *recessive*.
9. A field is planted entirely with one variety. Give the advantage and the serious risk.
10. Why should you bring in a new ram, boar or cockerel from outside every few generations? Explain the mechanism, not just the rule.
11. Write out the four steps of natural selection in your own words.
12. Explain why "survival of the fittest" does not mean "the strongest survive".
13. Explain, step by step, how failing to finish a course of antibiotics helps breed resistant bacteria.
14. Cabbage, kale, broccoli and sprouts are all the same species. Explain how that is possible.
15. Design a ten-year seed-saving plan for one crop: what you would select for, how you would record it, and what you would expect after ten generations.
16. Draw a food web with at least six things in it for somewhere near you. Mark the producers and the decomposers. Then cross out one species and write what happens next.
17. Why can more people be fed from an acre of grain than from an acre of beef?

## Answers

1. Both: membrane (controls what enters and leaves), cytoplasm (where the chemistry happens), nucleus (holds the instructions), mitochondria (release energy). Plants only: cell wall (rigidity), chloroplasts (photosynthesis), large vacuole (holds the plant firm).
2. The vacuole loses water and shrinks, so the cells are no longer pressing against their walls and the plant loses its stiffness.
3. A cold is caused by a virus. Antibiotics work by attacking structures and processes that bacteria have and viruses do not — a virus is not a cell and has almost none of the machinery the drug targets.
4. No fixed answer; look for the brick-like grid of cells and, in good light, a dot of a nucleus in each.
5. Cell (muscle cell) → tissue (muscle) → organ (heart) → system (circulatory) → organism (person).
6. Advantages: you get exactly the plant you liked; it is faster; no pollination needed. Disadvantages: no variation, so a single disease can take the lot; no improvement over time; problems in the parent are copied too.
7. An apple grown from seed is a mixture of two parents and is almost never like either. Orchards graft cuttings of a known variety onto rootstocks, which is asexual reproduction and gives an identical tree.
8. Brown is dominant. Each parent carries one brown and one blue version; each shows brown. If the child receives the blue version from both, there is no brown copy to mask it, so the eyes are blue.
9. Advantage: everything is uniform — it ripens together, it is the same size, it suits one method of harvesting. Risk: every plant has the same weaknesses, so one disease suited to that variety can take the whole field.
10. In a closed population everybody gradually becomes related, so parents increasingly share the same recessive versions of genes. Two copies of a harmful recessive then come together far more often, producing weakness, deformity and poor fertility. New blood breaks that up.
11. More are born than survive; they vary, and some variation is inherited; some variations make survival and breeding likelier in those conditions; so those become commoner in the next generation.
12. Fittest means best fitted to the conditions of the moment — which may mean smaller, better camouflaged, better at digesting a poor food, or able to breed earlier. Strength is only occasionally what the conditions reward, and when conditions change the advantage can reverse.
13. The course is long enough to kill the toughest bacteria as well as the weakest. Stopping early kills the weak ones and leaves the toughest alive with no competition; those are the ones that breed, and the next infection is harder to treat.
14. They were all bred from one wild ancestor by humans selecting different parts — the leaves, the buds, the flower head, the stem — over many generations. Selecting hard for different features produced very different-looking plants that are still the same species.
15. Look for: a clear selection criterion (earliness, yield, flavour, disease resistance, standing up to wind), saving from several of the best plants rather than one (to keep variation), avoiding the very smallest population, a written record of which parent and which year, and a realistic expectation — a real but gradual shift, not a new crop.
16. Look for arrows going the right way (from eaten to eater), plants marked as producers, fungi and worms as decomposers, and a sensible knock-on when one is removed.
17. Energy is lost at every step of a food chain, most of it as heat. Eating the grain directly takes one step; feeding it to cattle and eating the cattle takes two, and most of the energy is gone by then.
