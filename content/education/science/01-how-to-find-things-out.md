# How to find things out

This is the most important lesson in this entire vault.

The encyclopedia here holds an enormous amount of what humanity knows. But it is a snapshot, it has gaps, and it will not contain the answer to *your* specific question about *your* soil, *your* water, *your* problem. For those, you need the method that produced the encyclopedia in the first place.

## The method

1. **Notice something and ask a precise question.** "Why are my beans dying?" is a start. "Do the beans in the shaded bed die more often than the beans in the sunny bed?" is a question you can actually answer.
2. **Guess an explanation.** A hypothesis is just a guess that is specific enough to be proved wrong.
3. **Predict what you would see if the guess were right** — and, crucially, what you would see if it were wrong.
4. **Test it.** Change one thing. Keep everything else the same.
5. **Record what happened.** Write it down at the time, not afterwards from memory.
6. **Decide what it means.** Was the guess wrong? Good — you have learned something real.
7. **Tell someone.** Knowledge that stays in one head dies with it.

## The part people skip: the control

If you want to know whether the ash you spread helped the cabbages, you must have cabbages **you did not spread ash on**, growing in the same place, at the same time, in the same weather.

Without that comparison you learn nothing, because you cannot tell what would have happened anyway. This is the difference between an experiment and a story, and it is the single idea that separates science from folklore.

**Change one thing at a time.** If you spread ash *and* water more *and* move them to a sunnier spot, you cannot know which change did it.

## Why our own judgement misleads us

Teach these explicitly. They are the reasons careful people reach wrong conclusions.

- **We remember hits and forget misses.** The remedy that "always works" is remembered on the days it worked.
- **Most things get better on their own.** Nearly any illness treated at its worst point appears to improve because of the treatment. This is why the control matters so much in medicine.
- **We find what we expect to find.** If you believe the ash worked, you will notice the healthy cabbages.
- **Correlation is not cause.** Two things happening together may share a cause, or be coincidence. More people drown in months when more ice cream is eaten; ice cream does not cause drowning.
- **Small numbers lie.** Three good results prove very little. Chance produces runs.

An honest experimenter looks hardest for evidence that they are wrong. That is the whole discipline, and it is a habit of character as much as of method.

## Experiments to actually do

**Which insulation is best?**
Fill identical jars with hot water, wrap each in a different material (wool, straw, newspaper, nothing), measure the temperature every ten minutes. Plot it. You now know something genuinely useful about keeping warm, and you have done real science.

**Does soaking seeds help them sprout?**
Two sets of ten seeds, one soaked overnight, one not. Same soil, same water, same light. Count how many sprout and how fast. Ten in each group is a small sample — repeat it.

**Which way does mould grow fastest?**
Slices of bread in different conditions: warm and damp, cold and damp, warm and dry. This teaches food preservation from the microbe's point of view.

**How much does a wet cloth cool things?**
Two thermometers, one wrapped in wet cloth in moving air. Evaporation carries away heat — this is why sweat works, why wet clothing is dangerous in the cold, and how a simple evaporative cooler keeps food fresh.

**Make a filter and test it.**
Layers of gravel, sand and charcoal in a bottle. Pour muddy water through. Compare clarity to unfiltered water. Then discuss the crucial point: it *looks* clean, but looking clean is not being safe. Link it to the water chapter in the handbook.

## Keeping a notebook

Give every child a notebook. In it goes: the date, the question, what was done, what was observed, and what they think it means — kept separate from what they observed.

This is how knowledge accumulates across seasons and across generations. Weather, planting dates, what yielded well, what failed, when the frost came, which repairs held. In a world without records, every generation starts again. A shelf of notebooks is how a family stops doing that.

## Exercises

1. Turn each into a testable question with a control: "Does compost help?" · "Is the river water safe?" · "Does this herb cure headaches?"
2. Someone says a remedy cured their cold in three days. What is wrong with that as evidence? Design a better test.
3. You notice plants near the compost heap grow taller. List three possible explanations besides the compost.
4. Design an experiment to find which of three firewood types burns longest. What must you keep the same?
5. Your experiment gives the opposite result to what you expected. What should you do, and what should you not do?
