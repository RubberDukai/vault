# The human body

**What they need first:** *Living things and what they need* at KS1, and *Living things* for cells and microbes.

A child who understands their own body takes better care of it, is far less frightened when something goes wrong, and can describe a symptom accurately — which, where there is no doctor, may be the most useful thing in this vault. Teach it on themselves: find the bones, take the pulse, time the breathing. Nothing here needs equipment.

**A note for the teacher.** This is the lesson where children ask about illness, blood, death and where babies come from. Answer plainly and without drama, at the level they asked; a straight answer costs nothing and a dodged one teaches them not to ask. The Handbook's medical chapters are the reference for anything real.

## The skeleton: frame, armour and levers

About **206 bones** in an adult. A baby has more — some fuse as it grows. Bone is living tissue: it has blood in it, it grows, it mends when broken, and it makes blood cells in its marrow.

Three jobs:

1. **Support** — the frame that holds the shape up.
2. **Protection** — skull round the brain, ribs round the heart and lungs, spine round the spinal cord.
3. **Movement** — bones are the levers the muscles pull on. (See *Forces, light and sound*: the forearm is a lever, and the elbow is its pivot.)

**Joints** are where bones meet: **hinge** at the elbow and knee (one direction, like a door), **ball and socket** at the shoulder and hip (all directions), **fixed** in the skull, and the small sliding ones in the spine and wrist. Have the child find each kind on themselves and name what it lets them do. **Ligaments** tie bone to bone; **cartilage** is the smooth, slippery padding at the ends.

## Muscles pull; they never push

Muscles are attached to bones by **tendons**, and a muscle can only **shorten and pull**. To move a bone back you need a second muscle on the other side: they work in **antagonistic pairs**. Bend your arm and feel the biceps harden and the triceps go soft; straighten it and feel the swap. That is the whole principle, and it can be felt in ten seconds.

Some muscles you choose to move (**voluntary** — arms, legs, face). Some work without being told (**involuntary** — gut, blood vessels). The **heart** is a muscle of its own kind that never rests.

## The heart and the blood

The heart is a pump about the size of your fist, sitting in the middle of the chest, tilted left. It has **four chambers** and beats about **60–100 times a minute** at rest — faster in a child, faster still when you run.

**The circuit**, in the order it happens: body → heart → **lungs** (to pick up oxygen and drop carbon dioxide) → heart → body. Two loops, one pump.

- **Arteries** carry blood *away* from the heart, at pressure, with thick walls. That pressure is what you feel as a **pulse**.
- **Veins** carry it back, at low pressure, with valves to stop it running backwards.
- **Capillaries** are so fine the blood passes almost cell by cell — this is where the exchange actually happens.

**Blood** carries oxygen (on red cells), food, warmth and waste; white cells fight infection; platelets clot a wound. That is four jobs, and knowing them makes bleeding, infection and shock make sense later.

**Take your pulse.** Two fingers — not the thumb, which has its own — on the wrist below the base of the thumb, or on the neck beside the windpipe. Count for 30 seconds and double it. Measure it at rest, after running for two minutes, and then every minute until it comes back down. Graph it (see *Graphs, tables and keeping records*). How quickly it returns is a measure of fitness, and the same measurement matters when somebody is ill.

## Breathing

Air goes in through the nose (which warms, wets and filters it), down the windpipe, and branches into two lungs like an upside-down tree. At the end of the smallest branches are millions of tiny air sacs, wrapped in capillaries, where **oxygen crosses into the blood and carbon dioxide crosses out**.

The **diaphragm** is the sheet of muscle underneath. Flatten it and the chest gets bigger, so air rushes in; let it dome up and air is pushed out. Breathing is not sucking — it is making space, and the air pressure outside does the rest.

At rest, about **12–20 breaths a minute** in an adult, more in a child. Count them on someone who does not know you are counting, or they will change it.

**Smoke, dust and smoke-filled rooms damage the lungs permanently.** That is a fact worth stating plainly in a household that burns wood indoors: ventilate, and keep the smoke out of the room.

## Digestion: what happens to food

**Mouth** — teeth cut and grind; saliva wets it and starts on the starch. → **Gullet** — muscles squeeze it down in waves (you could swallow upside down). → **Stomach** — acid and churning turn it to soup, and the acid kills most of what should not be there. → **Small intestine** — where the goodness is actually absorbed into the blood, through a lining folded into millions of tiny fingers to make the surface enormous. → **Large intestine** — takes the water back. → What is left leaves.

The **liver** processes what has been absorbed and deals with poisons; the **kidneys** filter the blood and make urine; the **bladder** stores it.

**Why diarrhoea kills** is the single most useful thing in this section: the gut stops taking the water back, so the body loses water and salts faster than they are replaced. The answer is to replace them — clean water with a little salt and sugar. The Handbook gives the proportions. A child who understands this at ten may save somebody at twenty.

## Teeth

Four kinds, with different jobs: **incisors** cut, **canines** tear, **premolars and molars** grind. Twenty milk teeth, replaced by up to thirty-two adult ones.

Enamel is the hardest thing the body makes — and it does not grow back. Bacteria in the mouth turn sugar into acid, the acid dissolves the enamel, and that is a cavity. **Clean them twice a day, keep sugar to mealtimes, and they will last a lifetime.** Where there is no dentist, this stops being advice and becomes maintenance of something irreplaceable. A frayed green twig works when there is no brush; salt water works when there is no paste.

## Senses and nerves

The **brain** decides and remembers; the **spinal cord** is the main cable; **nerves** run everywhere, carrying signals in both directions — in from the senses, out to the muscles.

**Five senses**, and what each detects: sight (light), hearing (vibration), smell and taste (chemicals), touch (pressure, heat, pain). Add balance, which lives in the inner ear.

**Reflexes** skip the brain entirely: a hand comes off something hot before you know it is hot, because the signal turns round at the spinal cord. Test the knee-jerk. Being fast matters more than being clever when something is burning you.

**Pain is information, not an enemy.** It says where and how bad. Teach the child to describe it — sharp or dull, where exactly, constant or coming in waves, what makes it worse — because that description is what a treatment decision is made from.

## What food is for, and what the body needs

| Group | What it does | Where from |
|---|---|---|
| **Carbohydrate** | fuel for everything | grain, potato, root vegetables |
| **Protein** | building and repairing | beans, eggs, milk, meat, fish, nuts |
| **Fat** | concentrated fuel, warmth, some vitamins | butter, oil, fat meat, nuts, seeds |
| **Vitamins and minerals** | small amounts, essential | fruit, vegetables, dairy, offal |
| **Fibre** | keeps the gut moving | whole grain, vegetables, pulses |
| **Water** | everything | 2–3 litres a day, more when hot or working |

Two that matter more than the rest where food is limited: **vitamin C** (green vegetables, fruit, rosehips — without it, scurvy) and **vitamin D** (sunlight on skin, oily fish, eggs — without it, weak bones).

**The daily care list**, which is the practical end of this whole lesson:

- **Sleep** — 9 to 11 hours for a child. It is when growing and repairing happen, and it is not optional.
- **Exercise** — every day, enough to be out of breath some of it.
- **Clean water and clean hands.** Hand-washing with soap, especially before food and after the latrine, prevents more illness than any medicine in the Handbook.
- **Keep wounds clean and covered.**
- **Warmth and dryness** — cold and wet make everything else worse.

## Exercises

1. Name three jobs the skeleton does and give an example of each.
2. Find on yourself a hinge joint, a ball-and-socket joint and a fixed joint. What does each let you do?
3. Bend and straighten your arm slowly with the other hand on the upper arm. Describe what you feel and explain it using the word *antagonistic*.
4. Put the circuit in order: lungs, body, heart, heart. What is picked up in the lungs, and what is dropped off?
5. What is the difference between an artery and a vein? Why can you feel a pulse in one and not the other?
6. Take your pulse at rest. Run or skip for two minutes and take it again, then every minute until it is back to normal. Record the numbers in a table and draw the graph. How long did it take to recover?
7. Count someone's breaths for a minute without telling them. Why does telling them spoil the measurement?
8. Explain what the diaphragm does when you breathe in. Why is "sucking air in" not quite right?
9. Put the digestive journey in order and say what happens at each stage: small intestine, mouth, large intestine, stomach, gullet.
10. Why does diarrhoea make a person dangerously ill, and what is the treatment?
11. Name the four kinds of tooth and what each is for. Why is a cavity permanent?
12. What is a reflex, and why is it useful that it does not go to the brain first?
13. You have burnt your hand. Describe the pain in the four ways a lesson said to: where, what kind, constant or coming in waves, what makes it worse.
14. Sort into food groups: potatoes, eggs, butter, cabbage, beans, oats, rosehips.
15. A person is living on grain and potatoes alone through a long winter. What are they likely to go short of, and what could you find or grow to fix it?
16. List, from memory, the five things in the daily care list and say why each one matters.

## Answers

1. Support (the spine holds you upright); protection (the skull guards the brain, ribs the heart and lungs); movement (bones are levers for the muscles).
2. Hinge — elbow or knee, bends one way. Ball and socket — shoulder or hip, moves in all directions. Fixed — the skull, no movement, for protection.
3. Bending: the biceps hardens (it is shortening and pulling), the triceps softens. Straightening: the opposite. They are antagonistic because a muscle can only pull, so it takes a pair working against each other to move a bone both ways.
4. Body → heart → lungs → heart → body. Oxygen is picked up; carbon dioxide is dropped off.
5. Arteries carry blood away from the heart at high pressure with thick walls; veins carry it back at low pressure and have valves. The pulse is the surge of pressure from each heartbeat, and only arteries carry it.
6. No fixed answer — look for a rest reading around 70–100 for a child, a rise with exercise, and a return within a few minutes. A quicker return means fitter.
7. No fixed answer, usually 12–20. Telling them spoils it because breathing is partly voluntary, so they change it without meaning to.
8. It flattens, which makes the chest cavity bigger. That lowers the pressure inside, and the higher pressure of the air outside pushes air in. You make space; the atmosphere does the pushing.
9. Mouth (cut, grind, saliva starts on starch) → gullet (squeezed down in waves) → stomach (acid and churning) → small intestine (goodness absorbed into the blood) → large intestine (water taken back).
10. The gut stops reabsorbing water, so the body loses water and salts faster than they go in. Treat by replacing them — clean water with a little salt and sugar, in small sips, kept up.
11. Incisors cut, canines tear, premolars and molars grind. Enamel does not regrow, so anything dissolved by acid is gone for good.
12. An automatic response that turns round at the spinal cord instead of going to the brain. It is faster, and speed is what matters when something is damaging you.
13. No fixed answer. Look for all four parts — the describing is the skill.
14. Carbohydrate: potatoes, oats. Protein: eggs, beans. Fat: butter. Vitamins: cabbage, rosehips. (Beans and oats also give fibre; eggs also give fat and vitamin D.)
15. Vitamin C above all — scurvy. Also vitamin D and fresh protein. Fix it with green leaves, sprouted grain or beans, rosehips, nettles, preserved fruit; and sunlight on skin for vitamin D.
16. Sleep (growth and repair); exercise (heart, muscles, lungs); clean water and hand-washing (stops the commonest illnesses); clean covered wounds (stops infection); warmth and dryness (the body fights nothing well when cold and wet).
