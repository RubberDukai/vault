# Living things

## Everything alive is made of cells

Every plant, animal, fungus and microbe is built from cells — tiny bags of chemistry, each one alive. A person is about thirty trillion of them. A bacterium is one.

Cells need the same things whatever they belong to: **water**, **food** (energy and building material), the right **temperature**, and a way to get rid of **waste**. Every fact in this lesson comes from that list.

**A lens or a microscope** shows it. Pond water under a good magnifying glass is full of moving things. Onion skin under a microscope is a wall of bricks — each brick a cell.

## Plants make food from light

Plants are the only living things that make their own food. In the green parts, with light, they turn **water** (from the roots) and **carbon dioxide** (from the air) into **sugar**, and give off **oxygen**. This is **photosynthesis**, and everything else alive eats the result, directly or indirectly.

Which is why:
- A plant in the dark dies. No light, no food.
- A plant needs water at the roots and air at the leaves.
- The oxygen you breathe was made by a plant.
- **Every calorie you will ever eat was sunlight first.**

Plants also need small amounts of minerals from the soil — chiefly **nitrogen** (for leaves), **phosphorus** (roots and seeds) and **potassium** (general health). These are what manure, compost and diluted urine put back. A crop takes them out; something must return them or the soil is exhausted.

**Experiment:** two identical seedlings, one in light, one in a dark cupboard, both watered. A week later, compare. Then put the pale one back in the light and watch it recover.

## Animals eat plants, or eat things that ate plants

A **food chain**: grass → rabbit → fox. Sunlight → grass → rabbit → fox → (when it dies) worms and fungi → soil → grass. Everything connects and nothing is wasted.

At each step, about **nine-tenths of the energy is lost** — as heat, movement, and the parts that cannot be eaten. A field of grass feeds many rabbits; the rabbits feed few foxes. Which is also why growing grain to feed to animals to eat produces far less food than eating the grain — a fact that matters enormously when land is short.

**Experiment:** trace every food you ate today back to a plant. Milk → cow → grass. Bread → wheat. Egg → hen → grain and insects → plants.

## Microbes — the ones you cannot see

Bacteria, fungi, and the viruses that are not quite alive. They are everywhere, in unimaginable numbers, and most are harmless or useful. A few are not, and those few explain most of the medical module.

**The useful ones:**
- **Compost** is microbes eating dead plants and turning them back into soil. Warmth, damp and air make them work faster — which is why a heap is turned.
- **Bread, cheese, yoghurt, sauerkraut, beer** — all made by microbes eating sugar and leaving something we like behind. Fermenting food is farming microbes.
- **Your gut** holds trillions of them and you could not digest without them.
- **Nitrogen-fixing bacteria** on the roots of beans and peas pull nitrogen out of the air and put it in the soil. That is why beans go in the rotation.

**The harmful ones:**
- Some bacteria produce **toxins**. Some multiply inside a wound. Some are carried in water from one person's gut to another's.
- They need what all cells need: warmth, water, food. **Take one away and they stop.** Cold slows them (the larder). Dryness stops them (dried food). Salt draws the water out of them (salting). Heat kills them (boiling, cooking). Acid stops them (pickling). This is the entire logic of food preservation and water treatment, and a child who understands it can work out any method from first principles.
- **Boiling water kills them** because the cells cannot survive 100 °C. Filtering removes the bigger ones physically. Chlorine poisons them. That is why the water chapter says filter *and* disinfect.

**Experiment:** two slices of bread, one touched with clean hands, one with dirty hands, each in a sealed bag in a warm place. A week later, look. Then do the same with one bag in the cold.

## Why you get better

Your body fights microbes with an **immune system** — cells that recognise invaders and destroy them, and that **remember**. Once you have had a disease, or a vaccine that imitates it, the memory cells respond so fast the next time that you do not get ill. That is all a vaccine is: a rehearsal.

**Fever** is the body raising its temperature to make life harder for the invaders. **Pus** is dead white cells that fought at a wound. **Swelling** is blood rushing supplies to the site. The body is not malfunctioning; it is at war, and mostly it wins.

## Reproduction and inheritance

Living things make copies of themselves, and the copies resemble the parents because instructions are passed on — in every cell, in a molecule called DNA. Seeds carry the plant's instructions; eggs and sperm carry an animal's.

Two things follow that matter for a farm:

- **Like breeds like.** Save seed from the best plants and, over generations, the crop gets better. This is how every crop and every farm animal was made from a wild ancestor, over thousands of years, by people who knew nothing of DNA.
- **Variety is insurance.** A field of identical plants dies of the same disease on the same day. A field of varied ones has survivors.

## Pollination

Most fruit and seed crops need pollen carried from one flower to another, and **insects do most of it** — bees above all. No bees, no apples, no beans, no squash. A garden that poisons its insects starves. Flowers left for the bees, a hive or two, and rough uncut corners are not decoration; they are infrastructure.

## Exercises

1. List five things every living cell needs. For each, name a way of preserving food that works by removing it.
2. Draw a food chain that ends with you. Where did the energy start?
3. Why does turning a compost heap make it work faster?
4. A field of wheat feeds 100 people for a year. If instead the wheat is fed to cattle and the people eat the beef, roughly how many does it feed? Why?
5. Why do we boil water rather than just filtering it? Why do we filter rather than just boiling?
6. Explain, in terms of cells, why a fever is not a bad thing in itself.
7. A gardener saves seed only from her tallest bean plants, every year for ten years. What happens to her beans, and why?

## Answers

1. Water — drying. Food (sugars) — there is no way to remove it, so it is denied to microbes instead by fermenting, which turns it to acid or alcohol. Warmth — chilling, freezing, a cold cellar. Oxygen — sealing, canning, submerging in brine or oil. A tolerable acidity — pickling in vinegar. Salting and smoking work mainly by removing water and adding what microbes cannot live in.
2. E.g. sun → grass → cow → milk → you; or sun → wheat → you. The energy started as sunlight, captured by a plant.
3. Turning lets air in. The microbes that break down a heap fastest need oxygen, and they use it up in the middle; turning also moves the cool outside to the hot centre, so everything is broken down rather than just the middle.
4. Roughly **ten people**, perhaps fewer. Most of the energy in the wheat is used by the cattle for living, moving and keeping warm, and is lost as heat — only about a tenth reaches the next step of a food chain.
5. Boiling kills microbes but leaves dirt, sediment and anything dissolved; filtering removes the dirt and some microbes but not all of them, and not viruses. Doing both, in that order — filter, then boil — is why the two are taught together.
6. A fever is the body raising its own temperature on purpose, because most microbes breed best at normal body temperature and are slowed by heat, while the immune cells work faster warm. It is the defence, not the disease. (A very high or prolonged fever is still dangerous in itself, especially in the very young and very old — see the Handbook.)
7. Her beans get taller, generation by generation. The tallest plants pass on the versions of their genes that made them tall, so those versions become commoner each year. It is artificial selection, and it is the same process as natural selection with the gardener doing the choosing — though selecting hard for one thing alone can lose others, such as flavour or yield, so it is better to select from several good plants than one.
