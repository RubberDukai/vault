# The Earth in space

**What they need first:** *Seasons, weather and the sky* at KS1; *Forces, light and sound* for gravity and light; *The continents and the climate* for why the tilt matters on the ground.

This lesson needs no equipment at all — only a dark night, a stick in the ground and the patience to look at the same thing twice. It ends with something immediately practical: a person who knows the sky can tell the time, find north, know the date and predict the tide without a single manufactured object.

**The safety rule, and it is absolute: never look at the sun**, with the naked eye and above all through any lens, binocular or telescope. It causes permanent blindness in less than a second, painlessly. Project the sun's image onto card instead.

## Day, year and the seasons

- **A day** is one spin of the Earth on its axis — 24 hours. The sun does not move across the sky; **we turn**. Say it that way round every time, because the words we all use ("sunrise", "the sun goes down") teach the opposite.
- **A year** is one trip round the sun — 365¼ days, which is why there is a leap year every fourth one to use up the quarters.
- **The seasons** come from the **23.5° tilt**, which stays pointing the same way all the way round. In our summer the northern half leans towards the sun: the light arrives steeper and the days are longer, so more heat arrives and less escapes. In winter it leans away. Distance from the sun has almost nothing to do with it — the Earth is actually nearest the sun in January.

**Prove the spin with a stick.** Push a straight stick into level ground and mark the tip of its shadow every hour through a day. The shadow is shortest at local noon, and at that moment it points **due north** (in the northern hemisphere). Do it again at the solstices and equinoxes and you have a calendar as well as a compass. This is the oldest scientific instrument in the world and it costs a stick.

## The Moon

The Moon goes round the Earth in about 27 days and takes about 29½ days from one new moon to the next.

- **Phases** are not a shadow of the Earth. Half the Moon is always lit by the sun; we see a different fraction of that lit half depending on where it is in its circuit. Demonstrate with a ball, a lamp and a slowly turning child in the middle, and it becomes obvious in a minute.
- **The same face always points at us**, because the Moon turns once per orbit.
- **Tides** are the Moon's gravity pulling the oceans — roughly two high tides a day. **Spring tides** (the biggest) come when sun and moon pull in line, at new and full moon; **neap tides** (the smallest) at half moon. For anybody working a coast, that is a working calendar, not an astronomical curiosity.
- **A lunar eclipse** is the Earth's shadow falling on the Moon — anyone on the night side can watch it. **[A solar eclipse](wiki:Solar_eclipse)** is the Moon's shadow falling on the Earth — visible only in a narrow track, which is why any one place sees one rarely.

## The solar system

Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune. The first four are small and rocky; the outer four are giants of gas and ice, with the asteroid belt between.

- **The sun is a star**, and it holds everything in orbit by gravity. It is over a million times the volume of the Earth and contains almost all the matter in the solar system.
- **Scale is the hard part, so pace it out.** If the sun is a football at one end of a field, the Earth is a peppercorn 25 metres away, Jupiter a marble at 130 metres, and Neptune a peppercorn most of a kilometre off. The next star would be another football — about six thousand kilometres away. Do this walk once; no diagram teaches it.
- **Planets wander.** That is what the word means. Watch one for a few weeks against the fixed stars and you will see it move — which is exactly how the ancients worked out something was different about them.
- **Moons, comets and asteroids** fill in the rest. Meteors ("shooting stars") are specks burning up in the atmosphere, most of them no bigger than a grain of sand.

## Stars, and using them

- A star is an enormous ball of gas, giving off light and heat from nuclear fusion in its core, where hydrogen is pressed into helium. Our sun does this and has done for about 4.6 billion years.
- **Light takes time.** Sunlight takes about 8 minutes to reach us; the nearest star's light takes over 4 years. Looking out is looking back in time.
- **Stars have life cycles** — they form from clouds of gas, burn, and end either quietly or in an explosion that scatters heavier elements. The iron in your blood and the calcium in your bones were made inside stars. That is literally true and worth saying out loud.
- **Galaxies** are vast collections of stars. Ours is [the Milky Way](wiki:Milky_Way) — the band across a really dark sky is our own galaxy seen edge-on from inside it.

**What to learn in the sky**, in this order, because each one finds the next:

1. **[The Plough](wiki:Big_Dipper|Ursa_Major)** (the Big Dipper), which is always up in Britain.
2. **[The Pole Star](wiki:Polaris)** — follow the two end stars of the Plough's bowl about five times their separation. It sits almost exactly over the north pole, so it barely moves all night and all year. **That is your compass**, permanently.
3. **Orion** in winter, with its three-star belt — the easiest constellation in the sky and a marker of the season.
4. **Cassiopeia**, a W opposite the Plough around the Pole Star.
5. **The planets** — they shine steadily where stars twinkle, and they move over weeks.

**Your latitude is the angle of the Pole Star above the horizon.** Measure that angle with a protractor, a plumb line and a straw and you know how far north you are, anywhere, with no instrument you could not make. That single fact was worth empires.

## Gravity and orbits

Every mass attracts every other mass. The bigger the masses and the closer they are, the stronger the pull. It is why you stay on the ground, why the Moon circles us, why we circle the sun, and why the tides move.

**An orbit is a permanent falling.** Throw a stone and it curves to the ground. Throw it harder and it lands further off. Throw it hard enough and the ground curves away beneath it as fast as it falls, and it never lands — that is orbit. Newton drew exactly this with a cannon on a mountain, and it is still the clearest explanation there is.

**Weightlessness in orbit is not the absence of gravity** — gravity up there is nearly as strong as down here. Everything is falling together, so nothing presses on anything else.

## How we know

Worth a lesson of its own, because "how do you know?" is the whole of science.

- **The Earth is round**, and has been known to be since the Greeks: a ship's hull disappears before its mast; the Earth's shadow on the Moon is always a circle; the stars you can see change as you travel north or south. [Eratosthenes](wiki:Eratosthenes) measured its size around 240 BC from two shadows and the distance between two towns, and got within a few per cent.
- **The Earth goes round the sun**, not the reverse: the phases of Venus, the moons of Jupiter orbiting something that is not us, and the slight yearly shift in the apparent positions of nearby stars.
- **The universe is very old and very large**: rocks dated by radioactive decay, the distances measured to stars, and light from objects that has been travelling for billions of years.

None of that required trusting anybody. Every one of those observations can be checked, and several of them can be checked by a child with a stick, a dark night and a written record.

## Exercises

1. Explain the difference between a day and a year in terms of the Earth's two motions.
2. Why is it summer in Britain while it is winter in Australia? Why is "we are closer to the sun in summer" wrong?
3. Do the stick-and-shadow experiment. Mark the shadow tip each hour for a day. When was it shortest, and which direction did it point then? What have you built?
4. Using a ball and a lamp, explain why the Moon has phases. Why is a crescent moon *not* the Earth's shadow?
5. Why do we always see the same face of the Moon?
6. When are the biggest tides, and why? Why does this matter to somebody fishing or gathering on a shore?
7. What is the difference between a solar and [a lunar eclipse](wiki:Lunar_eclipse), and why do you see far more of one than the other?
8. Name the planets in order. Which are rocky and which are giants?
9. Pace out a scale model of the solar system with the sun as a football. Record the distances. How far away would the next star be?
10. Find the Plough on a clear night. Use it to find the Pole Star. Describe exactly how, then check it against a compass.
11. Measure the Pole Star's angle above the horizon with a home-made instrument. What does the number mean? Compare it with your latitude on the Maps tab.
12. Watch a planet for four weeks, marking its position against nearby stars each week. What did it do that a star does not?
13. Explain an orbit using Newton's cannon. Why does an astronaut float if gravity is still strong up there?
14. Sunlight takes 8 minutes to reach us. What does that mean about what you are seeing when you look at the sun — and what about a star 400 light years away?
15. Give three separate pieces of evidence that the Earth is a sphere, and say how you could check each one yourself.
16. Where did the iron in your blood come from? Explain the chain.

## Answers

1. A day is one spin of the Earth on its axis; a year is one orbit round the sun.
2. The Earth's 23.5° tilt stays pointing the same way all year, so when the northern half leans towards the sun it gets steeper light and longer days — summer — while the southern half leans away. The distance explanation is wrong both because the change in distance is tiny and because the Earth is in fact nearest the sun in January, during a northern winter.
3. Shortest at local noon, pointing due north (northern hemisphere). You have built a sundial and a compass at once.
4. Half the Moon is always lit by the sun; we see varying amounts of that lit half as it moves round us. It is not the Earth's shadow, which only touches the Moon during the rare lunar eclipse — and the Moon has phases every month.
5. It turns on its own axis exactly once per orbit, so the same side stays facing us.
6. Spring tides at new and full moon, when the sun and moon pull in line and their effects add. It matters because the lowest low tides expose ground that is normally under water — and the highest high tides come in further and faster than expected, which is how people are cut off.
7. Lunar: the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon, visible from the whole night side of the Earth. Solar: the Moon's shadow falls on the Earth in a narrow track only a few tens of kilometres wide, so any given place sees one very rarely.
8. Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune. The first four are rocky; the last four are giants.
9. Roughly: Earth 25 m, Mars 40 m, Jupiter 130 m, Saturn 240 m, Neptune 760 m. The next star would be about 6,000 km away — which is the real lesson.
10. Follow the two stars at the end of the Plough's bowl, away from the handle, about five times the distance between them. Compass should agree: it is north.
11. The angle equals your latitude. In Britain it should read somewhere around 50–58°.
12. It moved against the background stars, while the stars kept their positions relative to one another. It also shone with a steady light rather than twinkling.
13. A cannonball fired harder lands further away; fired hard enough, the Earth's surface curves away as fast as the ball falls, so it never lands — it is in orbit, permanently falling. An astronaut floats because they and their craft are falling together, so nothing presses on anything; gravity is still nearly as strong there.
14. You are seeing the sun as it was 8 minutes ago. A star 400 light years away is seen as it was 400 years ago — it might not exist now.
15. Ships' hulls vanish before their masts (watch a boat leave); the Earth's shadow on the Moon is always circular (watch a lunar eclipse); the stars visible and the Pole Star's height change as you travel north or south (measure it in two places). Also: the same stick-shadow measured at two distant places gives different angles, which is exactly what Eratosthenes did.
16. It was made by nuclear fusion inside a star, scattered into space when that star died, gathered into the cloud that formed the sun and the Earth, taken up from the ground by plants, and eaten.
