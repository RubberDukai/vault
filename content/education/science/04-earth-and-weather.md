# Earth, water and weather

## The water cycle

There is a fixed amount of water on Earth and it goes round. The sun **evaporates** it from seas, lakes and leaves; the vapour rises, cools, and **condenses** into cloud; the cloud drops it as **rain or snow**; the water runs into streams, soaks into the ground, or is drunk by plants and breathed out again; and the rivers carry it back to the sea.

Which means: the water in your well fell as rain, ran through the ground being filtered on the way, and will evaporate and fall again. Rainwater is close to distilled. Groundwater has been cleaned by rock. River water has been through every field and village upstream — which is why the water chapter cares so much about where you draw from.

**Experiment:** a bowl of salty water, a cup standing in the middle, clear plastic over the top with a stone weighting the centre above the cup, left in the sun. Fresh water collects in the cup. That is the water cycle in a bowl, and it is also a way of making sea water drinkable.

## Why it rains

Warm air holds more water than cold air. When air cools — because it rises over a hill, or meets a colder mass, or the night comes — it can hold less, and the excess falls out as **dew, fog, cloud, rain**.

So:
- **Hills make rain** on the side the wind hits, and a dry "rain shadow" behind. This is why western Britain is wet and eastern Britain is drier.
- **Clear nights are cold** — no cloud to hold the day's heat in — and produce dew and frost.
- **A sudden drop in temperature** brings cloud and rain.

## Reading the sky

Nobody can forecast a week ahead without instruments and data, but a day ahead is often readable, and the old rhymes mostly encode real physics.

- **High wispy clouds** (cirrus, "mares' tails") thickening from the west — a warm front is coming; rain within a day.
- **Small puffy white clouds** (cumulus) on a warm day — fair weather. If they grow tall and dark by afternoon, thunderstorms.
- **A grey sheet lowering** (stratus) — steady rain soon.
- **A ring around the sun or moon** — ice crystals high up; a front approaching.
- **Red sky at night** — the setting sun lighting dust in dry air to the west, which is where tomorrow's weather comes from. Fair. **Red sky in the morning** — the same dry air has passed; rain may follow.
- **Wind backing** (turning anticlockwise, say from west to south) — a low is coming; worsening. **Veering** (clockwise) — improving.
- **Swallows flying low**, insects close to the ground, smoke drifting downward — falling pressure, damp air; rain coming.
- **A sudden cold gust** on a hot day, with a dark base to the cloud — get under cover now.

**A barometer** — even a home-made one — turns all of this into a number. Falling pressure means worsening weather; rising means improving; a fast fall means a storm. Watch the trend, not the value.

**Keep a weather diary.** Date, morning sky, wind direction, what happened. In a year you will know your own valley's habits better than any rhyme.

## Seasons

The Earth is tilted. As it goes round the sun, first one hemisphere and then the other leans toward it. Leaning toward: long days, high sun, summer. Leaning away: short days, low sun, winter. **Not** because the Earth is nearer the sun in summer — it is actually slightly nearer in January.

For a farmer this matters as **day length**. Plants respond to it. The longest day (around 21 June) and the shortest (around 21 December) are fixed; the frost dates around them are what you learn from your own diary.

The sun's height also tells you direction. At local noon it is due south in the northern hemisphere. In summer it rises well north of east; in winter well south of it. See the navigation section of the maps chapter.

## Rock, soil and why the ground matters

Rock breaks down — by frost, roots, rain and time — into soil, and soil is rock dust plus the remains of living things. Its **texture** decides what it grows:

- **Sand** — big grains, drains fast, warms early, holds little water or food. Add compost.
- **Clay** — tiny grains, holds water and nutrients, heavy, cold, cracks when dry, waterlogs when wet. Add grit and compost; never dig it wet.
- **Loam** — the mix in between. What every gardener wants.

**The jar test:** half fill a jar with soil, top up with water, shake, leave a day. Sand settles first, then silt, then clay on top. The layers tell you what you have.

Underneath, **rock decides water**. Chalk and limestone drink rain and give it back as springs at the base of the hill. Clay holds water on the surface — ponds, marsh, floods. Granite sheds it fast. Knowing which you stand on tells you where to dig a well and where not to build a house.

## Air

We live at the bottom of an ocean of air, and its weight is pressure — about 1 kg on every square centimetre. Warm air rises; cold air sinks and flows in underneath; that flow is **wind**. A sea breeze by day (land heats faster, air rises over it, sea air flows in) and a land breeze at night is the smallest version; the great wind belts of the planet are the largest.

Air is a mix: about four-fifths nitrogen, one-fifth oxygen, a little argon, and a trace of carbon dioxide. **Fire needs the oxygen; you need the oxygen; a sealed room with a fire in it runs out.** Which is the carbon monoxide warning in the fire module from the other direction.
