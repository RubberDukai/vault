# Forces, light and sound

**What they need first:** *How to find things out* — fair testing, because half of this lesson is experiments. Nothing else.

This is physics done with string, a mirror and a plank. Everything here can be tested in an afternoon, and most of it explains a tool the household already uses.

## Forces are pushes and pulls

A **force** is a push or a pull. You cannot see one; you see what it does. A force can make something **start** moving, **stop**, **speed up**, **slow down**, **change direction**, or **change shape**.

Forces come in two kinds: those that need **contact** (a push, friction, air resistance, the tension in a rope) and those that act **at a distance** (gravity, magnetism, static electricity).

**Balanced and unbalanced.** When forces balance, nothing changes — a book on a table is pulled down by gravity and pushed up by the table, exactly equally, so it stays. When they do not balance, something changes: it moves, or speeds up, or breaks. Every still object the child can see is a picture of balanced forces, and that is worth pointing out until it is obvious.

Forces are measured in **newtons (N)**. A 1 kg mass weighs about 10 N on Earth. A rubber band or a spring with a scale beside it makes a usable force meter — the further it stretches, the bigger the force.

## Gravity

Gravity pulls everything towards the centre of the Earth. Two things surprise people:

- **Mass and weight are different.** Mass is how much stuff there is — the same everywhere. Weight is the pull of gravity on it, which is less on the Moon. A 10 kg sack has 10 kg of mass wherever it goes; it weighs about 100 N here and about 16 N on the Moon.
- **Heavy and light things fall at the same rate**, once air is out of the way. Drop a stone and a pebble together: they land together. Then drop a stone and a sheet of paper: the paper loses — not because it is lighter but because of **air resistance**. Screw the paper into a ball and drop it again, and it keeps up. That one experiment, done in that order, teaches more than a page of explanation.

## Friction and air resistance

**Friction** is the force between two surfaces rubbing. It slows things down and makes heat — which is a nuisance in an axle and the whole point in a brake, a fire drill and the soles of your boots.

Test it: pull a weighted shoe across wood, stone, grass and ice with a rubber-band force meter and record the stretch each time. That is a fair test with one variable changed, and it produces a table worth graphing.

**More friction** is wanted in: brakes, boot soles, a rope round a post, a wedge, a knot.
**Less friction** is wanted in: axles, hinges, saw blades, sledges — hence oil, grease, tallow and wheels.

**Air resistance** and **water resistance** are friction with a fluid. A parachute wants as much as possible; a boat's hull and an arrow want as little. Have the child make three paper parachutes of different sizes and time them down from the same height — same string, same weight, one variable.

## The six machines

A machine does not create force out of nothing. It trades **distance for force**: you pull with less force over a longer distance, and the work done is the same. Say that clearly, because "you get something for nothing" is what children conclude otherwise.

| Machine | What it does | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|
| **Lever** | a bar on a pivot multiplies force | crowbar, spade, wheelbarrow, scissors, your own forearm |
| **Pulley** | changes the direction of a pull; several together multiply it | well, hoist, sail, block and tackle |
| **Wheel and axle** | turns a small turn at the centre into a big one at the rim, or the reverse | windlass, winch, cart, doorknob, screwdriver |
| **Ramp (inclined plane)** | raise a load over a longer distance with less force | loading plank, path zig-zagging up a hill |
| **Wedge** | a moving ramp that splits | axe, chisel, knife, nail, plough |
| **Screw** | a ramp wrapped round a post | bolt, vice, press, auger |

**The lever is the one to teach properly**, because it explains so many tools. The further from the pivot you push, the more force you get — which is why a longer crowbar shifts a heavier rock, why you grip a spanner at the end, and why a wheelbarrow with the load near the wheel is easier to lift. Set up a ruler on a pencil with coins on each side and let the child find the balance point. Then move the pivot and watch what changes.

## Light

**Light travels in straight lines**, and fast — instantly, for any purpose you will have. Everything else follows from the straight lines.

- **We see because light bounces off things into our eyes.** This is worth insisting on: the eye does not send anything out. In perfect darkness you see nothing, however long you wait, because there is nothing to bounce.
- **Sources** make their own light: sun, fire, lamp, lightning, a glow-worm. Everything else only reflects. The Moon is not a source.
- **Shadows** are where an object blocks the straight lines. Because the lines are straight, the shadow has the shape of the object. Move a torch closer and the shadow grows — draw the straight lines from the torch past the edges of the object to see why. Follow a stick's shadow through a whole day and you have a sundial and a compass at once (see *Seasons, weather and the sky*).
- **Reflection.** A mirror bounces light at the same angle it arrives — like a ball off a wall. Two mirrors at an angle make multiple images; two facing each other make a tunnel of them. A periscope is two mirrors at 45°, and a child can build one from a milk carton in ten minutes.
- **Refraction.** Light bends when it passes into water or glass. That is why a stick in a bucket looks broken at the surface, why a coin in a bowl reappears when you add water, and why a lens works. A clear jar of water is a lens: it magnifies, and in sun it will light tinder.
- **Colour.** White light is all the colours together. A prism, a glass of water in a sunbeam, or a puddle with a film of oil splits it, which is what a rainbow is: sunlight split by raindrops, always with the sun behind you. A red cloth looks red because it absorbs the other colours and reflects red.
- **Opaque, translucent, transparent** — blocks light, lets some through scattered, lets it through clearly.

## Sound

**Sound is a vibration** travelling through a material. No material, no sound — which is why space is silent.

- **Feel it.** Put fingers on your throat and hum. Sprinkle grains on a drum skin and bang it. Twang a ruler off the edge of a table. Every sound has something shaking.
- **It needs a medium**, and travels *better* through solids and liquids than air — put an ear to the ground or to a fence rail and you hear a long way. Two tins and a taut string is a telephone, and it fails the moment the string goes slack, because the vibration cannot cross the gap.
- **Pitch** is how fast the vibration is: shorter, tighter, thinner and lighter all mean higher. Prove it by shortening the ruler over the edge, tightening a string, or blowing across bottles filled to different depths.
- **Volume** is how big the vibration is: hit it harder, get a bigger sound. It fades with distance because the vibration spreads out.
- **Sound travels about 340 m in a second** — slow enough to measure. Count the seconds between a lightning flash and its thunder and divide by three for the distance in kilometres. Do it in a real storm and the child will never forget it.
- **The ear** catches the vibration with a drum skin, passes it along three small bones and turns it into a signal the brain reads. Loud noise damages it permanently, which is why hearing protection is not optional around an axe, a mill or a gun.

## Exercises

1. Name the forces acting on: a book resting on a table; a bucket being lifted from a well; a sledge sliding to a stop.
2. A 5 kg sack: what is its mass, roughly what is its weight in newtons, and what changes if you take it to the Moon?
3. Drop a stone and a flat sheet of paper together, then a stone and the same paper screwed into a ball. Describe what happened and explain why.
4. Design a fair test to find out which of four surfaces has the most friction. Say what you change, what you keep the same and what you measure.
5. Which of the six machines is each of these, and what does it trade: an axe, a crowbar, a wheelbarrow, a well windlass, a loading plank, a bolt?
6. Why is it easier to carry a heavy load in a wheelbarrow with the load near the wheel than near the handles?
7. Make a parachute test: three sizes, same weight, same drop. Predict the result, then do it and record the times.
8. Explain, using straight lines, why a shadow gets bigger when you move the torch closer to the object.
9. Why can you not see anything in a completely dark cave, even after an hour?
10. Draw how a periscope works with two mirrors, showing the path of the light.
11. Put a stick in a bucket of water. Why does it look bent? Name the effect.
12. Why is the Moon not a light source, and where does its light come from?
13. Hold a ruler over the edge of a table and twang it. What happens to the pitch as you shorten the overhang, and why?
14. You see lightning and hear the thunder 9 seconds later. How far away was the storm?
15. Why can you hear a distant train better with an ear to the rail than standing up? Why is there no sound in space?
16. A tin-can telephone stops working when the string sags. Explain why.

## Answers

1. Book: gravity down, the table pushing up — balanced. Bucket: the pull of the rope up, gravity down, friction in the pulley. Sledge: friction with the snow slowing it, air resistance, gravity and the ground balanced.
2. Mass 5 kg; weight about 50 N. On the Moon the mass is still 5 kg but it weighs about 8 N, because gravity is weaker.
3. Flat paper falls slowly, the ball keeps up with the stone. The mass did not change — the air resistance did, because the flat sheet meets much more air.
4. Change: the surface. Keep the same: the object, its weight, how it is pulled, the distance. Measure: the force needed to start it sliding (the stretch of the band), or how far it slides from a fixed push.
5. Axe — wedge (a shorter, harder push for a longer split). Crowbar — lever. Wheelbarrow — lever (and a wheel). Windlass — wheel and axle. Plank — ramp. Bolt — screw. All trade distance for force.
6. It is a lever pivoting on the wheel. A load near the wheel is close to the pivot, so it takes less force at the handles to lift.
7. Bigger canopy, slower fall — more air resistance. Mark it right if the reasoning is right even when a badly made parachute misbehaves.
8. Light goes in straight lines from the torch past the object's edges. Moving the torch closer widens the angle of those lines, so they spread further apart by the time they reach the wall.
9. Nothing is giving off light, so there is nothing to bounce off the walls into your eyes. Eyes only receive; they do not send light out.
10. Two mirrors at 45°, facing each other at opposite ends of a tube. Light enters, bounces down off the top mirror, bounces out off the bottom one into the eye.
11. The light bends as it leaves the water and enters the air, so the underwater part appears to be in a different place. The effect is refraction.
12. It makes no light of its own; it reflects sunlight.
13. The pitch rises. A shorter length vibrates faster, and faster vibration is higher pitch.
14. 9 ÷ 3 = about 3 km. (Or 9 × 340 = 3,060 m.)
15. Sound travels better through a solid than through air, because the particles are packed close and pass the vibration on more efficiently. In space there is no material at all, so there is nothing to carry the vibration.
16. The vibration travels along a taut string. A slack string absorbs the movement instead of passing it on, so the sound does not reach the other tin.
