# Seasons, weather and the sky

**What they need first:** the months and days from *Shapes, measures, time and money*.

## The four seasons

**Spring** (March, April, May): days getting longer, buds, blossom, lambs, sowing. **Summer** (June, July, August): long days, warmth, flowers, hay, the first harvest. **Autumn** (September, October, November): days shortening, fruit and nuts, leaves turning and falling, the main harvest, animals fattening. **Winter** (December, January, February): short days, cold, bare trees, frost and snow, the store cupboard. Draw the same tree in each season. Walk the same path once a month and list what has changed — the best long science project a child can do, and it costs nothing.

**Why?** Not for now, beyond this: the sun is higher in the sky and up for longer in summer, lower and for less time in winter, so there is more warmth in summer. (The tilted Earth is *Earth and weather* at KS2.)

## Watching the weather

Every morning at breakfast, look out and record: **sunny, cloudy, rainy, windy, foggy, frosty, snowy** — with a symbol on a chart. Add **warm/cold** (or the thermometer reading once they can read numbers), and **which way the wind blows** (a ribbon on a stick; the compass points from *Reading a map*). At the end of the month, count: how many sunny days? Which was the wettest week?

**Make the instruments:**
- **Rain gauge** — a straight-sided jar with a ruler; empty it each day and write the depth.
- **Wind vane** — a card arrow on a pin on a stick; it points *into* the wind, and the wind is named by where it *comes from*.
- **Wind strength** — the old scale by what you see: smoke rises straight (calm), leaves rustle (light), small branches move (moderate), whole trees sway (strong), branches break (gale).
- **Thermometer** — a bought one, in the shade, read at the same time each day.

**Weather words** to use exactly: *drizzle, shower, downpour, breeze, gale, mist, fog, frost, hail, sleet, thunder*. Which weather is good for what: washing, sowing, haymaking, sledging?

**Clouds.** Three shapes: high thin wisps (fair, change coming), fluffy heaps (fair weather; tall dark heaps mean showers), grey sheets (rain). Watch the sky before deciding to cut the hay.

## The sun and the day

The sun **rises** in the east and **sets** in the west, and is highest at midday, in the south. Stand a stick in the ground on a sunny morning and mark the tip of its shadow every hour: the marks make a curve, shortest at midday — a sundial, and the beginning of astronomy. Notice through the year that the midday shadow is long in winter and short in summer, and that the sun rises and sets further round in summer.

**Day and night.** The Earth turns; when our side faces the sun it is day. A torch and an orange in a dark room: turn the orange and watch the pin on it go into the dark. **Never look at the sun** — not with eyes, never with glasses or binoculars.

**Length of day.** Record sunrise and sunset from the *almanac* in this vault once a week; draw the day getting longer to midsummer and shorter to midwinter. On the shortest day, light a candle and say the days will get longer now: every culture did.

## The moon and the stars

The moon changes shape over a month: new (invisible), crescent, half, gibbous, full, then back. Draw it every clear night for a month and pin the drawings in a row. It rises later each night. The shape is the sunlit half seen from different sides: the orange again, with a torch, and a walnut for the moon.

On a clear night, find the **Plough** (seven stars like a saucepan) and follow the two end stars to the **Pole Star**, which never moves and is always north. That is enough for now; the *Science → Night sky* tab in this vault has the rest, and it works without the internet.

## The weather diary

Keep the chart going for a year and it becomes something valuable: the date of the first frost, the last frost, the first swallow, the first blossom, the wettest month. Next year the family will know when it is safe to plant out. This is exactly what the almanac in the handbook is built from, and children who keep one grow into adults who read the sky.

## Exercises

1. Name the four seasons in order and one thing that happens in each.
2. Which months are winter? Which is the first month of spring?
3. Draw the weather symbols for sunny, cloudy, rainy, windy, snowy.
4. Look at your weather chart. How many rainy days were there this week? Which day was warmest?
5. Where does the sun rise? Where is it at midday? Where does it set?
6. Why is a shadow short at midday and long in the evening?
7. Draw the moon as it looks tonight. What shape will it be in a week?
8. The wind vane points to the west. Where is the wind coming from? What is the wind called?
9. Which weather is best for drying the washing, and why?
10. What are the two end stars of the Plough for?

## Answers

1. Spring, summer, autumn, winter; e.g. blossom, hay, falling leaves, frost.
2. December, January, February; March.
3. Any clear set of symbols.
4. From the chart.
5. East; high in the south; west.
6. The sun is high at midday, so the shadow is short; low in the evening, so it stretches out.
7. Check against the sky; a week on, roughly the next phase (crescent → half → gibbous → full …).
8. From the west; a west wind (or westerly).
9. Sunny and windy — warmth and moving air carry the water away.
10. Finding the Pole Star, which shows north.
