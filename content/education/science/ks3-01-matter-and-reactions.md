# Matter and chemical reactions

**What they need first:** *Materials* at KS1 and *Energy, heat and electricity*; the maths of *Decimals and percentages* for the proportions.

Chemistry is the study of what things are made of and what happens when they meet. Most of it can be done with a fire, water, salt, vinegar, wood ash, chalk and a few jars — and nearly all of it explains something the household does already: baking, brewing, preserving, tanning, soap, lime mortar, rust and fire.

**Safety first, and mean it.** Never taste anything unknown. Never heat a sealed container — it becomes a bomb. Never mix chemicals to see what happens, and in particular never mix acid with bleach or ammonia. Work outside or by an open window. Tie hair back, protect eyes, know where the water is. Wood ash solution and quicklime burn skin like an acid does. Teach this before the first experiment, not after.

## Everything is made of atoms

- An **atom** is the smallest piece of an element that is still that element — far too small to see with any microscope you will have.
- An **element** is a substance made of just one kind of atom: iron, oxygen, carbon, copper, sulphur. About ninety occur naturally.
- A **molecule** is atoms joined together: O₂ is two oxygen atoms; H₂O is two hydrogen and one oxygen.
- A **compound** is two or more different elements chemically joined — and it is **nothing like** the things it was made from. Sodium is a metal that catches fire in water; chlorine is a poisonous green gas; sodium chloride is table salt. That example is the best one-minute argument that compounds are genuinely new substances.
- A **mixture** is things jumbled together but not joined — air, sea water, soil, brass. A mixture can be separated by physical means; a compound cannot.

**The periodic table** is the list of elements arranged so that ones with similar behaviour fall in the same column. It is worth looking up in the encyclopedia and worth knowing exists, but do not make a child memorise it. The useful part is the handful they will actually meet: carbon, oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen, iron, copper, tin, lead, sulphur, calcium, sodium, potassium.

## States of matter

| State | Particles | Shape | Volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Solid** | packed, vibrating in place | fixed | fixed |
| **Liquid** | touching, sliding past each other | takes the container | fixed |
| **Gas** | far apart, fast, free | fills the container | fills the container |

Heating gives particles energy to move more; cooling takes it away. **Melting, boiling, evaporating, condensing, freezing** and **sublimation** (solid straight to gas — dry ice, frost disappearing on a cold dry day) are all the same story told at different temperatures.

- **A change of state is physical and reversible.** Ice, water and steam are all H₂O; nothing new has been made.
- **Water expands when it freezes**, which is unusual and enormously important: it is why pipes burst, why rocks split and make soil, and why ice floats instead of sinking and killing everything in the pond.
- **Evaporation cools.** The fastest particles leave, so the average of those remaining is lower. That is sweat, a wet cloth round a jug, and the unglazed pot standing in a draught that keeps food cool with no ice at all.

## Separating mixtures

Every one of these is a household technique as well as a laboratory one.

| Method | Separates | Where you use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Filtering** | insoluble solid from liquid | straining, cleaning water through sand and charcoal |
| **Evaporating** | dissolved solid from liquid | getting salt from brine |
| **Distilling** | liquid from dissolved solids, or liquids of different boiling points | making drinking water from sea water or from foul water |
| **Sieving** | solids of different sizes | flour, gravel, seed cleaning |
| **Decanting** | settled solid from liquid | pouring off clear liquid, clarifying |
| **Magnetism** | iron from anything else | picking nails out of ash |

**Distillation is the one to build.** Water boiled in a covered pot with a cool lid sloping to a catch-cup will give safe water from foul, because the salts, the dirt and almost all the microbes stay behind. Do it once and the principle is owned for life. (The Handbook's water chapters give the practical build.)

**Solutions.** A **solute** dissolves in a **solvent** to make a solution. Warmer water dissolves more of most solids — which is why sugar goes into hot tea and why a saturated solution left to cool grows crystals. Grow some: salt, or better, a sugar or alum crystal on a thread over a week.

## Acids and alkalis

- **Acids** taste sour (vinegar, lemon, sour milk), react with metals and with chalk, and have a pH below 7.
- **Alkalis** feel soapy (wood-ash water, lime, soda, soap), and have a pH above 7.
- **Neutral** is 7 — pure water.
- The **pH scale** runs 0 to 14, and each step is ten times: pH 4 is ten times more acid than pH 5.

**Make your own indicator.** Boil red cabbage, keep the purple liquid. It goes red-pink in acid and green-yellow in alkali. Test vinegar, lemon, water, soap, wood-ash water, and — the practical one — **your soil**. Soil pH decides what will grow, and this is how you find it without buying anything. Test it in several places; it varies across one garden.

**Neutralising** is an acid and an alkali cancelling to make a salt and water. Its uses are everywhere: **lime or wood ash on acid soil**; chalk or soda for indigestion; soda on an acid spill; vinegar on limescale, which is the reverse.

## The reactions worth knowing

**Burning (oxidation).** Fuel + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + heat. The **fire triangle** is fuel, oxygen and heat: remove any one and the fire goes out, which is what every method of putting one out actually does. **Incomplete burning**, when there is not enough air, makes soot and **carbon monoxide** — invisible, odourless and deadly, and the reason a stove or a generator indoors kills people every winter. Ventilate.

**Rusting.** Iron + oxygen + water → rust. It needs both air and water, which is why a tool left oiled or kept dry does not rust, and why one left wet in a shed does. Prove it with three nails over a week: one in dry air with a lid, one in water that has been boiled and covered with oil to exclude air, one half in water and half in air. Only the third rusts properly. That is a controlled experiment worth doing.

**Fermentation.** Yeast turns sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide; bacteria turn sugar into acids. It is the same family of processes behind bread, beer, wine, vinegar, yoghurt, cheese, sauerkraut and silage — and the acid is what preserves the food, by making it a place other microbes cannot live. (The Handbook's *Fermenting, curing and the dairy* is the practical side; this lesson is why it works.)

**Conservation of mass.** Nothing is created or destroyed in a reaction — the atoms are rearranged. A burnt log seems to lose mass only because the carbon dioxide and water go up the chimney; weigh the gases and the total is the same. Show this with a sealed jar and a reaction that fizzes, weighed before and after.

**Lime, the household's chemistry set.** Heat limestone or chalk hard and it gives off carbon dioxide and becomes **quicklime** (burns skin badly). Add water — violently hot — and it becomes **slaked lime**: for mortar, limewash, sweetening acid soil, and disinfecting a latrine. Left in the air, it slowly takes carbon dioxide back and becomes limestone again, which is how lime mortar sets and why it is one of the oldest and most useful cycles anybody worked out.

**Soap.** Fat plus a strong alkali — wood-ash lye or slaked lime — makes soap and glycerine. Soap works because one end of its molecule grips water and the other grips grease, so grease can be lifted off and rinsed away. That explains, in one sentence, why washing your hands works, and washing them is the most effective piece of medicine in this vault.

## Exercises

1. Sort into element, compound or mixture: iron, water, air, salt, sea water, carbon dioxide, brass, soil, oxygen.
2. Sodium burns in water; chlorine is a poison gas; together they are table salt. What does this tell you about compounds?
3. Draw the particles in a solid, a liquid and a gas, and explain what happens to each when heated.
4. Why do pipes burst in a frost, and why does ice float? Why does the second fact matter to a pond full of fish?
5. Explain why a wet cloth wrapped round a jug keeps it cool, using the word *evaporation*.
6. How would you separate: sand from water; salt from sea water; iron filings from sand; gravel of two sizes; muddy water into clear water?
7. Design and build a still. Draw it, label it, and say exactly what is left behind and what comes over. What does it not remove?
8. Make red-cabbage indicator. Test six things and record the colours in a table, then put them in order from most acid to most alkaline.
9. Test the soil in three places in your garden. What did you find, and what would you add to an acid soil?
10. Set up the three-nail rusting experiment. Predict the outcome, run it for a week, record it and explain.
11. What are the three parts of the fire triangle? For each of these, say which one is being removed: throwing water on a fire; smothering a pan with a lid; cutting a firebreak; closing the air vent on a stove.
12. Why is a generator or a charcoal brazier used indoors so dangerous, and what exactly kills the person?
13. A log weighing 2 kg burns to 50 g of ash. Has mass been destroyed? Explain where the rest went.
14. Explain the lime cycle: what you start with, what heating does, what adding water does, and what happens over the following months in the wall.
15. Explain in one sentence why soap removes grease, and why that makes hand-washing the most useful medicine there is.
16. Write the safety rules from the top of this lesson from memory, and add one of your own that applies to your own house.

## Answers

1. Elements: iron, oxygen. Compounds: water, salt, carbon dioxide. Mixtures: air, sea water, brass, soil.
2. A compound is a genuinely new substance with its own properties, not a blend of the properties of what went into it. The elements are chemically joined, not mixed.
3. Solid: packed, vibrating in place. Liquid: touching but sliding. Gas: far apart and fast. Heating gives the particles more energy, so they vibrate more, then break free to slide, then break free altogether.
4. Water expands as it freezes, so the ice inside a full pipe pushes outwards until the pipe splits. It floats because that expansion makes it less dense than liquid water. For a pond this is what saves it: ice forms a lid on top instead of sinking, so the water below stays liquid and the fish live.
5. Water evaporating from the cloth takes the fastest-moving particles with it, leaving the rest cooler — and the jug loses heat to the cloth. It works best in dry moving air.
6. Filter; evaporate (or distil if you want the water too); magnet; sieve; let it settle and decant, then filter.
7. Look for: a boiling vessel, a cool surface for the steam to condense on, and a way to catch and lead off the condensate away from the source. Left behind: salts, dirt, heavy metals, most microbes. It does *not* remove substances that boil at or below water's boiling point — some alcohols and light solvents come over with the steam.
8. Check the table has the substances, the colour and the conclusion, and that the order is sensible: lemon and vinegar most acid; water neutral; soap and wood-ash water most alkaline.
9. No fixed answer, but the variation between the three spots is the interesting part. Add lime or wood ash to an acid soil — and not too much at once.
10. Only the nail that has both air and water rusts. The dry one and the one sealed from air do not, showing that rusting needs both.
11. Fuel, oxygen, heat. Water removes heat; a lid removes oxygen; a firebreak removes fuel; the vent removes oxygen.
12. Burning without enough air produces carbon monoxide, which has no colour, taste or smell. It binds to the blood in place of oxygen, so the person suffocates without ever feeling short of breath — often while asleep.
13. No. The carbon and hydrogen left as carbon dioxide and water vapour up the chimney, and those gases have mass. Weigh everything, including the gases and the oxygen taken from the air, and the totals match.
14. Start with limestone or chalk (calcium carbonate). Heating drives off carbon dioxide and leaves quicklime. Adding water slakes it, with great heat, to slaked lime, which is used for mortar and limewash. In the wall it slowly absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and turns back into a form of limestone — which is the mortar setting.
15. One end of a soap molecule holds on to water and the other holds on to grease, so grease can be lifted off and rinsed away — which is why washing your hands physically removes the microbes rather than merely moving them about.
16. Look for: never taste, never heat a sealed container, never mix unknowns, never mix acid with bleach or ammonia, ventilate, protect eyes, tie hair, know where the water is, treat lye and quicklime as burns.
