# Trade, ledgers and keeping records

A group that keeps no records argues about everything, twice. Who has had their share, what was agreed with the neighbours, when the last frost came, who was born and who died. Memory is unreliable, self-serving, and dies with the person. **Paper does not.**

## What to record, from day one

**A log book.** One book, in one place, with a date on every entry. What happened, what was decided, who arrived and who left. Five minutes a day. It is the group's memory and, in time, its history.

**A register of people.** Who is here: name, age, skills, needs, next of kin, and the date they joined. Updated when anyone comes, goes, is born or dies.

**Births and deaths.** Name, date, parents or cause. This sounds bureaucratic. It is the one record that people will come looking for in twenty years, and it is a kindness to the dead and to those who will want to know.

**Agreements.** Anything two parties agree about property, work, or obligation — written, dated, signed by both and a witness, kept by a third. The settlement of almost every dispute is "what does the paper say?"

**An inventory.** What the group holds in common: food by weight, water by litre, fuel, medicine with expiry, tools, seed. Counted on a schedule — weekly for food, monthly for the rest — so a shortage is seen coming rather than discovered.

**The garden and the weather.** Sowing dates, first and last frosts, rain, what yielded, what failed. This is the knowledge that took farmers generations to gather and that no one alive now has, because a shop made it unnecessary. See the science lesson *How to find things out*.

**Medical notes.** Who was ill with what, when, and what was done. Patterns appear — the water source that keeps making people sick, the household with the recurring infection.

## The ledger, when there is no money

Trade does not stop when money does; it goes back to how it was for most of history, which is **credit and account**. Pure barter — my eggs for your nails, right now — is rare and clumsy. What people actually do is keep a tally.

**The community ledger.** A book with a page per household. When A gives B a sack of grain, it is written: date, from, to, what, and an agreed value. B's page shows a debt, A's a credit. Debts are settled over time, in kind, in work, or forgiven at a festival. Nobody carries coins; the book is the money.

The value unit can be anything the group agrees on and everyone has a feel for — an hour of ordinary work, a kilo of grain, a day's ration. **Hours of work** is the fairest and the hardest to hoard.

The ledger keeper is a rotating role, the book is open to all, and two people check it monthly. Trust in the ledger is trust in the group.

## Trading with outsiders

Between groups, credit does not work — there is no shared book and no way to enforce it. There, it is barter, or a physical token.

**What trades well:** salt, seed, nails and wire, fuel, medicine, tools, ammunition, alcohol, skills (a day's carpentry, a delivery), information, and above all **food surplus**, which every group has at some time of year and lacks at another.

**Rules that keep trades honest:**

- Inspect before agreeing. Weigh what is weighed. Open what is sealed.
- Both parties state the deal aloud before it happens, in front of someone.
- Small trades first with a new partner. Trust is built in increments.
- Never trade what you cannot spare for what you do not need.
- A deal written and copied, one copy each, for anything that will take time to complete.

**A market day** — a fixed day and place where neighbouring groups bring surplus — is worth establishing early. It is where trade happens, but also where news, marriages, alliances and disputes between groups get handled. Every human society reinvents it.

## Tokens and money

Sooner or later a group wants something that circulates. The rules are old and simple: the token must be **hard to make, easy to check, and issued by someone trusted**. Salt, nails and ammunition have all served. So have stamped tallies from a trusted ledger keeper, redeemable against the book. Any group that issues more tokens than it can redeem learns about inflation the way every government has.

## Keeping the records safe

The record book is the most important object the group owns, and paper burns.

- **Two copies**, kept in two places. The log is copied out weekly by a second hand.
- **Fireproof and dry.** A metal box, off the floor, away from the stove.
- **The Vault** holds a digital copy — the comms channels are a log of their own, and anything typed here is on the disk — but the paper is the master. Machines fail; see *Making data outlive the hardware*.
- **Write in pencil or permanent ink.** Ballpoint fades; felt-tip runs.
- **Date everything, including the year**, and sign it. A record with no author and no date is gossip.

## The habit

Records are kept by habit or not at all. Five minutes at the end of every day, same book, same place, same person until the rota turns. It feels pointless for the first month. In the first dispute it settles, the first frost it predicts, the first grandchild who wants to know, it repays itself completely.
