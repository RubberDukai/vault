# Organising a group

Every account of a real disaster says the same thing: the people who did well were the ones who were already part of something. A street that knew each other's names, a village with a hall, a family with cousins nearby. The technology in this Vault matters less than whether there is a group to use it.

Groups fail for predictable reasons — nobody decides, one person decides everything, work falls on the same few, grievances fester, and newcomers are either turned away or let in without terms. All of those are solvable, and all of them are far easier to solve on a calm afternoon than in a crisis.

## Start smaller than you think

Three to eight households is a group. Twenty is a village and needs structure. Fifty needs institutions. Do not try to organise a town; organise your street, and let streets federate.

The first step is embarrassingly simple: **know who is there.** Names, skills, needs, what they have. A nurse, a plumber, someone with a well, someone with a wood-burning stove, someone with a large family and someone alone. Write it down. Most streets have never done this, and it is worth more than any generator.

## Deciding

The two failure modes are paralysis and dictatorship. Between them:

- **Small things, anyone decides.** The person doing the job decides how to do it. Nobody votes on how to stack firewood.
- **Medium things, the people affected decide,** quickly, and tell everyone else.
- **Big things — moving, admitting people, allocating scarce food, anything irreversible — the whole group decides,** at a meeting, with time to think.

**Meetings** are short, regular, and have a written list of what was decided. Weekly at first. One person runs it and it is not the same person every month. Anything not decided goes on next week's list; nothing is left hanging with no owner.

**Aim for consent, not consensus.** Consensus means everyone agrees; it takes forever and gives one person a veto. Consent means nobody has a serious objection they can state. Ask "can you live with this?" rather than "do you agree?"

**When it must be fast**, one person decides, and that decision is reviewed at the next meeting. Speed in the moment, accountability afterward.

## Roles, and rotating them

Someone has to keep the water register, run the watch rota, hold the medical supplies, keep the records, coordinate the garden. These are **jobs, not ranks.** Give them names, write down what they involve, and **rotate them** — quarterly, say — so that knowledge spreads, nobody burns out, and nobody becomes indispensable. The person who cannot be replaced is the group's biggest vulnerability.

Two people know every job. When one is ill, the other does it, and when the rota turns, one of them teaches the next.

## Work

Work is shared or the group ends. Not equally — a child and a grandmother do not carry what a fit adult carries — but visibly and fairly. A **rota on the wall** that everyone can see does most of it. The person who does nothing is dealt with early, in private, plainly: what is stopping you, and what can you do instead?

**Everyone contributes something.** The old teach and mind children. Children fetch and carry and feed animals. The ill do what can be done sitting down. Being useful is a psychological necessity as much as an economic one — see *Minds under strain*.

## Disputes

They will happen — over food, work, noise, space, a slight. Most are small and most can be handled by two people talking. For the rest:

- **A third person listens to both sides**, separately, then together. Not to judge, at first; to make sure each has heard the other. Most disputes are two people who have not actually heard each other.
- **Agree on facts before arguing about fault.** What happened, in order, that both accept.
- **Look for the repair, not the verdict.** What would put this right? Often it is an apology and a small restitution, and the matter closes.
- **Write down what was agreed.** A dispute with no record is a dispute that returns.
- **For serious matters — theft, violence — the whole group hears it**, with time to cool first. The sanctions a small group has are: restitution, extra work, loss of a role, and, last, exclusion. Exclusion is grave. It is used when someone is a danger to others and nothing less has worked, and it is decided by everyone, not by one.

The one rule that prevents the most trouble: **no one settles a grievance alone and at night.** Everything waits for daylight and a third person.

## Newcomers

People will arrive. Turning everyone away makes enemies of the desperate and loses skills you need. Taking everyone in breaks the group. So: **terms.**

- Anyone joining is told the rules — the work, the meetings, the way disputes are handled — before they join, and agrees to them.
- A **probation** — a month, say — during which they are part of things but not yet a full member, and can leave or be asked to leave without it being a crisis.
- **Sponsorship**: an existing member vouches for them and helps them settle. Someone is responsible for them.
- **What they bring** is recorded and remains theirs. What is shared is agreed.

And a group that can say no needs to be able to say it kindly and clearly, with directions to somewhere else and a day's food. The way a group treats strangers is the way strangers will treat it.

## Security, without becoming what you fear

A group with something is a group someone may want to take it from. The answer is mostly not weapons:

- **Be known, and be useful.** A group that trades, teaches, and helps its neighbours has neighbours who will help it. Isolation is not safety.
- **Watch, in shifts, especially at night.** Not armed sentries — awake people who will raise a noise. A dog is worth three of them.
- **Do not display.** Full stores are not shown to visitors.
- **Talk first, always.** Almost every threat is a frightened person; a frightened person offered food and a conversation is usually a person who leaves or joins.
- **Agree in advance** what the group will and will not do if it comes to more than that. In advance, calmly, together. Not in the moment.

## The two things that hold it together

**Eating together.** A shared meal — daily, or weekly at least — is the single most effective institution a group can have. It is where information passes, disputes surface early, the lonely are noticed, and the group remembers it is one.

**Marking things.** Births, deaths, harvests, the solstices, the anniversary of the day it started. Rituals are not decoration. They are how a collection of households becomes a people, and a people endures what households do not.
