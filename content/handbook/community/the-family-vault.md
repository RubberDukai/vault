# The family vault

Everything else in this library can be downloaded again by anyone. This chapter is about the things that cannot: the papers, pictures and knowledge that exist only in your household, and that a fire, a flood, a move or a death would take with them.

It is the smallest part of the archive and the only irreplaceable one.

## What belongs in it

**Identity.** Birth certificates, passports, driving licences, marriage and adoption papers, immigration documents. In a functioning world these get you across a border or into a hospital; in a broken one they are proof of who you are to people who have never met you.

**Ownership.** Deeds, tenancy agreements, vehicle documents, insurance policies, receipts for anything valuable. Land disputes are the first thing a recovering society fights about, and paper decides them.

**Money.** Bank and account details, pension records, debts owed to you and by you, tax records. Not for now — for after, when institutions come back and ask for evidence.

**Medical.** Every family member's conditions, medications, allergies, blood group, vaccinations with dates, and any surgery. In a crisis this is read by a stranger; make it read like a chart, not a diary.

**People.** Names, addresses, birthdays and a photograph of everyone who matters — family, friends, the neighbours, the doctor, the people who owe you a favour. Phone numbers for the day phones work again.

**Knowledge that is only yours.** Where the water shut-off is. The password to the machine holding this Vault. The recipe, the way your grandmother did it. The route to the cousins' farm. How to start the generator. Anything that lives in one head and would be lost with it.

**Photographs.** Not all of them — the hundred that matter. Faces, places, the house as it was.

**Letters.** See below.

## Paper first

Every document above is scanned into the digital vault **and** kept on paper. Paper needs nothing to be read. Originals in a **fireproof, waterproof box** — a document safe, or a sealed ammunition tin inside a metal cabinet — off the ground, away from the stove. Copies in a second place: a relative's house, a workplace, a buried cache. Laminate the ones you will handle.

A **one-page summary** on top: who is in the household, the medical essentials, who to contact, where the rest of it is. This is what someone reads in the first five minutes.

## The digital copy

Scan everything at decent resolution as **PDF or JPEG** — formats that will open in thirty years. Name files so a stranger understands them: `birth-certificate-joseph.pdf`, not `scan0042.pdf`.

Put the folder in the Vault's `library/docs` — it will appear on the shelf and be searchable — but **encrypt the sensitive ones** if the machine could be reached by people you would not show them to. An encrypted archive with a strong passphrase, and the passphrase on paper in the fireproof box.

Then three copies: the Vault's disk, a USB stick in the fireproof box, and a stick somewhere else entirely. Refreshed yearly, per *Making data outlive the hardware*. Flash forgets.

## An inventory of what you have

A list, on paper and in the machine, of the physical stores: food by type and weight with dates, water, fuel, medicine with expiry, seed, tools, spare parts, ammunition if any. Updated when things are used or added.

Two reasons. First, so you know when you are short before you are out. Second, so that if you are not there, someone else can find what they need without turning the house over. A stranger should be able to run your household from the inventory and the one-page summary.

## The letter

Write a letter to be opened if you are gone. Not a will — a will is separate, and worth making properly while there are still solicitors — but the things a will does not say.

Where things are. What you would want done. What you know that they do not. What you think matters. Anything you have never got round to saying. It does not have to be long or well written. It has to exist.

Seal it, date it, put it on top of the papers in the box, and tell one person it is there. Rewrite it every few years. Most people never do this and their families spend years wishing they had.

## The Vault's part

Everything above is files, and the Vault will hold files:

- Documents and scans go in `library/docs` and are searchable.
- The inventory can live as a simple text file there, or as a message pinned in a Comms channel called `#stores`, where anyone in the household can update it from any device.
- The people register, likewise, in a channel or a document.
- The log book, if you keep one digitally, is the Comms history — every message stamped with who and when.

But the paper is the master. A hard drive is a thing that will fail; a metal box of paper is a thing that will be found.

## Doing it

Most of this is an afternoon: gather the documents, scan them, write the one-page summary, buy a fireproof box, put it all in. The letter takes an evening and is the part people avoid.

Do the afternoon this week. Do the letter this month. Then put a note in the calendar for a year from now to check the box, refresh the drives, and read the letter to see if it is still true.
