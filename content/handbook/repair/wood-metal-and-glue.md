# Wood, metal and glue

Most broken things are broken in one of four ways: a joint has come apart, a part has snapped, a fixing has pulled out, or something has worn away. Each has a standard repair, and none of them needs electricity.

## Wood

**Green or seasoned.** Freshly cut wood is full of water; it is easy to carve and split but shrinks and warps as it dries. Seasoned wood (a year per 25 mm of thickness, stacked with spacers under cover) is stable and is what you build with. Ash, oak, beech, elm, sycamore for strength; pine and larch for planks and frames; hazel and willow for anything bent; birch and alder for turned and carved things.

**Read the grain.** Wood is a bundle of straws. It is strong along the grain, weak across it, and splits along it. A handle, a lever, a beam must have the grain running its length. A crack that runs with the grain can be glued; one across the grain means the piece is finished.

**The joints that hold.** In rough order of strength, and all cut with a saw and a chisel:

- **Butt joint** — two ends meet, held by nails or screws. Weak; use with a bracket or a block behind.
- **Lap joint** — half the thickness cut from each piece so they overlap flush. Glued and pegged, strong enough for frames and gates.
- **Mortise and tenon** — a tongue on one piece into a slot in the other. The joint of chairs, tables, doors, timber frames. Cut the mortise first, fit the tenon to it, and drive a wooden peg through both, slightly offset so it pulls the joint tight (*draw-boring*).
- **Dovetail** — interlocking wedge-shaped fingers. For boxes and drawers; it cannot pull apart in the direction of the load.
- **Scarf joint** — two long pieces joined end to end with a long sloping overlap. For extending a beam or splicing a broken rail.

A joint that has worked loose is repaired by cleaning out the old glue, gluing, and adding a peg or a wedge; a joint that has broken is recut on a new shoulder if there is length to spare.

**Fixings.** Screws hold better than nails and can be removed. Nails driven at alternating angles (*skew nailing*) hold far better than straight. Pilot-drill hardwood or it splits. A screw hole that has stripped: fill with matchsticks or slivers and glue, let it set, re-drive. A bolt with a washer each side outlasts everything, and can be taken apart.

**Wedges.** The oldest fixing. A tool head is held on its handle by a wedge driven into a saw cut in the end of the handle; a loose head is fixed by soaking the handle end in water overnight (it swells) and driving a new, larger wedge. Do not use a hammer whose head moves.

**Bending.** Steam green ash or hazel for an hour per 25 mm in a pipe over a pot, then bend it round a former and tie it until it dries. This is how chair backs, sledge runners and snowshoes are made.

## Metal, without a forge

You cannot make steel, but you can shape, join and fix it.

**Cutting.** Hacksaw for anything; tin snips for sheet; a cold chisel and hammer for thick bar. Cut on the waste side and file to the line.

**Drilling.** A hand drill or brace with a centre-punch mark first so the bit does not wander. Oil the bit; slow and heavy pressure for steel, fast and light for aluminium and brass.

**Rivets.** A rod through a hole in both pieces, the end hammered over to form a second head. This is how metal was joined before welding, and a riveted joint outlasts a bolted one because it cannot work loose. Use a piece of copper or soft steel rod, cut it to stand 1.5 times its diameter proud, support the factory head on something solid, and round the other end with light hammer blows working round the edge. Pop rivets need only the hand tool and are fine for sheet.

**Threads.** A tap cuts an internal thread in a drilled hole; a die cuts an external one on a rod. A small set of both in the common sizes (M4–M10) means you can make bolts and repair stripped threads for the rest of your life. Oil, half a turn forward, quarter turn back to clear the swarf.

**Soft soldering** joins copper, brass and tinplate with a lead or tin alloy melted by a soldering iron heated in the fire or a gas flame. Clean the metal bright, apply flux, heat the *work* until the solder flows into the joint by itself. It is watertight and electrically sound but not strong.

**Brazing** is the same idea with a brass rod and a much hotter flame (a blowtorch, or a charcoal fire with air blown into it) and is strong enough to repair a bicycle frame, a bucket handle or a broken tool. Silver solder is brazing with a lower-melting rod and is the mender's best friend.

**Welding** joins steel to steel by melting both. An arc welder needs mains or a generator; a car battery, thick cables and a coat-hanger rod will strike an arc in an emergency and make an ugly, usable weld. Never look at an arc without a proper dark lens — the eyes burn in seconds and you find out that night.

**Cold shaping.** Mild steel bends cold in a vice up to about 6 mm thick. Heat it to red in a charcoal fire with air blown through and it bends like putty; hammer it on any heavy lump of iron. Quench in water to harden, then heat gently to a straw colour and quench again to temper, or it will be brittle. This is smithing, and it is learnable with an afternoon and a bucket of charcoal.

**Rust** is covered in *Tools and keeping an edge*: keep it dry, keep it oiled, paint what lives outside.

## Glue

Glue is a joint's third hand. None of it is as strong as a mechanical joint, and all of it needs clean, dry, well-fitting, clamped surfaces.

| Glue | Sticks | Doesn't | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PVA** (white wood glue) | wood, paper, cloth | metal, plastic, anything wet | Clamp an hour, cure a day. Waterproof grades exist. The everyday glue |
| **Epoxy** (two-part) | almost anything to almost anything | flexible things | Mix equal parts, work in five minutes, cured overnight. Fills gaps. The repair glue |
| **Cyanoacrylate** (superglue) | small, tight, rigid joints; skin | gaps, shock loads, wet | Instant, brittle. Sprinkle bicarbonate of soda on it to fill and set instantly |
| **Contact adhesive** | rubber, leather, laminate, sheet | rigid butt joints | Coat both sides, wait until touch-dry, press once — no second chance |
| **Hot-melt** | quick jigs, packaging | anything that gets warm or loaded | Needs power for the gun |
| **Hide glue** | wood, especially instruments | outdoors | Made from skin and bone scraps simmered for a day; reversible with heat and water. What every antique is held together with |
| **Pitch / resin** | hafting stone and metal to wood | — | Pine resin, beeswax and charcoal dust melted together. Stone-age, works |

Store glue sealed, cool and out of the light; most of it dies in a year once opened. When it runs out, hide glue and pitch are what you can make.

## The repair, in order

1. Find out *why* it broke. A repair that ignores the cause breaks the same way next week.
2. Take it apart as far as the break and no further. Photograph or sketch it.
3. Clean every surface that will be joined.
4. Choose the strongest repair you can manage, not the quickest. A mechanical joint over glue, glue over tape.
5. Clamp it, leave it alone for the full curing time, and test it gently before trusting it.
6. Put back one fixing more than it had.

A thing mended well is often stronger than it was.
