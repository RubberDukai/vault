# Cloth, rope and leather

Clothes wear out and no shop replaces them. Rope frays and holds up everything from a tarp to a bucket in a well. Leather is boots, belts, straps and hinges. These are the materials of a working life, and the skills to keep them going are old, quick to learn, and mostly done sitting down in the evening.

## Sewing and mending

**A sewing kit** is one of the highest-value, lowest-weight things you can own: needles in several sizes (including a couple of heavy sailmaker's needles and curved ones), strong thread (linen, polyester, and a spool of button thread), pins, scissors, a thimble, a seam ripper, safety pins, buttons, a tape measure, and a few metres of cloth. It fits in a tin.

**The stitches that do everything:**

- **Running stitch** — in and out, evenly. Fast, for seams that do not take strain.
- **Backstitch** — each stitch goes back to meet the last. Strong, for seams that do. This is the hand-sewn equivalent of a machine seam.
- **Whipstitch / overcast** — over the edge, for hems, raw edges and joining two edges.
- **Blanket stitch** — a looped edge stitch that stops fraying and reinforces.
- **Ladder stitch** — invisible closing of a seam from the outside.

Learn these five and you can make or mend any garment.

**Patching.** A patch on the **inside** for neatness, on the **outside** for strength (knees, elbows, seats). Cut it larger than the hole by a finger's width all round, turn the edges under, pin, and backstitch round the edge, then round the hole. A patch on a patch is fine. Clothes were patched for centuries and nobody thought anything of it.

**Darning** — for holes in knitted things — is weaving with a needle: rows of thread across the hole one way, then the other way over and under them. A darning egg or a smooth stone inside the sock holds it in shape.

**Buttons, zips, elastic.** Sew buttons on with a shank — a few thread wraps under the button — so they sit up and do not tear the cloth. A broken zip is replaced or, more practically, the garment is converted to buttons. Elastic is impossible to make; hoard it, and learn drawstrings.

**Making clothes** is a bigger subject, but a tunic, trousers with a drawstring, a simple dress, an apron and mittens are all rectangles and can be cut from a sheet, a blanket or a curtain with a tape measure and common sense. Wikibooks and the old sewing manuals cover the rest.

## Wool

Fleece to yarn to cloth is the whole textile chain, and it can be done with equipment made from sticks. **Spinning** on a drop spindle — a weighted stick that twists fibre as it hangs — is learnable in an afternoon and produces usable yarn. **Knitting** on two sticks, and **weaving** on a frame loom, turn it into cloth. Slow, but it needs nothing from outside, and wool is warm wet.

## Rope and cordage

Rope holds up the tarp, hauls the log, moors the boat, lowers the bucket and ties the load. It is consumed constantly and it can be made.

**Buy now:** paracord (hundreds of metres, endlessly useful, the inner strands are thread), 6 mm and 10 mm polyester or nylon rope, natural fibre rope for anything that will get hot, bailing twine by the roll, and wire.

**Making it.** Any long, strong fibre twists into cord: nettle stems (retted — soaked and dried until the fibre separates — then stripped), flax, bramble bark, willow bark, lime bark, dried grass and rushes for coarse work, and strips of any cloth or plastic. The method is **reverse twist**: twist two bundles of fibre each in one direction, then lay them together twisting the opposite way, adding fibre as each bundle thins. The opposing twists lock. A child can do it; it is the oldest technology there is.

**Care.** Keep rope dry, out of the sun, coiled, and away from sharp edges and acids. Inspect before trusting a life to it. Whip or melt the ends so they do not unlay.

## Knots — the eight that matter

A knot that slips is worse than none. Learn these until you can tie them behind your back.

1. **Overhand** — the simplest stopper.
2. **Figure of eight** — a better stopper; will not jam.
3. **Reef knot** — joins two ends of the *same* rope round something (a parcel, a bandage). Not for joining two ropes under load; it slips.
4. **Sheet bend** — joins two ropes, including of different sizes. Also the knot nets are made with.
5. **Bowline** — a fixed loop that will not slip or jam, and unties easily after load. The rescue loop, the mooring loop, the everything loop.
6. **Clove hitch** — fastens a rope to a post or pole quickly. Adjustable, and it can roll under a shifting load; back it up.
7. **Round turn and two half hitches** — fastens a rope to a ring or post securely under any load. The one for tying a boat or a tarp.
8. **Trucker's hitch** — a pulley made of rope, for tensioning a load or a ridgeline tight. Tie a loop in the standing part, pass the end round the anchor and back through the loop, haul, and lock off with two half hitches.

Plus one **lashing** — the square lashing, for joining two poles at right angles — and you can build a frame, a tripod, a raft, a ladder.

The Boy Scouts Handbook on the shelf of this library has drawings of all of these. Practise with a metre of cord while you read.

## Leather

Boots, belts, straps, sheaths, hinges, bellows, harness. Leather is repaired with an **awl** to punch holes, a **heavy needle** or two, and **waxed linen thread**, using the **saddle stitch** — two needles on one thread, passing through each hole from opposite sides, which does not unravel when a stitch wears through.

**Boots** are the item to keep going: resole with a cut sheet of leather or old tyre glued and stitched, patch the uppers, replace laces with cord, and **grease** them — tallow, beeswax, dubbin, any animal fat — regularly so they stay supple and shed water. Dry boots slowly, never by the fire; heat cracks leather.

**Tanning** a skin into leather is a real project: scrape the flesh side clean, remove the hair with a lime or wood-ash soak, and then either **bark-tan** (months in a tea of oak or willow bark) or **brain-tan** (worked with an emulsion of the animal's own brain, then smoked). Both are old, both work, and both are described in the woodcraft books on the shelf. Rawhide — a scraped, dried, untanned skin — is stiff, immensely strong, and makes lashings, drum heads and hinges with no tanning at all.

## The habit

Mend it the day it tears. A small hole is a five-minute job; the same hole a month later is a new garment. Keep the kit where you sit in the evening, and let mending be what the hands do while the talk goes on. That is how it was always done, and it is not a bad way to spend an evening.
