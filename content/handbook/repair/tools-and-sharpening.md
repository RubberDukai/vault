# Tools, and keeping an edge on them

Power tools stop with the power. Hand tools were doing every job on earth two hundred years ago and will do them again. A good hand tool, looked after, lasts several lifetimes; the skill is in the looking after, and most of that is **keeping it sharp and keeping it dry.**

## The tools that do everything

Buy old and good rather than new and cheap. Second-hand tools from before about 1980 are usually better steel than anything sold in a shed today, and cost less.

**Cutting.** A fixed-blade knife with a full tang · a folding knife · an axe (a small one for splitting kindling, a felling axe if you have woodland) · a bow saw and spare blades · a hand saw for planks · a hacksaw and blades · a pair of secateurs · a pair of tin snips · a good pair of scissors.

**Holding and turning.** Claw hammer · club hammer · adjustable spanners in two sizes · a socket set · combination pliers · long-nose pliers · mole grips · a set of screwdrivers · a brace or hand drill and bits · a vice, bolted to something solid.

**Measuring and marking.** Tape measure · steel rule · square · spirit level · a plumb line, which is a string and a weight.

**Shaping.** A plane · a set of chisels · a rasp and files · a spokeshave · a drawknife if you work green wood.

**Digging.** A spade, a fork, a mattock, a pick. These four move the earth.

**Sharpening.** See below. Without this the rest goes blunt and stays blunt.

**Fixings.** Nails, screws, bolts, washers, wire, in every size you can afford. These are the things that will be impossible to make and easy to run out of.

## Why sharp matters

A dull edge needs force. Force means the tool slips, the cut wanders, and the hand behind it ends up in the way. **Sharp tools cut what you aim at with the pressure you intend; dull ones cut you.** Every experienced hand agrees, and every casualty ward confirms it.

A sharp edge also does the work in half the time and half the fatigue, and leaves a clean cut that heals — on wood, on a plant, on an animal.

## Sharpening anything

The principle is the same for a knife, an axe, a chisel and a scythe: **grind two flat faces until they meet in a line, then polish that line.**

**What you need:**
- A **coarse stone** to reshape a damaged edge.
- A **medium stone** for ordinary sharpening.
- A **fine stone** to finish.
- A **strop** — a strip of leather glued to a board, rubbed with polishing compound or just clean — to align the edge after the stone.
- Water or oil for the stone, depending on its kind. Never mix them on one stone.

Natural stones, diamond plates, ceramic rods and a flat concrete step will all work. A good stone lasts forever.

**The method, for a knife:**

1. Hold the blade at the angle of its existing bevel — usually **about 20°** for a knife, 25–30° for an axe, 25° for a chisel. Rest the blade flat on the stone, then lift the spine until the bevel sits on the stone. Hold that angle. Consistency matters more than the exact number.
2. Push the edge across the stone as if slicing a thin layer off it, heel to tip, using the whole stone. Ten strokes.
3. Feel for a **burr** — a tiny wire of metal — on the opposite side, by drawing a thumb *across* the edge (never along it). When the burr runs the whole length, that side is done.
4. Turn over. Repeat until the burr moves to the first side.
5. Alternate, a few strokes each side, reducing pressure, on the medium then the fine stone, until the burr is gone.
6. **Strop**: draw the edge *backwards* along the leather — spine leading — ten times each side. This straightens the last microscopic teeth and makes the difference between sharp and shaving sharp.

**Test:** it should slice paper cleanly, shave hair from your arm, or bite into a fingernail without sliding when rested at a shallow angle.

**An axe** is done with a round stone or a file in a circular motion along the edge, working from both sides, then stropped. Keep the profile convex — a fat, strong edge — not a thin knife edge that chips in wood.

**Chisels and plane irons** are ground on one side only (the bevel) with the back kept dead flat on the stone. A flat back is what makes the edge true.

**A saw** is sharpened with a triangular file, one stroke per tooth, at the angle the teeth already have, and then the teeth are **set** — bent alternately left and right — with a saw set or a punch, so the cut is wider than the blade and it does not bind. Fiddly, learnable, and it turns a useless blunt saw into a working one.

**Scythes and sickles** are peened — the edge hammered thin on a small anvil — then honed with a stone every few minutes of mowing. An old skill, and the only way to cut a field without fuel.

## Rust

Rust is iron returning to ore. It is slow if the tool is dry and fast if it is not.

- **Dry after use**, always, before it goes away.
- **Oil the metal** lightly — any oil: linseed, vegetable, engine, gun. A rag in a tin, wiped over.
- **Store dry and off the floor.** A damp shed floor rusts a tool from the bottom up in a winter.
- **Remove rust** with a wire brush, sandpaper, or a soak in vinegar overnight, then oil at once.
- **Wooden handles** get linseed oil once a year. A dry handle cracks; a cracked handle lets the head fly.

A loose axe head is refixed by driving the head down the handle, hammering a fresh wooden wedge into the top, and soaking the head overnight in water or oil to swell the wood.

## Making handles

Handles break, and a tool with no handle is a lump of iron. **Ash** is the wood — straight-grained, strong, springy. Split, never sawn, so the grain runs the length of it; shaped with a drawknife and spokeshave; seasoned a few months if you can wait, fitted green if you cannot. Learn to hang an axe and to fit a hammer head, and a box of tool heads found in a barn becomes a toolkit.

## The habit

Tools are cared for at the end of every day they are used: wiped, dried, oiled, edges touched up on the stone, hung in their place. Ten minutes. A tool put away wet and blunt is a tool that will be wet, blunt and rusty when it is next needed, which is always in a hurry.
