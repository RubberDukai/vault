# Bicycles, plumbing and the house

Everything in this chapter is a job a competent person did for themselves a hundred years ago. None of it needs a specialist; all of it needs the right small part, so the last section is a list of what to keep.

## The bicycle

Without fuel, the bicycle is the fastest thing you own. It needs almost nothing and everything it needs is small.

**Puncture.** Take the wheel out (undo the quick release or the two axle nuts; on the back wheel, shift to the smallest sprocket first and pull the derailleur back). Let the last air out, push one side of the tyre off the rim with tyre levers, pull the tube out. Pump it up and listen or dunk it to find the hole; **then run your fingers round the inside of the tyre** to find the thorn or glass that made it — miss this and the new tube goes the same way. Roughen the tube, thin coat of rubber solution, wait until it is dull and dry, press the patch on hard for a minute. Fit the tube slightly inflated, work the tyre back on with thumbs (levers pinch tubes), check the tube is not trapped under the bead, and pump to the pressure on the tyre wall. A tyre with a cut can be booted from the inside with a piece of old tyre or a folded banknote.

**Chain.** Clean and oil it every few weeks; a dry chain wears the sprockets, which are the expensive part. A broken chain is fixed with a chain tool: push a pin out, remove the damaged links, push the pin back in (or fit a quick link). A chain that skips under load is worn out; measure twelve links — if they span more than 308 mm the chain is done and the sprockets soon will be.

**Brakes.** Rim brakes: the cable stretches, so take up the slack with the barrel adjuster, then at the cable clamp. Replace blocks when the grooves are gone. A frayed cable is a brake failure waiting; carry a spare. Keep the rims clean and unbent — a buckled wheel is trued by tightening the spokes on the side opposite the bulge, a quarter turn at a time.

**Gears** that will not shift are almost always cable stretch: turn the barrel adjuster at the derailleur a quarter turn at a time. Bent hanger: bend it back gently.

**Bearings** in the hubs, bottom bracket and headset want grease once a year. Play in a wheel (rock it side to side) means the cones need tightening; grinding means the grease is gone.

Keep: tubes, patches and solution, tyre levers, pump, spare chain and quick links, brake blocks, brake and gear cables, spokes, grease, oil, a multi-tool with the hex keys, a chain tool, a spoke key.

## Plumbing

Water in the wrong place rots a house faster than anything but fire. First: **know where the stopcock is** — usually under the kitchen sink or where the main comes into the house — and check it turns. Then find the isolating valves on each appliance and tap.

**Dripping tap.** Turn off the water, open the tap to drain it, put the plug in the sink (small parts fall down plugholes), unscrew the head. Traditional tap: replace the rubber washer on the end of the spindle; if it still drips the brass seat is scored and needs a reseating tool or grinding paste. Modern lever tap: a ceramic cartridge, replaced as a unit. Reassemble with plumber's tape on any thread.

**Leaking joint.** Compression joint: tighten the nut a quarter turn; if that fails, undo it, and replace the olive (the brass ring) with a smear of jointing compound. Push-fit: pull off with the release collar, check the pipe end is cut square and unscratched, push back on to the depth mark. Soldered copper: drain fully, dry the pipe completely (a leaking joint will not solder — stuff bread in the pipe to hold back drips), clean bright, flux, heat the joint until solder wicks in.

**Burst pipe.** Stopcock off, taps open to drain. Temporary: a length of hose or rubber sheet wrapped round the split and clamped with jubilee clips, or a proprietary pipe-repair clamp, holds for weeks. Permanent: cut out the split and join in a new piece with two compression or push-fit couplings. Pipes burst because they froze: lag every pipe in the loft and outside, and leave a trickle running through a hard frost.

**Blocked drain.** Sink: a plunger, then undo the trap under the sink (bucket beneath), clear it, refit. Never pour fat down a sink. Outside drain: rods, turned clockwise only or they unscrew underground. Toilet: a bucket of water poured fast from height, then a plunger.

**Toilet that keeps running** is the float valve or the flush valve washer; both are cheap parts that lift out. A toilet with no mains water is flushed with a bucket of any water poured quickly into the pan.

**Hot water cylinder, boilers, gas.** Gas work is for someone trained, and the danger is real: if you smell gas, turn it off at the meter, open windows, no flames, no switches. An immersion heater element can be swapped by a careful person with the power isolated at the fuse box.

Keep: plumber's tape, washers in the common sizes, olives, a few push-fit couplings and stop-ends in 15 and 22 mm, a pipe cutter, a repair clamp, two adjustable spanners, a plunger, drain rods, jointing compound, silicone sealant.

## Doors and windows

**A door that sticks** has either dropped on its hinges or swelled with damp. Look at the gap: wide at the top on the latch side means the hinges are loose — take out the screws one at a time, plug the holes with glued matchsticks, re-drive. Even gap but tight: plane the edge, take off the paint first, and repaint the bare wood or it will swell again. A door that will not latch: the keep on the frame is out of line — look at the marks the latch leaves, move the keep or file it.

**A broken pane.** Wear gloves and eye protection. Pull out the broken glass, chisel out the old putty and the sprigs (the small nails) or the beading. Measure the opening and cut the glass 3 mm smaller each way: lay it on a flat surface, score once firmly with a glass cutter along a straight edge, tap under the score and snap. Bed the pane on a thin layer of putty, press it in, tap in sprigs every 150 mm, then face-putty at 45° with a putty knife. Paint after a fortnight. No glass: a board, or heavy clear plastic tacked on with battens, keeps the weather out until there is.

**A window that will not open** on a sash is usually painted shut; run a knife round the joint. A broken sash cord is replaced by taking off the staff bead, lifting out the sash, opening the pocket in the frame, and threading a new cord over the pulley to the weight.

**Draughts.** The cheapest heating you will ever install: foam or brush strip round doors and windows, a brush strip on the letterbox, curtains that reach the floor, a rolled towel at the door foot.

## The roof

Water comes in at the joints: chimneys, valleys, and where a slate or tile has slipped. From inside the loft on a rainy day you can see exactly where. A slipped slate is refixed with a strip of lead or copper (a *tingle*) nailed to the batten and bent up over the slate's bottom edge; a cracked tile is lifted out by pushing up the ones above it and a new one slid in. Flashing that has lifted is pressed back and pointed with mortar or sealed with flashing tape. Clear gutters every autumn — a blocked gutter soaks a wall and rots the joist ends behind it. Ladders on a roof: tied at the top, footed at the bottom, never in wind.

## Small engines and generators

A petrol engine needs three things: **fuel, spark, air**. When it will not start, check them in that order.

- **Fuel:** is there any, is the tap on, is it fresh? Petrol goes stale in a few months and stale petrol is the commonest cause of a dead mower or generator. Drain it, fill fresh. Is the carburettor bowl full — undo the drain screw. Gummed carburettor: take it off, take it apart on a clean tray, blow through every jet, refit.
- **Spark:** take the plug out, clean and gap it (a business card thickness is close for most), hold the body to the engine and pull the cord — a blue spark should jump. No spark: new plug, then the lead, then the kill switch.
- **Air:** clean the air filter. A blocked filter floods the engine.

Change the oil every 25 hours or every season; an engine run low on oil is scrap. Store for winter with the fuel drained or with stabiliser in it, and pull the cord until the piston is at the top of its stroke to keep the valves shut. Diesel engines are simpler — no spark — but hate air in the fuel line: bleed it at the injector pump after running dry.

A generator is a small engine turning an alternator. Run it outside, well away from windows, never in a garage — its exhaust is carbon monoxide — and never feed it into a wall socket, which sends current back up the street and kills line workers (see *Solar and batteries*). the engine side is above, and the electrical side fails mostly at the brushes and the capacitor, both replaceable.

Keep: spark plugs, air filters, oil, fuel stabiliser, a plug spanner, a small can of carburettor cleaner, a feeler gauge.

## The shed list

The repairs above use, between them, a small box of parts that cannot be made and cost little now. Buy two of each and write the date on the box:

washers and olives · push-fit couplings · plumber's tape and sealant · hinges and screws in three sizes · sash cord · putty and a glass cutter · a sheet of glass or heavy plastic · roofing nails and lead strip · draught strip · inner tubes and patches · chain and cables · spark plugs and filters · fuses in every rating the house uses · a roll of wire · duct tape and cable ties · epoxy.

The habit that matters more than any of it: fix things the week they break, while they are still small.
