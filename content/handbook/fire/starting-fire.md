# Starting a fire

Fire needs three things at once: **fuel, oxygen and heat**. Remove any one and it stops. Almost every failed fire is a failure of one of two things — wet or insufficient tinder, or not enough air.

## The ladder

You cannot light a log with a spark. You climb a ladder of sizes, and each step must be burning well before you add the next.

1. **Tinder** — takes a spark. Dry, fine, fluffy: birch bark, dry grass, cattail fluff, cramp ball fungus, char cloth, cotton wool, feather sticks, dryer lint, teased-out rope.
2. **Kindling** — matchstick to pencil thickness. Dead twigs snapped from standing trees, not picked off the wet ground.
3. **Small fuel** — thumb to wrist thickness.
4. **Fuel** — wrist thickness and up.

Gather **three times as much as you think you need**, and gather it all *before* you strike anything. The commonest failure in the field is a good flame that dies while you go looking for the next size up.

## Methods, in order of reliability

**Lighter.** Keep several. They fail wet, in cold, and when the fuel runs out, but nothing is faster. A lighter with no fuel still throws sparks from the flint — do not throw it away.

**Ferrocerium rod.** A steel rod throwing ~3,000 °C sparks. Works soaking wet, works in wind, lasts for thousands of strikes, does not expire. Press the striker against the rod and **pull the rod back** rather than scraping the striker forward — you scatter your tinder pile otherwise. This is the one to own.

**Matches.** Storm-proof ones in a waterproof container. Finite, but simple and reliable.

**Battery and steel wool.** Touch a 9 V battery across **0000-grade steel wool**. It glows immediately. Any battery works with fine enough wire; a car battery and a wire will start a fire dramatically. Store the two apart — this method works just as well by accident inside a rucksack.

**Lens.** A magnifying glass, a camera lens, spectacles, a clear plastic bottle filled with water, or a polished can bottom with chocolate or toothpaste as the polish. Needs bright direct sun, and dark, fine tinder. Free and unlimited.

**Friction — the bow drill.** The one that always works and never works easily. A spindle spun by a bow against a fireboard, throwing hot dust into a notch until it coalesces into an ember, which is transferred into a tinder bundle and blown to flame. Softwood on softwood: willow, cedar, hazel, lime. Expect to fail for hours the first time. Learn it now, in comfort, not later, in need.

## Building it

- **Platform first.** Never build on wet ground or snow — lay a bed of dry sticks. The ground will steal your heat and drown your coals.
- **Teepee** for fast heat and light: kindling leaned around the tinder, air drawn in at the base.
- **Log cabin** for a stable cooking bed of coals.
- **Long fire** — two parallel logs — for cooking with pots across the gap.
- **Dakota fire hole** — two connected holes, one for fire, one for air. Nearly smokeless, hidden, wind-proof, very efficient with fuel.

## Wet weather

- **Standing dead wood is dry inside.** Wood off the ground is wet through. Snap dead branches from the trunks of standing trees.
- **Split wood** to reach the dry heart. Feather the split faces into curls.
- **Birch bark burns wet.** So do resinous conifer knots and pine sap.
- Build under cover, and shelter the flame with your body until it is established.

## Keeping it

Banking a fire is easier than lighting one. Rake the coals together, cover with ash, and in the morning uncover, add fine tinder and blow. A well-banked fire holds overnight and sometimes far longer.

## Safety, which is the part people skip

> **Warning** **Carbon monoxide is colourless, odourless and kills people in their sleep.** Never burn charcoal indoors — not in a tent, a garage, a shed or a room with the window open. Charcoal in an enclosed space is one of the most reliable ways to die in a crisis and it happens in every disaster, every time, to sensible people. Any indoor burning needs a real flue to the outside and permanent ventilation.

- Clear a metre of bare ground around the fire, and mind overhanging branches and roots — peat and root systems carry fire underground and it can resurface days later.
- Never leave it unattended; never sleep with an open flame in a shelter.
- Wet ground under fire can throw steam explosions; river stones with trapped water can crack violently. Use dry stones.
- Keep water or soil to hand before you light anything.
