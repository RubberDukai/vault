# Cooking without a kitchen

Cooking on an open fire once is an adventure. Cooking on one three times a day for a family, in the rain, for months, is a logistics problem. It comes down to three things: **a fire you can control, a way to hold pots over it, and not wasting the heat.**

## Cook on coals, not flames

Flames are for boiling water and for light. **Everything else cooks better on a bed of glowing coals.** Flames are uneven and sooty and scorch the outside of food while the inside stays raw; coals give steady, even, controllable heat.

Let the fire burn down for 30–45 minutes before you cook. Rake coals to one side for a cooking bed and keep the fire going on the other to make more. Hardwood makes better coals than softwood, and lasts.

**Judge the heat with your hand** held where the food will sit: if you must pull away in 2 seconds it is very hot (searing), 4 seconds is hot (frying, grilling), 6 is medium (most cooking), 8 or more is low (simmering, baking).

## Holding the pot

- **Three stones** or bricks of equal height, pot on top, fire between. The oldest method. Works.
- **A tripod** of green poles lashed at the top, with a chain or wire and an S-hook. Raise or lower the pot to control heat — this is the single most useful cooking rig for a long fire.
- **A crane**: an upright post with a swinging horizontal arm, so the pot swings over the fire and away from it.
- **A grate** — an oven shelf, a barbecue grill, expanded metal — on stones or bricks. Flat, stable, fast to set up.
- **A trench fire**: a narrow slot in the ground, fire in it, pots across the top. Wind-proof, and the pots sit stably.

Anything cast iron will outlive you. A **Dutch oven** — a heavy lidded cast pot with a rimmed lid — is the most versatile single cooking vessel there is: it boils, stews, fries, and with coals heaped on the lid it bakes. If you buy one thing, buy this.

## Baking without an oven

**Dutch oven baking:** bread or a stew inside, the pot sitting on a thin layer of coals, and **twice as many coals on the lid as underneath** — heat rises, and the top needs more. Turn the pot a quarter turn every ten minutes for even heat. A loaf takes 35–45 minutes.

**Bread on a stick:** a stiff dough wound in a spiral round a green stick, held over coals and turned. Bannock — flour, water, a pinch of salt, baking powder if you have it — is the same dough flattened and cooked in a dry pan or on a hot stone.

**In the ashes:** potatoes, onions, squash, fish or meat wrapped in wet leaves or clay, buried in hot ash and coals. Slow, foolproof, and it needs no pot at all.

**A clay oven** — a dome of cob built on a plinth, fired from inside until the walls are hot, then the fire raked out and the food put in. It holds heat for hours; bread first, then a stew, then something slow overnight. Every village once had one, and it is a day's work with mud and straw.

**A cardboard box oven:** a box lined with foil, a rack inside on bricks, coals in a tin underneath. Genuinely bakes.

## Not wasting the heat

Fuel is the constraint. Every technique that cuts fuel use is worth more than any recipe.

- **Lids on everything.** Halves the fuel for boiling.
- **Soak grains and pulses** overnight. Halves the cooking time.
- **The hay box:** bring a stew to the boil, seal the pot inside a box packed with hay, straw, blankets or wool, and walk away. It is still cooking four hours later on the heat it had. Beans, grains, stews and porridge want nothing more.
- **Cook in batches.** One big pot of stew for two days costs less fuel than four small ones.
- **A rocket stove** for everything that is not a whole-fire job. See **Stoves, heat and fuel**. It boils a litre on a handful of twigs.
- **Cook while the fire is doing something else** — heating water, warming the room, drying clothes.

## Food safety with no fridge

This is where people get ill, and in a world with no clinic it matters more than usual.

- **Cook thoroughly**, especially meat and anything from a bird. Juices run clear; the middle is steaming hot.
- **Eat it or preserve it.** Cooked food left warm for more than **two hours** is growing bacteria. In summer, one hour. Reheating does not make it safe again for everything — some toxins survive boiling.
- **Cool fast** if you are keeping something: a small pot in a bowl of cold water, then somewhere cold. A big pot cooling slowly in a warm room is a culture medium.
- **Cold storage without power:** a north-facing outhouse, a hole in the ground, a stream, a well shaft, a **zeer pot** (a clay pot inside a larger one with wet sand between, in a breeze — evaporation keeps it cool). Or a proper root cellar.
- **Boil rice and cool it fast** or eat it at once. Cooked rice left out breeds *Bacillus cereus*, which causes violent vomiting and is a classic camp illness.
- **Separate raw meat** from everything else — its own board, its own knife, hands washed after.
- **Wash produce** in clean water, particularly anything that grew at ground level.

## The kit worth having

A Dutch oven · a large pot with a lid · a frying pan · a kettle or billy can · a tripod and chain · a grate · a long-handled spoon and tongs · a good knife and a board · oven gloves or thick leather · a fire blanket · a food thermometer if you can get one · sealed tins for flour, salt, oil and sugar.

And the knowledge of **how to make salt, oil and flour last**, because the recipe does not matter if the ingredients are gone. See **Preserving food**.
