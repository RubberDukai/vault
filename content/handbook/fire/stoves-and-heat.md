# Stoves, heat and fuel

An open fire delivers perhaps 5–15% of its heat into a cooking pot. A well-built rocket stove reaches 25–40%. When every stick of wood has to be gathered by hand, that difference decides how much of your day is spent collecting fuel.

## The rocket stove

The principle: **burn the tips of a few sticks in a small, insulated, well-drawing chamber, and force the hot gas past the pot.**

Its parts, and why each exists:

- **A short horizontal fuel tunnel** where sticks are fed in at floor level.
- **A shelf** in that tunnel, so sticks rest above the airflow and air passes *underneath* them. This is the detail people leave out and it is the one that makes it work.
- **A vertical insulated chimney** above the join, roughly **three times the height of the combustion chamber**. This creates the draw that gives the stove its name.
- **Insulation around the chamber** — wood ash, vermiculite, perlite, pumice. Not mass. A heavy mass chamber steals heat and the fire burns dirty.
- **A tight gap between the chimney top and the pot**, so hot gas scrapes past the pot sides rather than escaping around it. A pot skirt improves this further.

Built from a large tin, an insulated inner can and ash packed between them, it costs nothing and boils a litre of water on a handful of twigs.

Signs you built it right: almost no smoke once it is up to temperature, a clear roaring draw, and flame that pulls sideways into the chimney rather than licking out of the feed tunnel.

## Heating a space

**Never heat a living space with an unvented flame.** See the warning below.

- **Heat the person, not the room.** Clothing, blankets, a hot stone or bottle in the bed, and a small insulated sleeping area inside a large cold one. This is an order of magnitude cheaper in fuel than heating air.
- **Shrink the space.** Close off rooms, hang blankets across doorways and in front of windows, and live in one small room.
- **Thermal mass.** Stone, brick or a water barrel near the stove absorbs heat while the fire burns and releases it for hours after. A masonry stove is this principle taken seriously: burn hot and fast, store the heat in mass, radiate gently for a day.
- **Insulate downward.** More heat is lost through an uninsulated floor and through the ceiling than most people expect. Straw, leaves, cardboard, carpet, anything with trapped air.

> **Warning** **Carbon monoxide.** Any combustion in an enclosed space produces it, and it is undetectable without an alarm. Charcoal and camping stoves indoors kill people in every cold-weather crisis, in every country, every year. If you must burn indoors, use a stove with a proper flue to the outside, keep a permanent ventilation opening that cannot be blocked, and get a battery carbon monoxide alarm now while they are still purchasable. Keep spare batteries. Symptoms — headache, nausea, confusion, drowsiness — are easily mistaken for cold and tiredness, and the confusion stops people acting on them. **If more than one person in a room has a headache at the same time, or the alarm sounds: everyone out into fresh air at once, doors and windows open, the fire out, and nobody back inside until the source is found.** Anyone who is confused or drowsy needs fresh air and rest for hours, and a doctor if there is one — the damage continues after the exposure stops.

## Fuel

**Seasoned wood** — cut, split, stacked off the ground under cover, and given six to twelve months — carries roughly 20% moisture. Green wood is 50% or more, and much of your fire's energy goes into boiling water out of it instead of heating you. Season a year ahead. Always be a year ahead.

Rough energy ranking per volume: dense hardwoods (oak, ash, beech, hornbeam) > softwoods (pine, spruce) > dried dung > straw and grass. Ash burns well even relatively green, which is why it has a reputation.

**Charcoal** is wood that has been heated in the near-absence of air until the volatiles are driven off. It burns hotter, lighter and almost smokelessly, which makes it the right fuel for forging and for carrying. It is made in a pit or a sealed drum with a small vent: load with wood, light, let it burn until the smoke turns from white (steam) to thin blue (volatiles gone), then seal completely and let it cool for a day. You lose around 70% of the mass and keep most of the energy density.

**Other fuels:** dried dung (a mainstay across much of the world), straw briquettes, paper bricks made from soaked and pressed waste paper, and vegetable oil in a wick stove.

## Cooking with less fuel

- **A lid.** Doubles the efficiency of boiling anything. This is the cheapest improvement available.
- **A hay box / retained-heat cooker.** Bring to the boil for a few minutes, then seal the pot inside a heavily insulated box of hay, wool or blankets. It carries on cooking for hours on stored heat. Superb for grains, beans and stews, and it cannot burn.
- **Soak beans and grains** beforehand — it cuts cooking time by half or more.
- **Cut food small.** Surface area is cooking speed.
- **Solar cooker.** A reflective parabola or a simple insulated box with a glass lid reaches 120–150 °C on a clear day. Slow, free, and it uses no fuel at all — which on a sunny day is the whole point.
