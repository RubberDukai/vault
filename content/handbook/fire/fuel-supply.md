# Fuel — making it, storing it, not running out

A household cooking and heating on wood through a British winter burns something like **four to six tonnes** of seasoned firewood a year. That is a serious amount of gathering, cutting, splitting and stacking, and it has to be done **a year ahead**, because wood cut today is not fit to burn until next year.

Fuel is the hidden job that makes every other job possible. Plan it like food.

## The wood year

**Cut in winter**, when the sap is down and the wood is drier to begin with and lighter to move.

**Split immediately.** Wood dries from the cut faces, not through the bark. Split logs season in six to twelve months; whole rounds can take three years. Anything thicker than your forearm gets split.

**Stack off the ground, under cover, open to the wind.** On pallets or poles, bark up, in a single row where air passes through, with a roof — a sheet of tin, a tarp, an overhang — that keeps rain off but does not seal the sides. A stack against a house wall with a lid on it is a stack that rots.

**Season for a year.** The target is **below 20% moisture**. You can tell: seasoned wood is lighter than it looks, the bark loosens, the ends check and crack, and two pieces knocked together ring rather than thud. Green wood hisses and bubbles at the cut ends when it burns, and wastes a third of its energy boiling its own water off.

**Keep a week's wood indoors**, dry and warm, and refill it from the stack.

## Which wood

By energy per volume when dry, best to worst, of what grows in Britain:

**Hornbeam, beech, oak, ash, hawthorn, apple** — dense hardwoods, slow-burning, good coals. Ash is the one that burns tolerably even green, which is why the old rhyme praises it.

**Birch, sycamore, elm, hazel** — good middling firewood. Birch burns fast and bright.

**Willow, poplar, alder** — light, burn fast, low heat. Fine for kindling and summer cooking, poor for heating.

**Pine, spruce, larch** — resinous, burn hot and fast, spit, and coat a chimney in tar. Excellent kindling; sweep the flue often if you burn much of it.

**Never burn** treated timber, painted wood, chipboard, MDF or anything from a demolished modern building indoors. The fumes are poisonous.

## Coppicing: the fuel that grows back

Cut a hazel, ash, willow, sweet chestnut or alder to a stump in winter and it throws up a dozen straight poles, which you cut again in seven to fifteen years. A **coppice** managed in rotation — cut a section each year — gives a permanent, self-renewing supply of firewood, poles, fencing and charcoal wood without ever felling a tree.

Roughly a hectare of well-managed coppice heats a house. This was how Britain fuelled itself for two thousand years, and much of it is still there, overgrown and waiting to be cut.

**Willow** is the fastest: cuttings pushed into wet ground grow two metres a year and can be cut every two or three years for stove fuel. Not the best wood, but the most of it.

## Charcoal

Charcoal is wood with the water and volatiles driven off. What remains is nearly pure carbon: it burns **hotter** (hot enough to melt copper and forge iron), **cleaner** (almost no smoke — which is exactly what makes it the deadliest fuel indoors: it gives off carbon monoxide with no smoke to warn you. **Never burn charcoal indoors, in a tent, or in a closed garage, flue or no flue.** Cook with it outside), and **lighter** to carry. You lose about 75% of the weight and keep most of the energy.

**The retort method** — the one to use:

1. A metal drum with a tight lid and a small hole or pipe in the lid. Pack it full of split hardwood, sized like kindling to fist-thick.
2. Build a fire **around and under** the drum. Not in it.
3. Steam comes out of the hole first — white. After an hour or so it turns to thin blue-yellow smoke that will burn. That is the wood gas; light it at the pipe and it helps heat the drum.
4. When the flame at the pipe dies away, the charring is done. **Seal the hole** with clay or a bolt, pull the fire away, and **leave it sealed until it is cold** — a day. Open it warm and the charcoal ignites.

The traditional **pit or clamp** — a stacked pile of wood covered in turf and earth, lit at the centre and left to smoulder for days with the air carefully controlled — makes more at once but needs constant watching and years of skill. Start with the drum.

**Store charcoal dry.** It soaks up moisture from the air and then will not light.

## Other fuels

**Peat** — cut in spring, stacked to dry all summer, burns slow and smoky. Enormous reserves in upland Britain and Ireland; digging it is heavy work and it takes a wet year badly.

**Dried dung** — cattle and horse dung, dried in cakes in the sun. Burns steadily with little smell once dry. The fuel of half the world.

**Straw and reed** — burn fast and hot. Compressed into bricks or logs they are far more manageable. A simple lever press and a wet slurry of straw or shredded paper makes bricks that dry in a few weeks.

**Waste vegetable oil** in a wick lamp or a drip stove. Smoky but real heat, and it stores for years.

**Wood gas** — the gas from a retort, or from a purpose-built gasifier, runs a petrol engine. Half the vehicles in occupied Europe ran on it in 1944. A serious project, but a proven one.

**Biogas** — a sealed digester of manure and food waste at 30–40 °C produces methane that runs a cooker. Small farm-scale digesters are common across Asia. Needs warmth, steady feeding, and respect: the gas is explosive and the digester can produce hydrogen sulphide.

## Storing fuel safely

- **Firewood and charcoal:** away from the house, so a fuel-store fire is not a house fire. At least 5 m.
- **Liquid fuels** — petrol, diesel, paraffin, oil — in sealed metal or approved plastic containers, in a ventilated outbuilding, off the ground, away from ignition and out of sun. Petrol degrades in months; add stabiliser and rotate. Diesel keeps a year or two and then grows algae. Paraffin and lamp oil keep for years.
- **Gas cylinders** upright, outdoors or in a ventilated store, valves closed, away from heat. Propane keeps indefinitely.
- **Never siphon by mouth.** A mouthful of petrol inhaled is a chemical pneumonia.
- **Keep a fire extinguisher or sand bucket at the store**, and know that a fuel fire is not put out with water.

## How much

Measure your winter. Weigh a day's wood on a cold day; multiply by the days of heating you expect; add a third for a bad year and a third again for cooking. Then cut it, now, for next year. If you have never done it, do a month's worth this season and learn what a month actually looks like stacked. It is more than people think, and it is the honest measure of what living on wood means.
