# Childbirth with no midwife

> **Note** Read this now, not during a labour. Better still, read *A Book for Midwives* (Hesperian, free) and *Where There Is No Doctor* chapter 19, which cover everything here in far greater depth and belong in your library. And while there are midwives, use them: the single best preparation for an unattended birth is attending an attended one.

The first thing to know is that **birth mostly works.** Women have given birth without professionals for the whole of human history, and the overwhelming majority of labours end with a healthy mother and baby with no one doing anything clever. Your job is to keep things clean and warm, not interfere, recognise the few situations that are genuinely dangerous, and act fast in the one that matters most.

## Before labour

- **Clean cloths and towels**, lots, washed and dried in the sun or ironed.
- **Clean string** and a **clean blade** — boiled for 20 minutes, kept wrapped — for the cord.
- **A bowl, soap, clean water**, and a bucket.
- **A warm room** — warmer than is comfortable for adults. Newborns lose heat fast and cannot make it well.
- A **plastic sheet** under the bedding, and a **place for the mother to squat or kneel** if she wants to.
- A **torch**.

Know the **due date**, roughly: 40 weeks from the first day of the last period. Anything from 37 to 42 weeks is normal.

## The stages

**First stage — opening.** Contractions start irregular and far apart and become regular, stronger and closer. This is long — **twelve to twenty-four hours for a first baby**, often much less for later ones. The cervix opens to about ten centimetres. There may be a "show" of blood-streaked mucus and the waters may break, clear or slightly pink.

What to do: **almost nothing.** Let her move, walk, eat lightly, drink often, empty her bladder every couple of hours, rest between contractions. Warm water — a bath or wet cloths on the back — helps pain. Do not do internal examinations; every hand inside is an infection risk and tells you little you cannot see.

**Second stage — pushing.** The urge to push is unmistakable and she will know. This lasts minutes to a couple of hours. Upright, squatting or on all fours is easier and safer than lying flat. She pushes with contractions and rests between; you encourage and do not hurry.

As the head crowns, **support it with a clean hand** — gentle pressure so it comes slowly rather than in a rush, which prevents tearing. The head usually comes out facing down, then turns to one side on its own. **Check for the cord around the neck**: feel with a finger; if it is there, slip it over the head. If it is too tight to slip, it can be clamped in two places and cut, but this is rare.

The shoulders follow with the next contraction, one then the other, then the rest of the baby all at once, slippery. **Do not pull.** Guide, support, catch.

**Third stage — the placenta.** Comes out on its own, usually within thirty minutes, with a small gush of blood. **Do not pull the cord** — a torn cord or an inverted uterus is a disaster. Put the baby to the breast at once; the suckling releases the hormone that contracts the uterus and delivers the placenta. When it comes, check it is whole — a lobed disc with membranes. A piece left inside means bleeding and infection.

## The baby

- **Dry vigorously with a warm cloth** the moment it is born. This is both warmth and stimulation; most babies cry within the first minute because of it.
- **Skin to skin on the mother's chest, covered**, with a hat if there is one. Warmth is the priority. Do not bathe the baby for at least a day.
- **The cord:** wait until it stops pulsing — one to three minutes — then tie it tightly with clean string in two places, about four and eight centimetres from the baby, and cut between with the boiled blade. Keep the stump clean and dry; it drops off in a week or two.
- **Breastfeed within the first hour.** The first milk is thick and yellow and is exactly what the baby needs. Feeding often is what makes the milk come.
- **A baby that does not breathe:** dry and rub the back firmly. Clear the mouth and nose with a finger or a cloth. If still not breathing after a minute: cover its nose and mouth with your mouth and give **five gentle puffs**, just enough to see the chest rise. If there is no heartbeat or it is very slow, **chest compressions with two fingers**, three compressions to one breath, and keep going. Most babies who need this respond within minutes.

## The one thing that kills

**Bleeding after birth** is the commonest cause of maternal death in the world. A normal loss is up to about half a litre — a soaked pad or two. **More than that, or a steady flow that does not slow, or any gush after the first hour, is an emergency.**

What to do, immediately and all at once:

1. **Rub the uterus hard** through the belly — the top of it is at about the navel after birth. Cup it and massage firmly in a circle until it contracts into a hard ball the size of a grapefruit. This is uncomfortable and it works. Keep checking every few minutes; if it goes soft, rub again.
2. **Put the baby to the breast**, or if the baby cannot feed, stimulate the nipples by hand. This releases oxytocin, which contracts the uterus.
3. **Empty the bladder.** A full bladder stops the uterus contracting.
4. **Check the placenta is complete.** Retained fragments keep the uterus from closing.
5. If bleeding continues: **bimanual compression** — one fist inside the vagina pressing up against the uterus, the other hand on the belly pressing down, squeezing the uterus between them, held for as long as it takes. This is exhausting and it saves lives.
6. **Misoprostol** tablets, if you have them — 800 micrograms under the tongue or in the rectum — are the drug that has changed this everywhere in the world. They keep for years. If you are preparing for a birth without a midwife, this is the single most important thing to have.
7. Raise her legs, keep her warm, keep fluids going by mouth.

Bleeding **before** the baby is born, with pain, is also an emergency and there is little to do at home but deliver the baby as fast as it will come and then manage as above.

## Other things that go wrong

**The cord comes out first.** A loop of cord visible before the baby is a dire emergency — the baby's blood supply is being crushed. Get her **on her knees with her chest on the floor and her bottom in the air**, which takes the weight off, and push the presenting part gently back up off the cord with a clean hand, and keep it there while the baby is delivered as quickly as possible. Survival is poor without surgery; this is the best that can be done.

**Bottom first (breech).** Three or four in a hundred. The baby delivers bottom, then legs, then body, then arms, then — last, and this is the danger — the head. **Hands off.** Do not pull; pulling traps the head. Let the body hang, supported only, until the shoulder blades appear; then the head follows with the next push, and a gentle lift of the body upward over the mother's belly helps it come. Most breech births at home end well if nobody pulls.

**Shoulders stuck** after the head is out and the head pulls back against the body like a turtle: get her **knees pulled hard up to her chest**, and have someone press firmly just above the pubic bone, downward, to push the shoulder under. Do not pull on the head. Usually frees in a minute.

**Labour that goes on and on** — active pushing for more than two hours with no progress, or a first stage well over a day with strong contractions and nothing changing — means the baby may not fit. Without a hospital there is little to do except keep her nourished, hydrated and rested, change positions, and wait. Many resolve. Some do not.

**Fever** in labour or the days after is infection and needs antibiotics if you have any.

**Fits** — seizures in late pregnancy or after birth, usually with headache, swelling and high blood pressure beforehand — are eclampsia, which is fatal without magnesium sulphate. Lie her on her side, protect her from injury, deliver the baby as fast as possible. Prevention is the only real answer: watch for severe headaches, visual disturbance and sudden swelling of the face and hands in the last months, and know it is coming.

## Tears

Small ones — the skin only — heal on their own, kept clean and dry with salt-water washing after every visit to the latrine. Larger ones, into the muscle, ideally want stitching within a day. If nobody can stitch, keep the legs together as much as possible for a fortnight, keep it scrupulously clean, and it will heal, though not neatly. A tear through to the back passage is a serious injury and needs skilled repair.

## After

- **Rest, food and fluids** for the mother for at least a week. She has done a day of hard labour and lost blood.
- Bleeding lessens over two to six weeks, red then brown then pale. Heavy fresh bleeding, clots, a bad smell, fever or belly pain after the first days is infection or retained tissue.
- **Feed the baby on demand**, eight to twelve times a day. Wet nappies six times a day means it is getting enough.
- **Yellow skin** in the first days is common and usually harmless; deep yellow in the first day, or spreading down the body, or a baby too sleepy to feed, is not.
- Keep the baby **warm, fed and close**, and keep visitors with coughs away.

Most of this you will never need. Read it anyway, twice, and keep the misoprostol.
