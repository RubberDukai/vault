# Minds under strain

Every disaster produces two kinds of casualty: the ones you can see, and the ones who look fine. The second group is larger. A community that keeps its people functioning — sleeping, eating, working, talking — comes through. One that does not fractures long before it starts to starve.

None of this needs a professional. Most of it is what a decent, steady person does anyway, made deliberate.

## What is normal

After something terrible, people shake, cannot sleep, cannot eat, cannot stop crying or cannot cry at all, feel nothing, feel everything, replay it constantly, or forget whole hours. Children wet the bed, cling, regress, act it out in play. Adults snap, withdraw, work frantically, or sit and stare.

**All of this is normal.** It is the mind doing what it does with an overload. For most people it eases over days to weeks, on its own, if the basics are in place. Your first job is to make sure the basics are in place, and your second is not to treat a normal reaction as an illness.

## Psychological first aid — look, listen, link

The approach used by every major aid agency, because it works and needs no training.

**Look.** Who is hurt, who is alone, who is frozen, who is caring for someone else and getting nothing themselves. Who has not eaten. Who has not slept. Who has disappeared.

**Listen.** Sit with them. Ask what they need. Let them talk if they want to and let them not if they do not. **Do not force anyone to tell the story** — making people relive it does harm, not good. Do not say it will be fine, or that you understand, or that at least something. Say that you are here, that this is a hard thing, and that you will help with the next practical step.

**Link.** Connect them to what they need: food, water, warmth, a safe place, their family, a task, information about what is happening. Practical help is the most psychologically effective thing you can offer, by a wide margin.

## The basics that do most of the work

- **Sleep.** Nothing repairs a mind like it, and nothing breaks one faster than the lack of it. Protect people's sleep as fiercely as their food. Watches in shifts, dark and quiet where people sleep, no one on duty for more than they can bear.
- **Food and water**, regularly, whether they want it or not.
- **Routine.** Meals at set times, tasks at set times, a rhythm to the day. Routine is a handrail for a mind that has lost its footing.
- **Purpose.** Give everyone a job, including the children and the elderly. Being useful is the opposite of being helpless, and helplessness is the wound.
- **Company.** People alone deteriorate. Pair people up. Eat together.
- **Information.** Not knowing is worse than bad news. Tell people what you know, when you know it, plainly.
- **Physical work.** Digging, carrying, building. It burns off the chemistry of fear and produces something.

## Children

Children take their reading of how bad things are from the adults. If you are steady, they will be steadier. That does not mean lying to them.

- **Tell the truth at their level.** "Something bad happened and some people are hurt. We are safe here. I am going to look after you." Not the details. Not nothing.
- **Keep them close and keep them busy.** Small real jobs — carrying water, feeding animals, minding a younger child — give them standing and purpose.
- **Routine matters even more for them.** Bedtimes. Meals. Lessons — the school module exists for exactly this. An hour of ordinary schoolwork in the middle of chaos is an hour of normality, and it is worth more than the arithmetic.
- **Let them play.** Play is how children process. If the play is disturbing — re-enacting what happened — that is not a problem to stop, it is the processing happening.
- **Expect regression** — thumbs, bed-wetting, clinging, tantrums from a child who had outgrown them. Do not punish it. It passes.
- **Physical closeness.** More than usual. It is not spoiling.

## Grief

People will die, and the people who loved them will have to carry on the next day, because there will be no one else to do their work.

Grief is not a problem to solve. Allow it. **Rituals matter** — a burial, words said, a marker, a meal afterward. These are not sentiment; they are how humans have always metabolised loss, and skipping them because there is no time costs far more later.

Let people work if they want to and rest if they need to. Watch the ones who do neither.

## Fear and panic

**A panic attack** — heart racing, cannot breathe, sure they are dying — is frightening and harmless. Stay with them. Slow your own breathing loudly so they can follow: in for four, hold for four, out for six. Ground them: name five things you can see, four you can hear, three you can touch. It passes in minutes.

**Acute fear** in a crisis is dealt with by **giving the person something to do**. A task with their hands narrows attention and displaces the spiral.

## When someone is dangerous to themselves

Watch for: talking about ending it, or about being a burden; giving things away; sudden calm after deep distress; withdrawal plus hopelessness; saying goodbye.

**Ask directly.** "Are you thinking of killing yourself?" Asking does not put the idea there — that is a myth, and a dangerous one. It opens a door.

If the answer is yes or close to it: **do not leave them alone.** Remove the means — the rope, the blade, the tablets, the firearm — quietly and without argument. Stay, listen, feed them, get them to sleep, and be there in the morning. Most suicidal crises are hours long, not weeks. Being with someone through the hours is the intervention.

## De-escalation

Frightened, exhausted people get angry, and angry people in a crisis are dangerous. Almost all of it can be talked down.

- **Lower your voice.** Slower, quieter, calmer than theirs.
- **Space.** Do not corner them, do not crowd them, do not stand square-on. An angle, an arm's length, hands visible and open.
- **Acknowledge.** "You are angry, and I can see why." Not agreement; recognition.
- **One person talks.** Everyone else steps back.
- **Offer a choice**, both acceptable to you. Choice restores a sense of control, and loss of control is what the anger is about.
- **Do not argue the facts** while they are shouting. Facts can wait. The heat cannot.
- **Know when to walk away.** A person you cannot calm is a person to leave alone, with distance and an exit, until they burn down.

## The people in charge

Whoever is leading will try to do everything, sleep least, eat last, and break. They are the hardest to help because they are the ones helping.

**Rotate.** No one decides everything for more than a day or two. **Force rest** on the leaders; make it a rule that applies to everyone so it is not a weakness. Watch for the signs — irritability, mistakes, withdrawal, drinking — and step in before the collapse, not after.

## What not to do

- **Alcohol as medicine.** It numbs tonight and makes everything worse tomorrow, and a crisis is when dependence starts.
- **Isolation.** The person who "just needs to be alone" for days is in trouble.
- **Debriefing.** Making everyone sit in a circle and recount the worst of it was standard practice for years and has been shown to do harm. Talk is welcome when wanted. It is not a procedure.
- **Heroics without end.** Nobody who has not slept in three days is helping anyone.

## For yourself

You will be doing this for other people while carrying your own. The rules are the same: sleep, eat, work with your hands, talk to someone, keep a routine, and accept that being shaken is not failing. The people who get through are not the unshakeable ones. They are the ones who keep going anyway, and let others help them do it.
