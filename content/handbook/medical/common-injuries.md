# Sprains, breaks, teeth and eyes

The big emergencies are covered in **The first five minutes**. This is the everyday damage — the things that will happen to somebody in your household every year, and that used to mean a trip to a clinic.

## Sprains and strains

A sprain is a stretched or torn ligament; a strain is the same in a muscle or tendon. Both hurt, swell and bruise. Neither shows bone deformity.

For the first two days: **protect it, elevate it above the heart, compress it with a firm but not tight bandage, and cool it** — cold water or a wrapped cold pack for 15 minutes at a time, never ice directly on skin. After that, **start moving it gently**. The old advice of complete rest for weeks makes recovery slower and stiffer; controlled use within pain is what heals a soft-tissue injury.

**Tell a sprain from a fracture** by these — any of them means treat it as a break:

- Cannot bear weight at all, or take four steps
- Bone tenderness when pressed directly, rather than soft-tissue tenderness
- Deformity, or a joint that looks wrong
- A snap heard at the time
- Numbness or pins and needles beyond the injury

## Fractures

Immobilise, check circulation, keep them warm, get them somewhere they can rest. Detail is in **The first five minutes**.

**The healing timeline without surgery** — fingers and toes 3–4 weeks, wrist and forearm 6–8, lower leg 8–12, thigh 12–16 and longer. Splinted well and kept still, most simple fractures heal acceptably. They will not all heal straight.

**Setting a displaced fracture** is not for the untrained, but if there is no one else and a limb is bent where it should not be, with a pulse absent or the skin stretched white over the bone: gentle, steady traction along the line of the limb, pulling it straight, then splint in the corrected position. Done within the first hour, before swelling and muscle spasm set in, it is far easier and far less painful than later.

**An open fracture** — bone through skin — is a wound first. Irrigate copiously, cover, splint, and treat infection as the primary threat. Do not push the bone back in. See **Wounds and infection**.

## Dislocations

A joint out of its socket, usually shoulder, finger or kneecap. Obvious deformity, intense pain, inability to move it.

**A finger** is straightforward: firm traction along the finger while pushing the base of the displaced bone back toward where it belongs. It clunks in. Splint to its neighbour for a couple of weeks.

**A kneecap** displaced to the outside: straighten the leg gently, and it usually pops back on its own or with a light push inward.

**A shoulder** is harder and the risk of doing damage is real. If help exists, immobilise in a sling and go. If it does not, the safest method needs no force: the person lies face down on a table with the arm hanging straight down, holding a weight of 2–5 kg in the hand — a bucket of water — and simply waits. Muscle fatigue lets the joint slide back in over 15–30 minutes. Do not lever, twist or yank. After relocation, sling it for three weeks.

Check pulse and sensation before and after any attempt. If they are absent after, something is wrong.

## Teeth

Dental problems are the unglamorous crisis of any long emergency. Pain that stops sleep and eating, infections that spread to the jaw and beyond. **Prevention is worth more than any treatment here**: clean teeth twice a day with anything — a stick chewed into a brush, salt, charcoal, bicarbonate — and keep the sugar down.

**Knocked-out tooth.** An adult tooth can be replanted if you are fast. Pick it up by the crown, never the root. Rinse briefly in milk or saliva — not water, not scrubbed. Push it back into the socket the right way round, firmly, within **thirty minutes** if possible, and bite gently on cloth to hold it. Success drops sharply after an hour. Baby teeth are not replanted.

**Broken tooth.** Rinse, save any fragment, cover the exposed part with sugar-free gum, softened candle wax or a temporary filling material to keep air and food off the nerve.

**Lost filling or a cavity.** Clean it out, pack it with temporary filling material or clove oil on cotton wool. Clove oil is a genuine and quite powerful topical anaesthetic; keep some.

**Abscess.** A swollen, throbbing gum, sometimes with fever. Warm salt water rinses several times a day; if a point forms it will drain, and draining is the cure. Antibiotics help if you have them. A spreading infection — swelling into the face or neck, difficulty swallowing or breathing — is a genuine emergency.

**Extraction** is a last resort and a real skill. If a tooth cannot be saved and is causing recurring infection, and there is no dentist: local anaesthetic if available, loosen it by rocking firmly side to side to break the ligament, then pull with steady force rather than a jerk. Bite on cloth for an hour. Expect it to be worse than it sounds.

*Where There Is No Dentist* covers all of this properly and belongs in your library.

## Eyes

**Something in the eye.** Do not rub. Blink under clean water, or pull the upper lid over the lower. A grain on the white can be lifted with the corner of a clean damp cloth. **Anything embedded or on the coloured part — leave it. Cover both eyes** (they move together) and find help.

**Chemical splash.** Flush with clean water for **at least 20 minutes**, continuously, eyelids held open. Nothing else matters as much as the flushing, and it cannot be done for too long.

**A blow to the eye.** If vision is affected, if there is blood visible inside the eye, or if the eye looks misshapen, cover it loosely and rest lying down with the head raised. Do not press on it.

**Arc eye / snow blindness.** Painful, gritty eyes hours after welding without a mask or a bright day on snow. Dark room, cool compresses, it resolves in a day or two. Prevent it: eye protection, or slits cut in cloth or bark.

## Bites and stings

**Adder** — Britain's only venomous snake. Bites are rarely fatal but can be very unpleasant, especially in children and the elderly. Keep the limb still and below the heart, remove rings, and get antivenom if it exists. **Do not cut, suck, tourniquet or ice** the bite; all of these make it worse.

**Insect stings.** Scrape a bee sting out sideways with a card or fingernail rather than pinching it. Cold compress. Antihistamine if available.

**Anaphylaxis** — swelling of the face and throat, wheezing, collapse, within minutes of a sting, food or drug — is fatal without adrenaline. An **auto-injector** into the outer thigh, through clothing, then a second after 5 minutes if not improving. Anyone with a known severe allergy should have two, and everyone around them should know where they are.

**Animal and human bites** are wounds with a very high infection rate. Irrigate long and hard, never close them, and watch for infection daily. See **Wounds and infection**. Rabies is not present in the UK; elsewhere, a bite from a mammal acting strangely needs the vaccine series, and there is no substitute.

**Ticks.** Grip as close to the skin as possible with fine tweezers and pull straight out, steadily. Do not twist, burn or smother it. A spreading circular rash days to weeks later is Lyme disease and needs antibiotics.

## Heat

**Heat exhaustion** — sweating heavily, pale, dizzy, nauseous, cramping — is treated by stopping, shade, cooling and fluids with salt. It recovers.

**Heatstroke** — hot skin that may be **dry**, confusion, staggering, collapse, temperature above 40 °C — is a life-threatening emergency. Cool them by every means at once: shade, strip, douse with water, fan, ice packs to neck, armpits and groin, and keep going. Cooling is the treatment; nothing else will do.

## Drowning

Get them out without becoming a second casualty — reach or throw, do not swim unless trained.

**Five rescue breaths first, then CPR.** Drowning is a breathing problem before it is a heart problem, so the breaths matter more here than in any other collapse. Expect water and vomit; roll them to clear it and carry on.

Anyone who has inhaled water and recovered needs watching for **24 hours**. Breathing can deteriorate hours later.

## Cold injury

**Frostbite** — white, waxy, numb fingers, toes, nose or ears. Rewarm only when there is no risk of refreezing, in water at 37–39 °C (comfortably warm to an uninjured hand) for 30 minutes. It will hurt badly. Do not rub, do not use direct heat, do not burst blisters. A frozen part that thaws and refreezes is far worse than one left frozen until it can be kept warm.

**Trench foot** — from days of wet, cold feet. Prevent it with dry socks and drying feet daily. Treat by drying, warming gently and keeping off them.

Hypothermia is in **Staying warm and dry**.
