# Wounds and infection

Before antibiotics, a scratch from a rose thorn could kill a healthy adult. That world is one supply chain away. The good news is that most wound infection is prevented mechanically, not chemically — by getting the contamination physically out of the wound.

## Cleaning is the whole game

**Irrigation is the single most effective thing you will do.** The dose makes the poison: the more contaminated the wound, the more it needs washing out.

- Use **clean drinking-quality water**. Sterile is better, boiled-and-cooled is fine, and the volume matters far more than the sterility.
- Use **pressure**. A syringe without a needle, or a plastic bag or bottle with a pinhole squeezed hard. A gentle trickle does very little; a jet lifts bacteria and debris out.
- Use **a lot**. A guideline is **at least 500 ml for a small wound, a litre or more for anything dirty**. Keep going until what runs out is clear and you can see clean tissue.
- Pick out visible grit, gravel, splinters and clothing fragments with clean tweezers.
- Scrub the *surrounding skin* with soap; do not scrub inside the wound itself with anything harsh.

> **Warning** Do not pour hydrogen peroxide, undiluted iodine, alcohol or bleach into an open wound. They kill your own healing cells as readily as bacteria and they slow healing measurably. They belong on intact skin around the wound, not in it. Clean water under pressure beats all of them.

## To close or not to close

This is the decision people get wrong.

**Close** (steri-strips, glue, or stitches if you know how): clean, fresh cuts less than about 6–8 hours old, with straight edges, in a well-supplied area, that you have thoroughly irrigated.

**Do not close**: anything dirty, any bite, any puncture, anything crushed or ragged, anything more than about 12 hours old, anything you cannot get properly clean. Closing contamination inside a wound is how you make an abscess, and an abscess in a world without surgery is a very serious problem.

Instead, leave it open, pack it loosely with clean moist gauze, dress it, and change the dressing daily. If it is clean and healthy after **3–5 days**, it can be closed then — this is called delayed primary closure and it is standard practice in field medicine for good reason.

**Bites — animal or human — are never closed.** Human bites, including the knuckle wound from punching someone in the teeth, are among the most reliably infected wounds there are.

## Dressings

Keep it **moist, covered and clean**. Wounds heal faster moist than dry; a scab is not the goal.

- Non-adherent layer against the wound, absorbent layer over it, secured.
- Change when it strikes through, gets wet or smells — daily at first, less often as it improves.
- If gauze has dried onto the wound, soak it off with clean water rather than pulling.
- **Honey** is a genuine antibacterial dressing with real evidence behind it: high sugar, low water activity, low pH, and it produces hydrogen peroxide slowly. Medical-grade is best; raw honey is a reasonable substitute. Do not give honey to infants under one year, ever, because of botulism spores.
- Clean cloth boiled and dried works when nothing else is available.

## Recognising infection

Check every wound daily and know what you are looking for.

| Sign | What it means |
|---|---|
| Increasing pain after day 2 | Early infection — pain should be improving by then |
| Spreading redness | Cellulitis, spreading in the tissue |
| Warmth and swelling | Inflammation, possibly infection |
| Pus or foul smell | Established infection or abscess |
| **Red streaks running up the limb** | Lymphangitis — spreading fast, serious |
| **Fever, chills, confusion, fast heart rate** | Possible sepsis — life-threatening |

Draw a line around the edge of redness with a pen and write the time. If it has moved outward in a few hours, this is escalating quickly.

**Sepsis kills fast.** Fever with confusion, rapid breathing, a racing heart, mottled skin or very low urine output means the infection is systemic. Without antibiotics your options are drainage of any abscess, aggressive fluids by mouth, and rest. This is where a stocked medical supply saves a life and nothing else will.

## Abscesses

A closed pocket of pus will not resolve with antibiotics alone; it has to drain. Warm compresses several times a day encourage it to come to a head and open by itself, which is much preferable to cutting. If you must open one: the incision goes at the softest, most prominent point, along skin lines, and afterwards it is irrigated and packed loosely so it drains from the bottom up rather than sealing over.

## Tetanus

Tetanus lives in soil and manure and enters through punctures, crush injuries and dirty wounds. It causes muscle spasm starting in the jaw, and untreated it kills roughly one to two in ten of those infected — more without intensive care.

There is no home treatment. **The vaccine is the answer and it lasts about ten years.** If you are prepping seriously, know when everyone in the household last had a tetanus booster, and get them current. It is one of the cheapest and highest-value things on this entire list.

## Antibiotics, used properly

If you have them, they are precious and finite.

- They do **nothing** for viruses — colds, flu, most sore throats and most diarrhoea.
- **Finish the course.** Stopping early selects for the bacteria that survived.
- Under-dosing is worse than not treating: it breeds resistance and buys you nothing.
- Know what you have, what it treats, its dose, and who is allergic. Write it down and keep it with the supplies.
- Note that a serious allergic reaction to an antibiotic — swelling, breathing difficulty, collapse — is itself an emergency.

Stocking and self-prescribing antibiotics is not something to do casually or on internet advice; talk to a pharmacist or doctor about what is appropriate for your household while you still can.
