# Long-term conditions without a pharmacy

Most households contain someone who takes a medicine every day and would be in trouble without it. In an ordinary emergency the pharmacy reopens in a week. In the scenario this Vault is built for, it does not — and the honest truth is that for some conditions there is no home substitute, only preparation.

This chapter is therefore mostly about **what to do now**, with a clear-eyed section on what can and cannot be managed later.

## The general rules

**Build a reserve, legally, now.** Most doctors will prescribe a longer supply if asked for a good reason — travel, remote living, a stated wish to hold a buffer. Two to three months is achievable for most medicines. Rotate it: use the oldest, replace with the newest, so the reserve is always in date.

**Know the medicine, not just the box.** The generic name, the dose, what it is for, what happens if it stops, and what could substitute. Write it on a card in the medicine box.

**Expiry dates are conservative.** For most tablets and capsules, the drug is still effective for years past the date — potency declines slowly, and the main risk is reduced strength rather than harm. The exceptions matter: **insulin, liquid antibiotics, eye drops, nitroglycerin, adrenaline injectors** degrade meaningfully, and **tetracycline** antibiotics past their date have been linked to kidney harm. Store everything cool, dark and dry, and it lasts longer.

**Halve before you stop.** If a medicine is running out, a reduced dose for longer usually beats a full dose then nothing, for most conditions. The exceptions are noted below.

## Diabetes

**Type 1** — the body makes no insulin. **Insulin is life.** Without it, death follows in days to weeks, and there is no herb, diet or substitute that changes that. This is the hardest fact in this chapter.

What can be done: hold as much insulin as can legally be obtained; keep it **cold** (a fridge, a cool cellar, an evaporative pot, a stream — insulin in use is fine at room temperature for a month, and refrigerated insulin keeps well past its date); and **stretch it** by cutting carbohydrate to the minimum, which cuts the insulin needed to cover it. A very low-carbohydrate diet with insulin only for the basal need can extend a supply severalfold. Learn to do this while there is a doctor to help.

**Type 2** — insulin resistance. This one is genuinely manageable without drugs for many people: **weight loss, very low carbohydrate, and daily physical work** can bring blood sugar to normal, and in a world of manual labour and no processed food it often does. Metformin keeps for years and is worth a reserve; the sulfonylureas (gliclazide and its relatives) can cause dangerous low sugar if food is short and should be dropped first.

**Know the signs.** High sugar: thirst, passing lots of urine, exhaustion, sweet-smelling breath, and eventually deep sighing breathing and confusion — an emergency needing insulin and fluids. Low sugar: sweating, shaking, confusion, aggression, collapse — give sugar at once by mouth if conscious.

## Asthma

**Preventers** (the brown or purple inhalers — steroids) keep well past their date and are the ones to stockpile; used daily they stop attacks happening. **Relievers** (the blue one, salbutamol) are for attacks. Keep several of each.

Without inhalers: **avoid triggers** — smoke, dust, cold air, exertion in cold, animal hair; **caffeine** in strong coffee or tea is a weak but real bronchodilator; **sitting upright, leaning forward**, breathing slowly out through pursed lips; **steam**. A severe attack — cannot speak in sentences, lips going blue, exhaustion — is an emergency with no home answer beyond the above. An adrenaline auto-injector, if one is held for allergy, does open the airways.

Smoke is the enemy. Anyone with asthma needs to be as far from the cooking fire as the house allows.

## Epilepsy

**Do not stop the medicine suddenly.** Abrupt withdrawal can trigger prolonged seizures. If supply is running out, reduce slowly over weeks. Stockpile as much as can be obtained; most anticonvulsants keep for years.

**A seizure:** protect the head, move objects away, do not restrain, put **nothing in the mouth**, time it. Roll them on their side when it stops. Most end in under five minutes and need nothing more. **One lasting more than five minutes, or one following another without recovery**, is an emergency — without rescue medication (buccal midazolam, rectal diazepam — worth holding if prescribed) there is nothing to do but protect them and wait.

Sleep, regular meals, and avoiding alcohol and flashing light reduce seizures without any drug.

## High blood pressure

Rarely an emergency in itself. Without tablets, pressure drifts up and the long-term risk of stroke and heart attack rises — but **weight loss, far less salt, no alcohol, daily hard work and no processed food** lower it substantially, and that is the world you would be living in. Stockpile a reserve; if it runs out, this is one of the conditions where a person mostly carries on.

## Heart disease

**Aspirin** — 75 mg daily — is cheap, keeps for years, and is the single most valuable heart drug to hold. Anyone with known heart disease should have a large reserve. **Nitroglycerin** spray for angina is worth holding but degrades. **Statins** keep well and are worth a reserve; stopping them raises long-term risk but is not an emergency. **Beta-blockers** should be reduced gradually rather than stopped dead.

A heart attack — crushing chest pain spreading to arm or jaw, sweating, nausea, breathlessness — is treated with **300 mg of aspirin chewed at once**, rest, and reassurance. There is no other home treatment; survival depends on the size of the attack.

**Heart failure** — swollen ankles, breathlessness lying flat — is eased by sleeping propped up, restricting salt and fluid, and the diuretic if held. Keep furosemide if it is prescribed.

## Thyroid

**Underactive** (levothyroxine): the tablets keep for years; stockpile freely. Without them, symptoms return over weeks to months — cold, tired, slow, gaining weight — unpleasant and eventually serious, but slow. This is a condition where a large reserve buys years.

**Overactive**: carbimazole reserve; without it the condition returns. Rare, and it needs a doctor.

## Blood thinners

**Warfarin** needs monitoring that will not exist; without it, the risk it was preventing — clots, stroke — returns. The newer anticoagulants need no monitoring and keep well; hold a reserve. Anyone on these should know that a head injury while anticoagulated is far more dangerous.

## Mental health medication

**Antidepressants** should be tapered over weeks, not stopped; withdrawal is unpleasant but not dangerous. **Antipsychotics and mood stabilisers** are a different matter — relapse can be severe and a danger to the person and others. Hold the largest reserve possible and taper slowly if it must end. **Lithium** needs blood monitoring and a steady salt and fluid intake; dehydration with lithium is dangerous.

## Contraception

The pill keeps for years past its date. **Condoms** keep for about five years cool and dark. Long-acting methods placed before things fail — an implant lasts three years, a coil five to ten — are the most robust answer. The alternative is knowing the cycle: fertility is highest in the days before and around ovulation, roughly the middle of the cycle, and the fertility-awareness methods work when learned properly and followed strictly.

## Allergies

**Adrenaline auto-injectors** for anaphylaxis: hold two, keep them cool, and know they lose potency past their date but are **still worth using** in an emergency — a weak dose beats none. **Antihistamines** keep for years.

## What to do this month

1. List every regular medicine in the household — name, dose, why.
2. Ask the prescriber for a three-month supply of each. Explain why. Most will help.
3. Buy over the counter, in quantity: paracetamol, ibuprofen, aspirin, antihistamines, oral rehydration salts, antacids, loperamide, a topical antibiotic, a topical antifungal.
4. Store it all cool, dark, dry, labelled, and start rotating.
5. Get everyone's vaccinations current, including tetanus.
6. Read *Where There Is No Doctor*. Then put it on the shelf in this library.
