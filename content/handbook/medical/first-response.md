# The first five minutes

> **Note** This is a memory aid for someone who has already been taught, and a starting point for someone who has not. Get real hands-on first aid training while courses still exist. Reading is not the same as having done it.

Panic comes from not knowing what to do first. The cure is a fixed order you follow every time, so the decision is already made.

## The order

**Danger.** Do not become the second casualty. Fire, traffic, electricity, gas, unstable structures, deep water, animals. If the scene is not safe, make it safe or move the casualty — otherwise there are now two people needing help and nobody giving it.

**Response.** Speak loudly, squeeze the shoulders. "Can you hear me?" A response means an airway and a heartbeat, and you can slow down.

**Catastrophic bleeding.** If blood is pumping or pouring, this jumps the queue ahead of everything else. Someone can bleed out from a limb in **two to three minutes**. Press hard, directly on the wound, with anything, and do not let go.

**Airway.** Unconscious: tilt the head back, lift the chin. In an unconscious person on their back, the tongue is the commonest cause of obstruction and this one movement fixes it. Suspected spinal injury: use a jaw thrust instead, but a blocked airway kills faster than a spinal injury, so if you cannot manage both, open the airway.

**Breathing.** Look, listen and feel for up to **10 seconds**. Occasional gasping is *not* breathing.

**Circulation.** Not breathing normally: start CPR now.

## CPR

- **30 chest compressions, then 2 rescue breaths**, repeat.
- Centre of the chest, heel of the hand, other hand on top, fingers interlocked.
- **5–6 cm deep** on an adult. **100–120 per minute** — the tempo of *Stayin' Alive* or *Nellie the Elephant*.
- Let the chest come all the way back up between compressions. Incomplete recoil is a common and serious error.
- If you are unwilling or unable to give breaths, **compressions alone are far better than nothing**. Do not let the breaths be the reason you do not start.
- Children: same rhythm, one hand or two fingers for an infant, about a third of chest depth, and begin with 5 rescue breaths because in children the cause is usually respiratory.
- Swap with someone else every two minutes if you can. Quality collapses with fatigue faster than people realise.
- Continue until they recover, until skilled help takes over, or until you physically cannot continue.

## Severe bleeding

1. **Direct pressure**, hard, on the wound itself. Not around it.
2. **Pack** deep wounds — push clean cloth firmly into the cavity and keep pressing. This is what works for a wound too deep for surface pressure.
3. **Keep pressing.** Ten minutes without peeking. Lifting to look restarts the clock.
4. **Tourniquet** for catastrophic limb bleeding that pressure will not stop: 5–7 cm above the wound, never over a joint, tightened until the bleeding **stops**, which will hurt a great deal. **Write down the time it went on.** Do not loosen it periodically — that is old and dangerous advice. A limb tolerates about two hours reliably.
5. Lie them down, keep them warm, and treat for shock.

**Shock** — pale, cold, clammy, fast weak pulse, rapid shallow breathing, thirst, confusion — means the circulation is failing. Lie them flat, raise the legs, keep them warm, give nothing by mouth if there is any chance of surgery, and reassure them. Reassurance is not nothing; fear raises oxygen demand.

## Choking

- **Coughing forcefully?** Let them cough. Do not interfere.
- **Cannot cough, speak or breathe:** lean them forward, **5 sharp back blows** between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
- Still blocked: **5 abdominal thrusts** — fist above the navel, sharply inward and upward.
- Alternate 5 and 5 until it clears or they lose consciousness — then start CPR, which itself can dislodge the obstruction.
- **Infants under one:** 5 back blows and 5 *chest* thrusts, head down along your forearm. **Never abdominal thrusts on an infant.**
- Anyone who has had abdominal thrusts should be checked for internal injury afterwards, if there is anyone to check them.

## Burns

- **Cool with clean running water for 20 minutes.** Not ice, not butter, not oil. Twenty minutes is the number, and it still helps up to three hours after the burn.
- Remove jewellery and non-stuck clothing before swelling starts.
- Cover loosely with cling film laid on in strips, or a clean non-fluffy cloth.
- **Do not burst blisters.** Intact skin is a sterile dressing.
- Keep the rest of them warm — large burns lose heat and fluid fast, and hypothermia is a real risk even in warm weather.
- Serious: any burn to face, hands, feet, genitals or across a joint; anything deeper than superficial; anything larger than the casualty's palm; any electrical or chemical burn; any burn to a child.

## Broken bones

Splint in the position found, immobilising the joint above and the joint below. Pad well. Check circulation past the splint before and after — fingers and toes should stay warm, pink, and have feeling. If they do not, loosen it.

**Open fractures** (bone through skin) are a surgical emergency and a very high infection risk. Cover with a clean dressing, do not push the bone back in, and treat infection risk as seriously as the fracture.

## The kit worth having

Bandages and plenty of gauze · a proper tourniquet · trauma shears · antiseptic · surgical tape · tweezers · a thermometer · gloves · a good torch · paracetamol and ibuprofen · antihistamines · oral rehydration salts · a printed first aid manual. Any of it that expires, rotate.
