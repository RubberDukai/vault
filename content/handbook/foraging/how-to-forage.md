# How to forage — method, seasons and regions

Foraging is not a survival skill in the sense of feeding a family from the hedgerow; it never did, even for people who lived by it. What it does is bigger than it sounds: **vitamins in the hungry gap**, the eight weeks of spring when the store is empty and the garden has not started; **calories from nuts in autumn**, the one wild food that is genuinely dense; **medicine, flavour, tea, and the knowledge of what is around you** — which is also what tells you where water is, which wood burns and which will poison you.

This module is written for **Britain and temperate north-western Europe**, because that is where its author lives and where every plant in it can be checked against the ground. The method is universal. The last section says how to carry it to another climate.

## The five-point identification

You are certain of a plant when you can answer all five, from the plant in front of you and not from memory of a picture:

1. **What is it?** Its name, and ideally its Latin name, so there is no confusion between two things called the same word in different counties.
2. **What could it be confused with?** Every edible worth eating has a lookalike. If you cannot name the lookalike, you have not finished learning the plant.
3. **How do I tell them apart, here, now?** A feature you can see, smell or feel on *this specimen*: the square stem, the smell of garlic, the milky sap, the ring on the stem. Not "the leaves are a bit greener."
4. **Which part, and when?** Rhubarb stalks are food and rhubarb leaves are poison. Elderflowers are food and elder leaves are not. Green potatoes, unripe elderberries, raw kidney beans — the same plant can be both.
5. **Is this place safe?** Roadside verges (lead, exhaust), field edges downwind of spraying, old industrial ground and tips (heavy metals), dog-walking routes at nose height, riverbanks below a sewage outfall. Plants concentrate what is in the soil and water.

If any answer is missing, **you put it down.** There is no hunger that a wrong mushroom improves.

**The confirmation habit.** Every entry in the identification logs gives a link to its encyclopedia article, which has photographs; look at the photograph *and* the written description *and* the lookalike's photograph before you eat anything for the first time. Better still, find someone who already knows and go out with them once. A single afternoon with an experienced forager teaches more than a book, because they teach you what to look at.

**Introduce every new food** in a small amount — a tablespoon — and wait 24 hours. People react individually to things that are safe for most.

## The ones to learn first

Before a single edible, learn to recognise the plants and fungi that kill, because they are few and they are the whole risk. They are described in full in the logs; the list is here so it is never more than a page away:

| Kills | Mistaken for | The tell |
|---|---|---|
| **Hemlock** (Conium) and **hemlock water-dropwort** (Oenanthe) | wild carrot, parsley, celery, parsnip roots | purple-blotched smooth stem, mousy smell (hemlock); roots like a bunch of pale parsnips by water, smells of parsley (water-dropwort). *Never eat a wild umbellifer you have not learnt individually.* |
| **Foxglove** | comfrey, borage (the leaf rosette) | foxglove leaves are soft, downy and *not* rough; no mucilage; grows dry |
| **Lords-and-ladies** (Arum) | wild garlic, sorrel (the young leaf) | arrow-shaped glossy leaf, no smell of garlic |
| **Yew** | juniper, other conifers | flat needles in two rows, red berry with a *hole* in the end; every part but the red flesh is deadly |
| **Deadly nightshade** | bilberry, other black berries | shiny black cherry-sized berry sitting in a green five-pointed star; dull purple bell flower |
| **Death cap** and **destroying angel** | field mushrooms, puffballs, edible white/green-capped fungi | white gills, a ring, and a **bag (volva) at the base** — dig it up whole to see |
| **Deadly webcap** | chanterelle | rusty-brown gills, cobweb veil, no funnel |
| **Funeral bell** | honey fungus, velvet shank | small brown, ring on stem, on wood |

## The forager's year — Britain and temperate Europe

Timings shift two or three weeks earlier in the south and west, later in the north and at altitude, and vary by a month between years. Learn the sequence, not the date.

**January – February.** The quiet months. Chickweed, hairy bittercress and winter purslane in mild spells; Jew's ear (jelly ear) on elder; velvet shank on dead wood after frost; seaweeds at the low spring tides; alexanders shoots on the coast; the last sloes and haws, sweetened by frost. Nettles start in sheltered spots by late February.

**March.** The hungry gap begins to close. **Nettles** (the year's best green), **wild garlic** (ramsons), sorrel, dandelion leaves, cleavers, hawthorn leaf buds ("bread and cheese"), young hogweed shoots *(only when certain — see the log)*, ground elder, garlic mustard. Morels from late March in the south on chalk and disturbed ground; St George's mushroom from the 23rd, its name day.

**April.** Everything above, at its best. Wild garlic flowers. Chickweed, wintercress, lady's smock. Sea beet, sea kale and sea purslane on the coast. Birch sap tapped at the very beginning of the month before the leaves open. Morels and St George's continue.

**May.** Elderflower opens at the end of the month. Hawthorn flower. Beech leaves in the first week they open. Broad-leaved sorrel, fat hen and orache appear in the garden as weeds. Pignuts. Mushrooms go quiet.

**June.** **Elderflower** (the month's harvest: cordial, champagne, fritters). Wild strawberry. Lime flowers for tea. Meadowsweet. Sea lettuce and dulse. Chicken of the woods begins on oak. Samphire (marsh samphire, glasswort) from mid-month on the saltmarsh.

**July.** Bilberries on the moor from mid-month. Wild raspberries. Cherries, cherry plums. Marsh samphire at its best. Wild marjoram, thyme, mint. Chanterelles start in wet years. Giant puffballs from late July.

**August.** **Blackberries** begin. Bilberries, wild plums and bullaces, crab apples begin. Hazelnuts green and milky. Rowan berries. Mushrooms start in earnest after rain: ceps, chanterelles, hedgehog fungus, puffballs, parasols, field and horse mushrooms in pasture.

**September.** The peak. Blackberries, elderberries (cooked only), haws, rosehips (after first frost is traditional but not necessary), sloes, damsons, crab apples, hazelnuts, first sweet chestnuts and walnuts, beech mast, acorns begin. The main mushroom season: everything in the log.

**October.** Sweet chestnuts, walnuts, acorns (for leaching), beech mast, medlars, quince, rosehips at their best, sloes after frost. Mushrooms continue: ceps, hedgehog, wood blewit, shaggy inkcap, honey fungus, oyster on dead wood. Last blackberries (folklore says none after Michaelmas, and the fact is they go mushy and mouldy).

**November.** Wood blewit, oyster mushroom, velvet shank, jelly ear. Late sloes and haws. Rosehips. Chestnuts on the ground. Seaweed on the low tides again.

**December.** Jelly ear, velvet shank, oyster. Chickweed and bittercress in mild spells. Seaweeds. The year turns.

## Where to look

- **Hedgerows and wood edges** — the richest ground: hazel, hawthorn, sloe, elder, crab apple, bramble, and the herb layer below them.
- **Old pasture** that has never been ploughed or sprayed — field and horse mushrooms, parasols, pignut, sorrel.
- **Deciduous woodland**, especially beech and oak — ceps, chanterelles, hedgehog fungus, beech mast, acorns, wild garlic in damp shade.
- **Birch woods** — birch boletes, chanterelles, sap in spring.
- **Moor and heath** — bilberry, crowberry, cowberry, heather honey if you keep bees.
- **Coast** — the most reliable larder in the country: seaweeds (nothing on the British coast is poisonous, though a few are unpleasant), samphire, sea beet, sea kale, rock samphire, shellfish at low tide (see *Hunting, trapping and fishing*).
- **Waste ground and your own garden** — fat hen, chickweed, dandelion, sow thistle, nettle, dock: the weeds are the crop you did not sow.
- **Not**: roadsides, sprayed field margins, golf courses, industrial land, the first metre of any footpath.

## The law and the manners

In England and Wales you may pick the "four Fs" — fruit, foliage, fungi and flowers — for your own use on land you have access to, but not uproot a plant without the landowner's permission, and not on nature reserves or SSSIs where bylaws forbid it. Scotland's access rights are wider. None of it will matter in a collapse; the manners will. **Take a third, leave two thirds** — for the plant, for the animals, for next year and for the next family. Never strip a patch. Never take the first or the last of anything.

## Kit

A knife; a basket or paper bags (plastic sweats mushrooms into slime and leaves into mush); gloves for nettles; a small trowel for roots (where permitted); a hand lens for the fine features of fungi; paper for spore prints; and this vault on a phone, with the logs and the encyclopedia photographs, or a printed copy of the logs in the bag.

## Taking the method to another region

The five points and the deadly-first rule do not change. What changes is the cast. To rebuild the logs for a new place:

1. **Find the killers first.** Every region has its own hemlock, its own death cap, its own castor bean or manchineel. The encyclopedia article for any deadly genus (*Amanita*, *Conium*, *Cicuta*, *Ricinus*, *Abrus*, *Hippomane*) lists where it grows.
2. **Find the families that travel.** Nettles, docks, chickweed, dandelion, plantain, mallow, brambles, wild roses, hazels, oaks and pines grow across the whole northern hemisphere and much of the south; what you know here works there.
3. **Find the local staple.** Every culture has a wild carbohydrate: acorns and chestnuts in the temperate zone, wild rice and cattail in wetlands, mesquite and agave in deserts, breadfruit and sago in the tropics, camas in the American west. The encyclopedia article on any region's indigenous food is the place to start.
4. **Rebuild the calendar** from the local seasons: a wet season and a dry season rather than four; or an austral year with the months reversed.
5. **Write it down** in your own copy of these chapters — the manual explains how to add pages — and get it checked by someone who knows.
