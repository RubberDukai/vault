# Trees — identification log

A tree is identified by more than its leaf: in winter there are no leaves, and in summer the bark, the buds, the way the twigs branch (**opposite** pairs or **alternate**), the fruit, and the shape of the whole tree tell you as much. Each entry gives the year-round tells, the confusions, and the uses. Firewood notes follow the old rhyme's judgement, checked against reality: ash and beech burn best; willow, poplar and elder barely burn at all.

## The four you must know

### Oak — *Quercus robur* / *Q. petraea* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Quercus_robur|Oak)
**Leaf:** lobed with rounded lobes, on almost no stalk (pedunculate oak) or a short stalk (sessile). **Bark:** grey, deeply fissured in vertical ridges. **Buds:** clustered at the twig tip, alternate. **Fruit:** acorns in cups, on long stalks (pedunculate) or none (sessile). **Winter:** the massive spreading crown, crooked branches, the clustered buds. **Confusion:** none, once you know the lobed leaf and the acorn. **Gives:** acorns (leach — see the plant log), the best structural timber there is, bark for tanning leather, galls for ink, good slow firewood when seasoned two years, and more insect life — hence birds — than any other tree.

### Ash — *Fraxinus excelsior* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Fraxinus_excelsior|Ash_tree)
**Leaf:** pinnate — 9–13 leaflets in opposite pairs on a central stalk, appearing late (May) and falling early, still green. **Bark:** pale grey, smooth then fissured into diamonds. **Buds:** **matt black**, in opposite pairs, the tip bud like a bishop's mitre — unmistakable in winter. **Fruit:** bunches of "keys", single-winged seeds hanging into winter. **Confusion:** rowan (leaflets toothed, red berries, alternate), elder (opposite, but shrubby, with corky bark and lenticels). **Gives:** the best firewood — it burns green; tool handles, bows, spear shafts, hurdles; fodder from the leaves ("tree hay"). Ash dieback is killing it across Europe; learn it while it stands.

### Beech — *Fagus sylvatica* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Fagus_sylvatica|Beech)
**Leaf:** oval, pointed, with a wavy edge and silky hairs when young, turning copper and often held through winter on young trees and hedges. **Bark:** **smooth silver-grey**, like an elephant's leg, at any age. **Buds:** long, thin, pointed, coppery, alternate — like little cigars. **Fruit:** bristly husks holding two or three three-sided nuts (mast), in good years. **Confusion:** hornbeam (bark grey but fluted and muscled, leaf doubly toothed, fruit a papery-winged nutlet). **Gives:** mast for people and pigs, excellent firewood and charcoal, timber for furniture and tools, the young leaves as a salad in the first week of May.

### Birch — *Betula pendula* / *B. pubescens* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Betula_pendula|Birch)
**Leaf:** small, triangular, toothed, on a slender stalk. **Bark:** **white** peeling in papery strips, with black diamond fissures on the silver birch. **Twigs:** fine and weeping (silver) or downy (downy birch). **Fruit:** small catkins that break into tiny winged seeds. **Confusion:** none — no other native tree is white. **Gives:** sap tapped in early spring (a litre or more a day from a hole drilled in the trunk, plugged after — drink, or boil down to syrup), bark that lights a fire wet, bark for containers, tar, fast-growing firewood, and the chaga and birch polypore fungi.

## The food trees

### Hazel — *Corylus avellana* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Corylus_avellana|Hazel)
A multi-stemmed shrub or small tree. **Leaf:** round-oval, doubly toothed, pointed tip, soft and hairy, alternate. **Bark:** smooth, brown-grey, shiny with lenticels. **Winter:** yellow lamb's-tail catkins from January, tiny red female flowers. **Fruit:** nuts in leafy husks, August–September. **Confusion:** young elm or alder leaves at a glance — alder is toothed but blunt-tipped and grows by water; hazel has the soft hairs and the catkins. **Gives:** nuts, the best coppice wood in the world — poles for hurdles, bean rows, wattle, thatching spars, rods that regrow every seven years — and water-divining rods, for what that is worth.

### Sweet chestnut — *Castanea sativa* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Castanea_sativa|Sweet_chestnut)
**Leaf:** long (up to 25 cm), narrow, glossy, with **sharp saw teeth**, alternate. **Bark:** grey-brown, developing a **spiral** of deep fissures with age. **Fruit:** October, dense spiny green cases splitting to show 2–3 flattened brown nuts with a pale tassel. **Confusion:** **horse chestnut** — hand-shaped leaves of 5–7 leaflets, sticky buds, a case with a few stout spikes and one glossy conker. **Horse chestnut is not food.** **Gives:** a bread staple (chestnut flour), good fence posts (rot-resistant), poor firewood (spits).

### Walnut — *Juglans regia* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Juglans_regia|Walnut)
**Leaf:** pinnate, 5–9 large glossy leaflets, aromatic when crushed, alternate, late to open. **Bark:** pale grey, smooth then fissured. **Fruit:** green plum-like husks, splitting to show the nut, October. **Confusion:** ash (opposite, black buds, more leaflets), hickory. **Gives:** nuts, pickled green walnuts (July), the finest gunstock and furniture timber, a brown dye and ink from the husks. Nothing grows well under it.

### Crab apple — *Malus sylvestris* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Malus_sylvestris|Crab_apple)
Small, often thorny, in hedges and wood edges. **Leaf:** oval, toothed, slightly hairy beneath, alternate. **Flower:** pink-white blossom in May. **Fruit:** small hard yellow-green apples, September–October. **Confusion:** feral orchard apples (larger fruit, no thorns — edible anyway). **Gives:** pectin for every other preserve, verjuice, cider, jelly; a hard timber for mallets and cogs; excellent firewood that smells of apple.

### Wild cherry — *Prunus avium* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Prunus_avium|Cherry)
**Bark:** shiny red-brown with **horizontal bands of lenticels**, peeling in horizontal strips. **Leaf:** oval, toothed, drooping, with two red glands on the stalk. **Flower:** white, April. **Fruit:** small red-black cherries in July, bitter to sweet. **Confusion:** bird cherry (flowers in spikes, fruit bitter and astringent). **Gives:** fruit, gum from the bark (edible, an old chewing gum), fine timber, good firewood.

### Blackthorn — *Prunus spinosa* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Prunus_spinosa|Blackthorn|Sloe)
A dense thorny hedge shrub with **black bark and vicious thorns** that turn septic. **Flower:** white, on bare wood, in March — before the leaves, which is how you tell it from hawthorn (flowers after the leaves, in May). **Leaf:** small, oval, dull. **Fruit:** sloes, blue-black with a bloom, September–November. **Gives:** sloes for gin, jelly and wine; the wood for walking sticks and shillelaghs; an impenetrable hedge.

### Hawthorn — *Crataegus monogyna* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Crataegus_monogyna|Hawthorn)
The hedge tree. **Leaf:** small, deeply lobed, glossy, alternate. **Thorns:** on the twigs, shorter than blackthorn's. **Flower:** white "May blossom" in May, after the leaves, smelling of almond and something less pleasant. **Fruit:** red haws, September–winter, one stone. **Confusion:** blackthorn (see above), midland hawthorn (two stones). **Gives:** young leaves ("bread and cheese") in April, flowers, haws for jelly, ketchup and fruit leather; a heart tonic in herbal medicine; hard dense wood that burns hot; the stock-proof hedge that made English farming.

### Elder — *Sambucus nigra* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Sambucus_nigra|Elderberry|Elder)
A shrubby small tree of waste ground and hedges. **Leaf:** pinnate, 5–7 toothed leaflets, **opposite**, smelling unpleasant when crushed. **Bark:** grey-brown, deeply furrowed and corky; twigs with a soft white pith. **Flower:** flat creamy plates in June, scented. **Fruit:** drooping clusters of black berries, September. **Confusion:** ash (also opposite and pinnate, but a real tree with black buds), **dwarf elder** (*Sambucus ebulus*, a herb not a shrub, dies back each year — poisonous). **Gives:** elderflower cordial, champagne and fritters; elderberry syrup for winter colds (**cooked only**); jelly ear fungus on the dead wood; hollow stems for pipes and pea-shooters. Leaves, bark, roots and green berries are poisonous. Poor firewood.

### Rowan, mountain ash — *Sorbus aucuparia* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Sorbus_aucuparia|Rowan)
**Leaf:** pinnate, 9–15 toothed leaflets, alternate. **Bark:** smooth silver-grey with lenticels. **Flower:** creamy clusters, May. **Fruit:** heavy bunches of orange-red berries, August–September. **Confusion:** ash (opposite, untoothed, black buds). **Gives:** berries, cooked, for jelly with game; the tree the old folk planted by the door against witches.

### Lime, linden — *Tilia* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Tilia_×_europaea|Lime_tree|Tilia)
**Leaf:** heart-shaped, toothed, lop-sided at the base, alternate, with tufts of hair in the vein-angles beneath; sticky with aphid honeydew in summer. **Bark:** grey, smooth then fissured; often a mass of sprouts round the base. **Flower:** fragrant pale yellow clusters hanging from a wing-like bract, June–July. **Gives:** the flowers dried for tea (calming; a favourite of the French), the young leaves in salads, the inner bark (bast) for the best native cordage — retted in water for weeks, then peeled into strong fibre — and a soft carving timber.

### Sea buckthorn — *Hippophae rhamnoides* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Hippophae_rhamnoides|Sea_buckthorn)
A thorny silver-grey shrub of dunes and, planted, roadsides; narrow silver leaves; dense **bright orange berries** along the twigs from September through winter. The most vitamin-C-rich fruit in the country, very sour; juice, syrup. Unmistakable.

### Juniper — *Juniperus communis* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Juniperus_communis|Juniper)
A prickly conifer shrub of chalk downs and highland moors; needles in threes, blue-green, sharp; berries green then blue-black over two years. **Confusion:** **yew** — yew's needles are flat, soft, in two rows, and its "berry" is red and cup-shaped; juniper's are sharp, in threes, and blue. **Gives:** the berries for gin, game and sauerkraut; the wood for smoking meat.

## The working trees

### Willow — *Salix* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Salix|Willow)
Many species, all by water: long narrow leaves (most), or oval (goat willow, "pussy willow"). Twigs flexible, buds with a single scale like a hood, alternate. **Gives:** basketry (osier rods), living fences, charcoal for drawing and gunpowder, the fastest-growing fuel wood for a coppice rotation (poor when burnt — it needs to be very dry), and **salicin in the bark** — the original aspirin: a tea of the inner bark for pain and fever, with aspirin's cautions.

### Alder — *Alnus glutinosa* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Alnus_glutinosa|Alder)
The tree of riverbanks and bogs. **Leaf:** round, **blunt or notched at the tip**, toothed, sticky when young. **Fruit:** small woody cones that stay on all winter — the only broadleaf with cones. **Bark:** dark, fissured. **Gives:** timber that lasts for ever under water (Venice stands on it), clog soles, charcoal, a poor fuel that makes the best gunpowder charcoal. Fixes nitrogen: plant it to improve wet ground.

### Sycamore — *Acer pseudoplatanus* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Acer_pseudoplatanus|Sycamore|Maple)
**Leaf:** large, five-lobed, hand-shaped, **opposite**, on a long stalk. **Bark:** grey-pink, flaking in plates. **Fruit:** paired winged "helicopter" keys. **Buds:** green, opposite. **Confusion:** field maple (smaller, lobes rounded, leaf stalk with milky sap, corky twigs), Norway maple (sharper lobes, milky sap). **Gives:** sap for syrup (weak but real), a clean white timber for kitchen boards and spoons, decent firewood, and seedlings by the thousand whether you want them or not.

### Field maple — *Acer campestre* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Acer_campestre|Field_maple|Maple)
A hedgerow tree: small five-lobed leaves with rounded lobes, opposite, turning butter-yellow in autumn; twigs often with corky wings; keys with wings in a straight line. Gives fine turning timber and good firewood.

### Hornbeam — *Carpinus betulus* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Carpinus_betulus|Hornbeam)
Like beech at a glance, but the **bark is grey with fluted, muscle-like ridges**, the leaf is doubly toothed and pleated, and the fruit is a nutlet under a three-lobed papery wing. Gives the hardest native timber (mallets, cogs, butchers' blocks) and the hottest firewood.

### Elm — *Ulmus* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Ulmus_glabra|Elm)
Now mostly hedgerow suckers and the surviving wych elm. **Leaf:** oval, rough as sandpaper above, doubly toothed, **lop-sided at the base** (one side of the leaf runs further down the stalk), alternate. **Fruit:** papery discs with the seed in the middle, April. **Gives:** timber that does not split (wheel hubs, chair seats, coffins, water pipes), poor firewood; the inner bark of slippery elm is a medicine; the seeds are edible in April.

### Poplar and aspen — *Populus* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Populus_tremula|Poplar|Aspen)
Tall, fast, by water; leaves triangular or round on **flattened stalks** so they tremble in any breeze (aspen). Gives nothing as food and almost nothing as fuel; matches, crates and fast shelter-belts.

### Holly — *Ilex aquifolium* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Ilex_aquifolium|Holly)
Evergreen, glossy spiny leaves (spineless higher up the tree, out of reach of browsers), red berries on female trees. **Berries are poisonous.** Gives winter fodder (it was cut for cattle for centuries), the whitest hardest timber, and firewood that burns green.

### Yew — *Taxus baccata* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Taxus_baccata|Yew)
Evergreen; flat dark-green needles in two rows; red-brown flaking bark; a red fleshy cup with a hole at the end holding one seed; churchyards, chalk downs, ancient. **Every part except the red flesh is deadly** — needles, seed, bark, wood dust — to people and to livestock, and cut branches are *more* poisonous as they wilt. The longbow tree. Know it to avoid it.

## The conifers

### Scots pine — *Pinus sylvestris* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Pinus_sylvestris|Scots_pine|Pine)
Needles **in pairs**, 4–7 cm, blue-green, twisted; bark **orange-red and flaking** in the upper trunk; small egg-shaped cones. **Gives:** pine needle tea (vitamin C — a scurvy cure), the inner bark dried and ground as famine flour, resin for waterproofing, glue and torches, "fatwood" (resin-soaked heartwood from stumps) that lights in any weather, pine nuts too small to bother with, and quick hot firewood.

### Larch — *Larix decidua* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Larix_decidua|Larch)
The only native-ish conifer that **drops its needles** in winter; soft needles in tufts on short shoots, golden in autumn; small upright cones. Gives durable timber for boats and fences, good firewood.

### Spruce and fir — *Picea*, *Abies* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Picea_abies|Spruce)
Spruce: needles single, sharp, square in section, on little pegs; cones hanging. Fir: needles flat, soft, with two white lines beneath, cones standing upright on the branch and falling apart on the tree. Both give spruce-tip tea and beer (the young growth in May), resin, and timber; poor, sparky firewood.

### Cedar, cypress, leylandii
Planted; flat scale-like leaves in sprays. Not food. Cypress and thuja are mildly poisonous; do not use them for tea.

## Reading a tree in winter

- **Opposite buds** (ash, sycamore, maple, elder, horse chestnut, dogwood): a short list — learn it and you have halved the problem.
- **Bud colour and shape:** black (ash), sticky brown (horse chestnut), long pointed copper (beech), green (sycamore), clustered at the tip (oak), a single hood-scale (willow), tiny and hairy (hazel, with catkins from January).
- **Bark:** white (birch), smooth grey (beech), muscled (hornbeam), horizontal bands (cherry), orange in the crown (Scots pine), corky and furrowed (elder, cork oak), spiral fissures (old chestnut).
- **What is on the ground:** acorn cups, beech husks, chestnut cases, ash keys, alder cones, sycamore keys, cherry stones.

## Coppice — the tree that never dies

Hazel, ash, oak, sweet chestnut, willow, lime, alder and hornbeam all **regrow from the stump** when cut near the ground in winter, and the regrowth is straight, useful and renewable every 7–25 years depending on the species and what you want: hazel rods at 7, chestnut fencing at 15, oak firewood at 25. A family's fuel and fencing came from a few acres of coppice for a thousand years; it is the single most useful thing to know about woodland. Cut in winter, leave a low clean stump, fence out deer and rabbits for the first three years, and cut a different section each year so the whole wood is a rotation.
