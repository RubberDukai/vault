# Wild plants — identification log

Each entry: **what to look for**, **what it can be mistaken for and how you tell**, **when**, **which part and how**, and a link to the encyclopedia article, which has the photographs. Read the lookalike's article too. The killers are at the top on purpose.

## The plants that kill — learn these before any edible

### Hemlock — *Conium maculatum* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Conium_maculatum|Hemlock|Poison_hemlock)
**Look for:** an umbellifer (flat-topped clusters of tiny white flowers) up to 2 m, with a **smooth, hairless, hollow stem blotched with purple**, finely divided fern-like leaves, and a **mousy, unpleasant smell** when crushed. Damp waste ground, riverbanks, verges. **Mistaken for:** wild carrot (hairy stem, carrot smell), cow parsley (grooved, hairy stem, earlier in the year), wild parsnip, chervil. **Tell:** purple blotches + no hairs + the smell. **A few leaves kill.** Rule: never eat any umbellifer you have not learnt individually, in flower, with certainty.

### Hemlock water-dropwort — *Oenanthe crocata* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Oenanthe_crocata|Hemlock_water-dropwort|Oenanthe)
**Look for:** an umbellifer of ditches, streams and wet meadows, 1–1.5 m, glossy bright-green celery-like leaves, white flowers in summer, and **roots like a bunch of pale parsnips ("dead man's fingers")** that ooze yellow juice and smell of parsley. Britain's most poisonous plant; a single root has killed adults who took it for wild parsnip or celery. **Tell:** the root cluster, the wet habitat, the yellow juice. Do not handle it with cut hands.

### Foxglove — *Digitalis purpurea* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Digitalis_purpurea|Foxglove)
**Look for:** in its first year, a rosette of soft, downy, grey-green oval leaves with a wrinkled surface and a winged stalk; in the second, the familiar spire of purple bells. Woodland edges, acid soil. **Mistaken for:** comfrey (rough, bristly leaves, on damp ground), borage (bristly, smells of cucumber), the leaves of great mullein (much woollier). **Tell:** foxglove is soft and downy, *never* bristly, and has no cucumber smell. Every part stops the heart.

### Lords-and-ladies — *Arum maculatum* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Arum_maculatum|Lords-and-ladies|Arum)
**Look for:** glossy **arrow-shaped** leaves, often black-spotted, in early spring in hedge bottoms and woods, often growing *through* wild garlic; later a pale hood, then a spike of scarlet berries. **Mistaken for:** wild garlic, sorrel. **Tell:** no smell of garlic when crushed; arrow shape with backward-pointing lobes; sorrel leaves are matt and taste sharply of lemon. The berries look like sweets to children; the sap burns the mouth.

### Deadly nightshade — *Atropa belladonna* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Atropa_belladonna|Deadly_nightshade)
**Look for:** a bushy plant to 1.5 m on chalk and disturbed ground, dull-purple bell flowers, then **a shiny black berry the size of a cherry, sitting in a green five-pointed calyx like a star**. **Mistaken for:** bilberry, black currant, cherry. **Tell:** the star; bilberries grow on knee-high heath shrubs, not chest-high bushes. Three berries can kill a child.

### Yew — *Taxus baccata* — see the tree log
Flat dark needles in two rows, a red fleshy cup with a hole in the end. The red flesh alone is harmless; seed, needle, bark and wood kill, quickly. Never let children near it, and never let livestock at clippings.

### Others to know by name
**Monkshood** (*Aconitum*, hooded blue flowers, deeply cut leaves — deadly to touch with broken skin); **cuckoo pint**'s cousin **bog arum**; **henbane** (sticky, foul-smelling, cream flowers netted purple); **hemlock's cousin cowbane** (*Cicuta*, wet places, deadly); **laburnum** pods (children mistake them for peas); **daffodil bulbs** (mistaken for onions in the kitchen — keep them apart); **rhubarb leaves**; **green potatoes**; **the seeds inside apple, cherry, plum and apricot stones** in quantity; **bracken** (carcinogenic, despite what old books say about fiddleheads); **ragwort** (liver, mostly for livestock); **buttercup** (blisters the mouth); **raw elder leaves, bark and unripe berries**.

## Greens

### Stinging nettle — *Urtica dioica* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Urtica_dioica|Stinging_nettle|Nettle)
**Look for:** everybody knows it; the sting confirms it. Opposite, toothed, heart-shaped leaves; square stem; grows in nitrogen-rich ground near people. **Mistaken for:** the dead-nettles (*Lamium*: no sting, flowers in whorls, also edible) — a harmless confusion. **When:** March to May for the tops; again after cutting; never after flowering (gritty crystals). **Use:** the top four to six leaves, gloves on; blanch 30 seconds and the sting is gone. Soup, purée, as spinach, tea, dried for winter. Among the most nutritious greens there are — iron, protein, vitamins A and C. The fibre makes cordage (see *Cloth, rope and leather*).

### Wild garlic, ramsons — *Allium ursinum* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Allium_ursinum|Wild_garlic|Ramsons|Allium)
**Look for:** carpets of broad, bright-green, spear-shaped leaves in damp woodland in March–May, starry white flowers in April, and above all **the smell of garlic** when a leaf is crushed. **Mistaken for:** lords-and-ladies (arrow-shaped, no smell — and it grows *in* the wild garlic patches), lily-of-the-valley (no smell, paired leaves, poisonous), autumn crocus leaves (no smell, poisonous). **Tell:** crush every leaf you pick and smell it. Every single one. **Use:** leaves raw or cooked, flowers, buds pickled like capers, bulbs sparingly.

### Sorrel — *Rumex acetosa* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Rumex_acetosa|Sorrel)
**Look for:** arrow-shaped leaves whose basal lobes point *backwards* down the stalk, matt green, in meadows and grassland; a sharp lemon taste. **Mistaken for:** lords-and-ladies (glossy, lobes not parallel to the stalk, no lemon taste, woodland). **Tell:** the taste, the matt surface, the habitat. **When:** March–June, then again in autumn. **Use:** raw in salads, soup, sauce for fish. Oxalic acid: not by the bowlful every day, and not for people with kidney stones.

### Dandelion — *Taraxacum* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Taraxacum|Dandelion)
**Look for:** the rosette of jagged leaves, one hollow unbranched flower stalk per flower with white latex, a single yellow flower. **Mistaken for:** cat's-ear and hawkbits (hairy leaves, branched or solid stalks — also edible), sow thistle (edible). No dangerous lookalike. **Use:** young leaves in spring (bitter; blanch under a pot for a week for pale sweet ones), roots roasted and ground as coffee in autumn, flowers for wine and fritters. Diuretic — the French name means wet-the-bed.

### Chickweed — *Stellaria media* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Stellaria_media|Chickweed)
**Look for:** a sprawling mat of small oval pointed leaves in opposite pairs, tiny white star flowers with five deeply notched petals, and the definitive tell: **a single line of hairs running up one side of the stem**, changing side at each leaf node. **Mistaken for:** scarlet pimpernel (square stem, red flowers, mildly poisonous — the line of hairs settles it), spurges (milky sap: never eat anything with milky sap unless you know it). **When:** all year, best in cool weather. **Use:** raw, by the handful, the mildest salad green there is.

### Fat hen — *Chenopodium album* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Chenopodium_album|Fat_hen|Chenopodium)
**Look for:** a garden weed to a metre, diamond-shaped toothed leaves with a **mealy white coating** on the young growth, green flower spikes. **Mistaken for:** its relatives (orache, good King Henry — all edible), and by the careless for black nightshade seedlings (shiny, no meal). **Use:** leaves as spinach June–September; seeds in autumn as a grain, like its cousin quinoa. Better than spinach and grows without being asked.

### Garlic mustard, jack-by-the-hedge — *Alliaria petiolata* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Alliaria_petiolata|Garlic_mustard)
**Look for:** heart-shaped, toothed leaves smelling of garlic when crushed, small white four-petalled flowers in April; hedge banks. No dangerous lookalike once you have the smell and the four petals (the cabbage family). **Use:** leaves raw or cooked March–May, seeds as a mustard.

### Hairy bittercress — *Cardamine hirsuta* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Cardamine_hirsuta|Hairy_bittercress|Cardamine)
Tiny rosettes of pinnate leaves in every plant pot and gravel path, white four-petalled flowers, explosive seed pods; tastes of cress. Winter and early spring. No dangerous lookalike. Eat by the pinch, raw.

### Cleavers, goosegrass — *Galium aparine* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Galium_aparine|Cleavers|Galium)
The sticky one that clings to clothes: square stems, whorls of narrow leaves, tiny hooks everywhere. No lookalike with hooks. Young shoots in March–April, cooked (the hooks vanish); the seeds roasted are the best coffee substitute in the hedge.

### Ground elder — *Aegopodium podagraria* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Aegopodium_podagraria|Ground_elder)
**Look for:** the gardener's curse: glossy leaves in threes-of-threes on a grooved triangular stalk, spreading by white roots, white umbels in June. It *is* an umbellifer, so learn it carefully: the leaf shape (three leaflets, each sometimes divided in three), the triangular stalk, the fact that it grows in dense patches. **Mistaken for:** other umbellifers including hemlock — but hemlock's leaves are fern-fine, not in broad threes. **Use:** young leaves before flowering, cooked like spinach with a taste of parsley and celery.

### Common hogweed — *Heracleum sphondylium* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Heracleum_sphondylium|Hogweed)
**Look for:** a large hairy umbellifer, hollow ridged hairy stem, big lobed leaves like a coarse hand, pinkish-white flat umbels. **Mistaken for:** **giant hogweed** (*H. mantegazzianum* — 3–5 m, purple-blotched stem, causes burns that blister in sunlight) and **hemlock**. **Tell:** common hogweed is hairy and blotch-free and never above 2 m; hemlock is smooth and blotched. **Use:** only the young unopened shoots in March–April, cooked (steamed, then fried in butter — a real delicacy). The sap sensitises skin to sunlight; wear gloves, and if you are not certain, this is the one to leave.

### Plantain — *Plantago major* and *P. lanceolata* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Plantago_major|Plantain|Plantago)
Rosettes of ribbed leaves in every lawn and path (broad-leaved) or field (ribwort, narrow leaves). No dangerous lookalike. Young leaves cooked; the seeds; and the leaf crushed onto a nettle sting, insect bite or small wound — a genuinely useful wound herb.

### Wintercress, land cress — *Barbarea vulgaris* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Barbarea_vulgaris|Wintercress|Barbarea)
Glossy dark rosettes of lobed leaves with a big end lobe, yellow four-petalled flowers in April; damp ground. Peppery. Cabbage family — the four petals and the taste confirm it. Winter and spring.

### Sea beet — *Beta vulgaris* subsp. *maritima* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Sea_beet|Beet|Beetroot)
The wild ancestor of beetroot and chard: glossy, dark, fleshy leaves on shingle and sea walls, all year. No dangerous lookalike on the strandline. The best wild green in the country; cook like chard.

### Sea kale — *Crambe maritima* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Crambe_maritima|Sea_kale|Crambe)
Blue-grey cabbage-like leaves on shingle beaches, white flowers in June. Young shoots in spring (traditionally blanched under the shingle), leaves, flower heads like broccoli. Protected on some beaches; take a little.

### Marsh samphire, glasswort — *Salicornia europaea* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Salicornia_europaea|Samphire|Glasswort|Salicornia)
Jointed, leafless, succulent green stems on saltmarsh mud, June–August. No lookalike. Steam or boil briefly, eat with butter, pulling the flesh off the central thread with your teeth.

### Rock samphire — *Crithmum maritimum* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Crithmum_maritimum|Rock_samphire)
Fleshy, grey-green, divided leaves on sea cliffs and rocks, yellow-green umbels, a strong resinous-citrus smell. An umbellifer, but the habitat and the fleshy leaves are unmistakable. Pickled, or cooked.

### Alexanders — *Smyrnium olusatrum* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Smyrnium_olusatrum|Alexanders)
Glossy yellow-green celery-like leaves in threes, yellow-green umbels, near the coast, from midwinter. An umbellifer: learn it with care alongside hemlock. Tell: glossy, in threes, the celery-myrrh smell, black seeds. Shoots and stems in winter–spring, cooked.

## Roots

### Burdock — *Arctium* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Arctium|Burdock)
**Look for:** huge rhubarb-like leaves, downy-white underneath, in the first year; burrs in the second. **Mistaken for:** butterbur (kidney-shaped leaves, wet ground, not for eating), rhubarb. **Tell:** the white felted underside and the burrs nearby from last year. **Use:** first-year roots dug in autumn or early spring, a hard day's work each, scrubbed and cooked like a parsnip. The Japanese gobo.

### Pignut — *Conopodium majus* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Conopodium_majus|Pignut)
A small delicate umbellifer of old grassland and open woods, fine feathery leaves, tiny white umbels in May; follow the thread-thin stem down with a finger to a brown tuber the size of a hazelnut, tasting of raw hazelnut and celery. Umbellifer rule applies: learn it in flower, with someone who knows.

### Dandelion, wild carrot, silverweed, and the rest
Wild carrot (*Daucus carota* — hairy stem, carrot smell, a single dark floret in the centre of the umbel, bristly bird's-nest seed head) is the one edible umbellifer root, tiny and woody, and it grows beside hemlock: leave it unless you are expert. Silverweed roots (*Potentilla anserina*, silver-backed leaves on damp tracks) were a staple in the Hebrides — small, starchy, dug in autumn. Bulrush/reedmace (*Typha*) rhizomes are a genuine wild starch, peeled and roasted or pounded for flour, from clean water only.

## Flowers

**Elderflower** (June — see the tree log), **hawthorn** (May, eaten raw in the field), **lime** (June–July, dried for tea, see the tree log), **gorse** (all year, coconut-scented, in wine and tea), **dandelion**, **wild rose** (petals in June, then the hips), **red and white clover** (*Trifolium*, the flower heads dried for tea or in fritters), **meadowsweet** (*Filipendula ulmaria*, creamy scented plumes by ditches in July — flavour for cordial, and the original source of aspirin, which means not for people who cannot take aspirin), **primrose and violet** (a few, for salads). Do not eat flowers you cannot name; buttercups, foxgloves and monkshood are flowers too.

## Fruit and berries

### Blackberry — *Rubus fruticosus* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Rubus_fruticosus|Blackberry)
No introduction needed and no dangerous lookalike among the thorny brambles. August–early October. Fresh, jam, jelly, wine, dried. The young shoots in spring, peeled, are edible too.

### Bilberry, whortleberry — *Vaccinium myrtillus* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Vaccinium_myrtillus|Bilberry)
Knee-high shrubs on moor and heath, small oval bright-green leaves, blue-black berries with a bloom, staining fingers purple, July–August. **Mistaken for:** deadly nightshade — but that is a chest-high bush with cherry-sized shiny berries in a star, on chalk, not a knee-high moorland shrub. **Tell:** habitat and size. Cowberry and crowberry grow alongside (both edible, sharper).

### Elderberry — *Sambucus nigra* — see the tree log
Ripe black clusters in September. **Cooked only** — raw berries, and everything green, cause vomiting. Syrup (the winter cold remedy), wine, jelly.

### Sloe — *Prunus spinosa* — see the tree log
Blue-black, plum-like, mouth-puckering until frost. Gin, jelly, wine. **Mistaken for:** buckthorn berries (purgative — buckthorn has no thorns at the tips of the twigs and its berries are in clusters along the twig, not single).

### Hawthorn — *Crataegus monogyna* — see the tree log
Red haws in September–November, mealy, apple-flavoured; jelly, ketchup, "fruit leather". Leaves in spring. Seeds not eaten.

### Rosehip — *Rosa canina* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Rosa_canina|Dog_rose|Rose_hip|Rose)
Scarlet oval hips on thorny arching stems, September–December. Twenty times the vitamin C of an orange. Syrup (the wartime child's vitamin supplement): simmer, strain through cloth **twice** — the seed hairs are an irritant (they are the itching powder of school memory).

### Rowan — *Sorbus aucuparia* — see the tree log
Orange-red clusters in August–September, bitter; jelly with crab apple for game. Cook them.

### Crab apple — *Malus sylvestris* — see the tree log
Small hard sour apples, September–October; pectin for setting every other jelly; verjuice; cider.

### Wild strawberry, raspberry, cherry, plum, bullace, damson
All recognisable from their garden relatives; the wild forms are smaller and better. Cherry plum (*Prunus cerasifera*) is the earliest tree fruit in July.

### Do not eat
Bryony berries (red, on a climbing vine with tendrils, in hedges — deadly); spindle (pink four-lobed capsules with orange seeds); privet; ivy; holly; mistletoe; honeysuckle; snowberry; laurel; cotoneaster and pyracantha (not deadly, but not food); any white berry at all.

## Nuts and seeds — the calories

### Hazel — *Corylus avellana* — see the tree log
The one wild food dense enough to matter. Green in August, brown and falling in September — the squirrels take everything, so pick green and ripen indoors. Store in the shell, dry. Milk, butter, flour, or simply eaten.

### Sweet chestnut — *Castanea sativa* — see the tree log
October, in prickly green hedgehog cases. **Mistaken for:** horse chestnut (conker — smooth-ish case with few spines, one big glossy nut, *not food*). **Tell:** sweet chestnut cases are dense with fine spines like a hedgehog and hold two or three flattened nuts with a tassel; the tree has long saw-toothed single leaves, not hand-shaped leaves in fives. Roast, boil, dry and grind for flour: a bread staple in the Cévennes and Corsica for centuries.

### Acorn — *Quercus* — see the tree log
Tannin-bitter and unusable raw, but a staple of half the world's peoples once **leached**: shell, grind, and soak in several changes of cold water over a few days (or simmer in changes of hot water) until the water runs clear and the meal no longer tastes bitter. Then dry and use as flour or porridge. A mature oak can drop 50 kg in a mast year.

### Beech mast — *Fagus sylvatica* — see the tree log
Small triangular nuts in bristly husks in a good year, September–October; peel and eat, or press for oil. Only every few years, and a small handful is enough — large amounts are mildly toxic raw.

### Walnut — *Juglans regia* — see the tree log
Where planted. Green in July for pickling; ripe in October, hulled (stains everything), dried.

### Other seeds
Fat hen and its relatives; nettle seed (dried, sprinkled — a real tonic); plantain; dock seed (winnowed and ground, bitter); wild oats and grasses in general — nearly all grass seed is edible if threshed, and all cereals are grasses; **but not** ergot-infected heads (purple-black spurs replacing the grain: deadly, and the cause of medieval "St Anthony's fire").

## Seaweeds

No British seaweed is poisonous. Take from clean water on a falling tide, cut rather than pull so the holdfast regrows, rinse, and dry for storage — dried seaweed keeps for years.

- **Dulse** (*Palmaria palmata*, [encyclopedia](wiki:Palmaria_palmata|Dulse)) — flat red-purple fronds; eaten raw, dried, or fried crisp.
- **Laver** (*Porphyra*, [encyclopedia](wiki:Porphyra|Laver_(seaweed)|Nori)) — thin dark-purple sheets on rocks; the Welsh laverbread (boiled for hours to a paste) and the Japanese nori.
- **Sea lettuce** (*Ulva lactuca*, [encyclopedia](wiki:Ulva_lactuca|Sea_lettuce|Seaweed)) — bright green translucent sheets; salads, soup.
- **Kelp / oarweed** (*Laminaria*, [encyclopedia](wiki:Laminaria_digitata|Kelp)) — the big brown straps at the lowest tides; stock, dried and powdered as a salt substitute rich in iodine, and the kombu that makes dashi.
- **Carrageen / Irish moss** (*Chondrus crispus*, [encyclopedia](wiki:Chondrus_crispus|Carrageen_moss|Carrageenan|Seaweed)) — small branching red-purple fronds; dried and boiled in milk it sets into a pudding — a wild gelatine.
- **Pepper dulse** (*Osmundea pinnatifida*) — small, feathery, tastes of pepper and truffle; a seasoning.
- **Gutweed** (*Ulva intestinalis*) — bright green tubes on the upper shore; fried crisp.

## A note on quantity

Wild greens are watery and low in calories; nuts, acorns, chestnuts and seaweed are the foods that add up. A family in a hard year lives on the garden, the animals and the stores, and the hedgerow keeps them healthy while they do. Learn it for that.
