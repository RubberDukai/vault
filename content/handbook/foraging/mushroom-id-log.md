# Mushrooms — identification log

Mushrooms are the one wild food where a mistake is routinely fatal and the symptoms arrive after it is too late to act: the death cap gives you a good night's sleep and kills your liver three days later. So the rules here are stricter than for plants:

1. **Learn the deadly species until you could pick them out of a basket in the dark.** They are described first.
2. **Learn each edible one at a time, from a living specimen, ideally with someone who knows.** Not from a photograph.
3. **Check every feature every time**: cap, gills or pores, stem, ring, base (dig it up whole), flesh colour and colour change when cut, smell, habitat, what tree it is under, and — for anything with gills — the **spore print**.
4. **Only eat mushrooms that are fresh, firm and clean**, cooked (very few are safe raw), a little the first time, and keep one specimen back uncooked in the fridge so that if anyone is ill, the hospital can identify it.
5. **No mushroom is worth the risk to a child.** Teach children that no wild mushroom is ever eaten without an adult who is certain.

## The spore print

Cut the stem off a mature cap, lay the cap gills-down half on white paper and half on black (or on glass), cover with a bowl, leave two to six hours. The dust left behind is the spore colour: **white** (the deadly *Amanita* family, but also many edibles), **pink**, **brown**, **rusty-brown** (webcaps — deadly), **purple-brown** (field mushrooms), **black** (inkcaps). It is the single most reliable feature there is, and it rules out the *Amanita* / field-mushroom confusion in one step.

## The killers

### Death cap — *Amanita phalloides* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Amanita_phalloides|Death_cap)
Responsible for most fatal poisonings in the world. **Look for:** cap 5–15 cm, **olive-green to yellowish, sometimes almost white**, smooth, slightly sticky, often with faint radiating streaks; **gills white**, free of the stem; stem white with a **skirt-like ring**; and, at the base — dig it up — a **white bag (volva)** the stem sits in like an egg in a cup. Smells sweetish, then sickly. Under oak, beech, hornbeam, hazel, from August to November. Spore print **white**. **Mistaken for:** field and horse mushrooms (spore print purple-brown, gills pink then brown, no volva), young puffballs (cut one in half: a solid white interior with no cap-and-stem outline inside), edible green-capped *Russula* (no ring, no volva, brittle flesh), paddy-straw mushrooms by people from south-east Asia (the commonest fatal confusion in Europe). **Half a cap kills an adult.** Symptoms 6–24 hours later: violent vomiting and diarrhoea, then apparent recovery, then liver failure on day three.

### Destroying angel — *Amanita virosa* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Amanita_virosa|Destroying_angel|Amanita)
**All white**: conical then flat cap, white gills, tall white stem, shaggy ring, **bag at the base**; in birch and mixed woods, August–November. Smells sickly-sweet. As deadly as the death cap. **Mistaken for:** field mushroom (pink-brown gills, no bag), white puffballs, horse mushroom. Any all-white gilled mushroom with a ring is left alone unless you have dug the base and found no volva and taken a spore print that is not white.

### Fool's funnel — *Clitocybe rivulosa* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Clitocybe_rivulosa|Clitocybe|Mushroom_poisoning)
Small white-to-buff funnel-shaped caps in rings in lawns and pasture, decurrent white gills, no ring, mealy smell. Muscarine: sweating, salivation, collapse. **Mistaken for:** fairy ring champignon (*Marasmius oreades*, which is edible and grows in the same rings — tan cap with a central bump, gills widely spaced and *not* running down the stem, tough stem). **Tell:** the gill attachment and the colour. Beginners should leave the fairy ring alone.

### Deadly webcap and fool's webcap — *Cortinarius rubellus*, *C. orellanus* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Cortinarius_rubellus|Cortinarius|Webcap|Mushroom_poisoning)
**Look for:** tawny-orange to rusty-brown caps and stems, **rusty-brown gills**, a cobweb-like veil between cap edge and stem in young specimens, in conifer or birch woods on acid soil. Spore print **rust-brown**. Kidney failure that begins days to weeks after eating. **Mistaken for:** chanterelle. **Tell:** a chanterelle has *no true gills* — blunt forking ridges running down a funnel — and smells of apricots; a webcap has real, sharp-edged, rusty gills and a proper cap-on-stem shape. Rule: never eat any small-to-medium brown mushroom with brown gills. There are hundreds, many are poisonous, and none is worth it.

### Funeral bell — *Galerina marginata* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Galerina_marginata|Galerina|Mushroom_poisoning)
Small, honey-brown, with a **ring** on the stem, in clusters on rotting wood, autumn. Same toxins as the death cap. **Mistaken for:** honey fungus (larger, usually in big clusters at the base of living trees, spore print *white* — funeral bell's is rust-brown), velvet shank (no ring, a dark velvety stem, grows in winter), sheathed woodtuft (an edible that experts confuse with it — so nobody else should try).

### False morel — *Gyromitra esculenta* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Gyromitra_esculenta|False_morel|Gyromitra)
A reddish-brown **brain-shaped** lump on a short stem under conifers on sand in spring. **Mistaken for:** morel. **Tell:** a true morel is a **honeycomb of pits and ridges**, hollow all the way through when cut lengthways, on a hollow pale stem; a false morel is a folded, lobed brain, chambered inside, not hollow. False morel is eaten in Finland after repeated boiling; it also kills people in Finland. Leave it.

### Others to know
**Panther cap** (*Amanita pantherina*: brown cap with pure white warts, white gills, ring, volva — under beech; can kill). **Fly agaric** (the red one with white spots — not usually fatal but you will wish it were). **Yellow stainer** (*Agaricus xanthodermus*: looks exactly like a field mushroom but **the base of the stem and the cap edge turn bright chrome-yellow when cut or rubbed**, and it smells of ink or hospitals; painful poisoning). **Brown roll-rim** (*Paxillus involutus*: brown, in-rolled margin, gills that peel off in a sheet — once eaten, now known to kill through a delayed immune reaction). **Sulphur tuft** (bright yellow clusters on stumps, bitter, greenish gills). **Livid pinkgill / lead poisoner** (*Entoloma sinuatum*: large grey-white cap, pink gills, mealy smell, in grassland — severe). **Jack o'lantern** (*Omphalotus*: orange, in clusters on stumps, true gills — mistaken for chanterelle).

## The safe start — the "foolproof" few

These have no deadly lookalike *if you check every feature listed.* Start here and stay here for your first year.

### Giant puffball — *Calvatia gigantea* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Calvatia_gigantea|Giant_puffball|Puffball)
A white ball the size of a football (or a head) in pasture and nettle beds, July–October. **Check:** cut it in half from top to bottom: the inside must be **pure white and uniform like a marshmallow, with no outline of a cap, gills or stem** inside it (which would make it an *Amanita* egg). Yellowing inside means it is past it. Slice and fry in butter. Smaller puffballs (*Lycoperdon*) are edible with the same cut-in-half test, but the earthballs (*Scleroderma*, hard, scaly, purple-black inside) are poisonous — the test finds them.

### Hedgehog fungus — *Hydnum repandum* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Hydnum_repandum|Hedgehog_mushroom|Hydnum)
A pale buff-cream cap with **soft spines instead of gills** underneath, like a tiny hedgehog, in deciduous and conifer woods, August–November. Nothing poisonous has spines. Mild, firm, excellent.

### Chicken of the woods — *Laetiporus sulphureus* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Laetiporus_sulphureus|Chicken_of_the_woods|Laetiporus)
Bright **sulphur-yellow and orange fan-shaped brackets** in tiers on oak, sweet chestnut, willow, cherry, June–September; **pores** underneath, not gills. Nothing else is that colour. Take the young soft edges; cook thoroughly; some people react to it, especially from yew or eucalyptus — never take it from yew.

### Cauliflower fungus — *Sparassis crispa* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Sparassis_crispa|Cauliflower_fungus|Sparassis)
A creamy-white **frilly ball like a brain coral or a cauliflower** at the base of a pine, August–November. Unmistakable. Wash it well — it is full of needles and insects.

### Jelly ear — *Auricularia auricula-judae* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Auricularia_auricula-judae|Jelly_ear|Wood_ear|Auricularia)
Brown, rubbery, ear-shaped, on dead and living elder all year, best in winter. No lookalike worth worrying about. Dried it keeps for years; the Chinese wood ear.

### Dryad's saddle — *Cerioporus squamosus* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Cerioporus_squamosus|Dryad's_saddle|Polyporus_squamosus)
A huge fan-shaped bracket with **brown scales like a pheasant's back** on ash, elm, sycamore, in May–June; pores underneath; smells of cucumber or watermelon. Only the young soft edge is worth eating.

## The next tier — learn one at a time

### Chanterelle — *Cantharellus cibarius* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Cantharellus_cibarius|Chanterelle)
**Look for:** egg-yellow, funnel-shaped, with **blunt forking ridges** (not sharp gills) running down the stem; flesh pale inside; **smell of apricots**; in mossy birch, beech and oak woods, July–November. **Lookalikes:** false chanterelle (*Hygrophoropsis*: thinner, more orange, *true* soft gills that fork, no apricot smell — not deadly but not food), jack o'lantern (on wood, in clusters, true gills), **deadly webcap** (brown gills, cobweb veil, cap-on-stem shape). **Tell:** ridges not gills; growing from the ground not wood; the smell.

### Cep, penny bun — *Boletus edulis* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Boletus_edulis|Cep|Porcini|Boletus)
**Look for:** a brown bun-like cap, **sponge of pores** underneath (white, then yellow-olive), a fat pale stem with a fine white net pattern near the top, flesh white and **not changing colour** when cut; under beech, oak, pine, August–October. **Lookalikes:** bitter bolete (*Tylopilus felleus*: pinkish pores, dark net on the stem, unbearably bitter — touch the pores to your tongue), devil's bolete and the red-pored boletes (red or orange pores, blue-staining flesh — poisonous). **Rule for beginners:** any bolete with **red pores** or flesh that **turns blue** is left alone; any bolete with white-to-yellow pores that does not change colour and is not bitter is safe. That rule alone gives you the ceps, bay boletes and birch boletes.

### Field mushroom and horse mushroom — *Agaricus campestris*, *A. arvensis* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Agaricus_campestris|Field_mushroom|Agaricus)
The shop mushroom's wild relatives, in old pasture, August–October. **Look for:** white cap, gills **pink in youth turning chocolate-brown**, a ring, **no bag at the base**, spore print purple-brown; horse mushroom is larger, creamy, smells of aniseed, and bruises faintly yellow *slowly* (not chrome-yellow instantly). **Lookalikes:** **death cap and destroying angel** (white gills, volva — dig it up), **yellow stainer** (instant chrome-yellow at the stem base, ink smell). **Tell:** gill colour (never eat a white-gilled "field mushroom"), the base, the stain test.

### Parasol — *Macrolepiota procera* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Macrolepiota_procera|Parasol_mushroom|Macrolepiota)
Tall — the cap opens to 25 cm on a stem up to 30 cm — with a **snakeskin-patterned stem**, shaggy brown scales on a pale cap with a central bump, a **double ring that slides up and down the stem**, white gills, in grassland at wood edges, July–October. **Lookalikes:** shaggy parasol (bruises red, upsets some people), the *Amanitas* (volva, ring does not slide, no snakeskin), and the small poisonous *Lepiota* species — so **never eat a parasol smaller than a hand-span** across the open cap.

### Shaggy inkcap, lawyer's wig — *Coprinus comatus* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Coprinus_comatus|Shaggy_ink_cap|Coprinus)
A tall white shaggy egg on lawns, verges and disturbed ground, which dissolves into black ink from the rim upward within a day. Eat it young, white and closed, the same day. **Lookalike:** common inkcap (*Coprinopsis atramentaria*, smooth grey — poisonous with alcohol for days afterwards). Tell: shaggy versus smooth.

### Oyster mushroom — *Pleurotus ostreatus* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Pleurotus_ostreatus|Oyster_mushroom|Pleurotus)
Grey to blue-grey **shelf-shaped caps in tiers** on dead beech and other hardwoods, gills running down the short off-centre stem, white spore print, all year but especially autumn–winter. **Lookalikes:** angel wings (on conifers — avoid anything oyster-like on conifer wood), and the small tough *Crepidotus* (brown spore print). Cultivate it: see *Growing food*.

### Wood blewit — *Lepista nuda* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Lepista_nuda|Wood_blewit|Blewit|Lepista)
Lilac-blue when young, fading to tan, with a **lilac stem**, pale pink spore print, in leaf litter and compost heaps, October–December, after the first frosts. Must be cooked. **Lookalikes:** the lilac *Cortinarius* webcaps (rust-brown spore print, cobweb veil) — the spore print settles it every time.

### Morel — *Morchella esculenta* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Morchella_esculenta|Morel|Morchella)
A honeycomb of pits on a pale hollow stem, **hollow all the way through** when cut lengthways, on chalk, in orchards, under ash, on disturbed and burnt ground, March–May. **Lookalike:** false morel (brain-shaped, chambered, not hollow). Always cooked; never with alcohol the first time.

### St George's mushroom — *Calocybe gambosa* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Calocybe_gambosa|St._George's_mushroom|Calocybe)
Creamy-white, crowded white gills, thick stem, a **strong smell of fresh meal or cucumber**, in rings in grassland and hedge banks from late April to June — almost nothing else edible fruits then. **Lookalikes:** the livid pinkgill (pink gills, later in the year) and fool's funnel — the smell, the season and the crowded white gills identify it.

### Beefsteak fungus — *Fistulina hepatica* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Fistulina_hepatica|Beefsteak_fungus|Fistulina)
A liver-coloured, tongue-shaped bracket on oak and chestnut that **bleeds red juice** when cut and has a marbled red flesh like raw beef, August–October. Unmistakable. Sour; sliced thin and fried, or raw in salads.

### Honey fungus — *Armillaria mellea* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Armillaria_mellea|Honey_fungus|Armillaria)
Dense clusters of honey-brown caps with a ring, at the base of trees it is killing, September–November; white spore print. Edible when well cooked but upsets many people; and its lookalike is the **funeral bell** (rust-brown spore print). One for the experienced.

### Velvet shank — *Flammulina velutipes* — [encyclopedia](wiki:Flammulina_velutipes|Enokitake|Velvet_shank|Flammulina)
Orange-tan sticky caps in clusters on dead elm, ash and gorse **in the depths of winter**, with a **dark velvety stem** and no ring. The Japanese enoki. **Lookalike:** funeral bell (ring, rust spore print, autumn) — the velvet stem and the white spore print separate them.

## Preserving the harvest

Dry sliced mushrooms on strings or racks in a warm airy place (ceps, hedgehogs, jelly ear, oysters dry best) and store in jars; rehydrate in warm water and keep the liquor. Duxelles (chopped, cooked down with onion in butter) freezes or keeps a week. Pickled in vinegar. Never store fresh wild mushrooms in plastic, and never keep them more than two days.

## If someone has eaten the wrong one

Get any remaining specimen and any vomit into a bag for identification. Hospital, at once, whatever the symptoms — and especially if there are **no symptoms**, because the deadly ones are the slow ones. If there is no hospital: induce vomiting only within the first hour; give fluids; activated charcoal if you have it; and for suspected *Amanita* poisoning, the delayed liver phase is the killer and the only known treatments (silibinin, penicillin in high doses, N-acetylcysteine) are hospital drugs. That is the honest position and the reason for every rule above.
