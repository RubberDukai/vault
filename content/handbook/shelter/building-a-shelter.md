# Building a shelter

Every shelter, from a pile of leaves to a cathedral, solves the same four problems: **keep the rain off, keep the wind out, keep the heat in, and don't fall down.** Get those in that order. A roof matters before walls; walls matter before comfort.

## Tonight: emergency shelter

Build small. A shelter you can barely fit in is one your body can heat.

**The debris hut** — the best natural shelter for one person in a wooded temperate climate:

1. A **ridgepole** about one and a half times your height, one end on the ground, the other propped at hip height on a stump, a fork, or two crossed sticks lashed together.
2. **Ribs** leaned against it on both sides at about 45°, close enough that debris cannot fall through — a hand's width apart, then a lattice of finer sticks across them.
3. **Debris** — leaves, bracken, grass, pine needles — heaped on until it is **at least 60 cm thick everywhere**. Push a stick through and check. Thin spots leak heat and rain. The heap should look absurdly large.
4. **A bed inside**, 30 cm of the same material compressed by lying on it. The ground steals more heat than the air.
5. **Plug the entrance** with your pack or a bundle of debris from inside.

Two hours of work with no tools. It will keep a person alive at temperatures well below freezing.

**A tarp** is the most useful single item you can carry. Three configurations cover almost everything:

- **A-frame** over a ridgeline between two trees — best in rain, sheds water both sides.
- **Lean-to** — one edge staked to the ground, the other on a ridgeline, open side facing away from the wind with a fire and a **reflector wall** of logs in front. Best for warmth.
- **Plough point** — one corner staked into the wind, the opposite corner raised. Fastest to pitch, very wind-stable.

Whatever the shape: **site it out of the wind, off low ground where cold air and water collect, away from dead standing trees, and not in a dry streambed.** Dry streambeds are dry until they are not.

## The principles that make a building last

An architect's rule from before there were architects: **give a building a good hat and good boots.** Almost every building failure is water, and it enters at the top or at the bottom.

- **A big roof overhang** — 45 cm or more — keeps rain off the walls. Earth and lime walls that would dissolve in a year survive centuries under a generous roof.
- **A raised, drained base** — a plinth of stone or rubble at least 30 cm high — keeps ground water and splash away from the wall base.
- **Slope the ground away** from the building on every side. Dig a shallow drainage ditch on the uphill side.
- **Ventilate.** Damp air must leave, or it condenses in the structure and rots it.

Roof pitch is dictated by the covering, not by preference. **Thatch needs 45° or steeper**, plain tiles 35°, slate 25–30°, corrugated metal or bitumen sheet can go down to 10°. Shallower than the material allows and it leaks, always.

## Foundations

Dig to **firm, undisturbed ground**, and below the depth frost reaches — about 45 cm in lowland Britain, a metre or more where winters are hard. Frost heaving lifts a shallow foundation unevenly and cracks everything above it.

The simplest sound foundation for a small building is a **rubble trench**: a trench 45–60 cm deep, filled with compacted stone or rubble, with a land drain pipe at the bottom leading downhill to daylight. Water that gets in runs out. Build the plinth on top of it. This is how most vernacular buildings in the world stand, and it needs no cement.

Compact in layers. Loose fill settles, and it settles unevenly.

## Walls — three ways with no factory

**Cob** — earth, sand, straw and water, mixed by treading and built up in lifts by hand. The oldest and simplest permanent wall there is. Thousands of British cob houses are five hundred years old.

- The subsoil needs **clay** in it — roll a ball, it should hold; a ribbon of it should bend without breaking. Test by shaking a jar of soil and water and reading the layers as they settle: you want roughly 20–30% clay.
- Mix by volume roughly **1 part clay soil to 2 or 3 parts sharp sand**, plus a generous armful of straw per barrow, adjusted until a dropped ball neither splats nor shatters.
- Build **lifts of 30–45 cm**, let each firm up for a day or two, and trim the sides back to plumb with a spade while it is still soft.
- Walls are **45–60 cm thick**. That mass is what makes cob houses warm in winter and cool in summer.
- **It must dry** — weeks per lift in summer — and it must never be sealed with cement render, which traps moisture and destroys it. Lime render or limewash, nothing else.

**Earthbag** — polypropylene sacks or a continuous tube, filled with moist subsoil, laid in courses like giant bricks, tamped hard, with two strands of **barbed wire between every course** as mortar and tension. Fast, forgiving, resists earthquakes and floods, and the bags can be salvaged feed and grain sacks. Plaster it, because sunlight destroys the polypropylene in a year or two.

**Timber frame** — posts and beams pegged together, walls infilled with anything: wattle and daub, straw bales, cob, planks. Green oak is the traditional British material because it cuts easily when fresh and hardens as it dries. Roundwood — whole thinned poles — is far stronger for its weight than sawn timber and needs no mill.

The joints that matter: **mortise and tenon** pegged with a dry hardwood peg, and **lap joints** where members cross. Triangulate every frame with diagonal braces — a rectangle is a hinge, a triangle is rigid.

## Roof structure

The roof carries its own weight, a covering, snow, and wind trying to lift it off. Wind uplift is the one people forget, and it is why roofs end up in fields.

- **Rafters** run from ridge to eaves. Their spacing depends on the covering and the span; 60 cm apart is a safe habit for small buildings.
- **Purlins** run horizontally under the rafters at mid-span and halve the effective rafter length, which is how you roof a wide space with short timbers.
- **Collar ties** across each pair of rafters stop them spreading outward and pushing the walls over. Skip these and the walls bow.
- **Tie the roof down** to the walls, and the walls to the foundation. Straps, wire, long pegs through the wall plate — anything continuous from ground to ridge.

For coverings with no shop: **thatch** (reed, straw, heather, rushes — a huge skill but enormously durable when done right), **split shingles** of oak or cedar, **turf on birch bark**, **slate** where it can be quarried, and **salvaged metal sheet** which is the fastest and most watertight of all of them.

## Insulation and mass — different jobs

**Insulation slows heat leaving.** It is trapped air: straw, wool, dry leaves, sawdust, feathers. A straw bale wall insulates about as well as a very good modern wall.

**Mass stores heat.** Stone, brick, earth, water. It soaks up warmth from the sun or the fire and releases it slowly. A thick cob wall is a battery, not a blanket.

A good building has **mass inside, insulation outside** — heavy internal walls and floors that stay warm, wrapped in a light insulating shell. Get this backwards and you spend the day heating a cold stone wall that dumps its heat outside at night.

## The small things that decide whether it works

- **Chimney and stove first.** A house with no way to burn fuel safely is a tent with walls. See the fire module before you close in the roof.
- **A door on the lee side**, with a porch or windbreak so opening it does not empty the room of heat.
- **Small windows**, and fewer of them, facing the sun. Glass loses heat ten times faster than wall. Shutters at night.
- **Floor**: raised timber over a ventilated void, or rammed earth sealed with oil, or stone flags on sand. Anything but bare soil.
- **Gutters.** Unglamorous, and the difference between a dry plinth and a rotting one.

## Where the real knowledge is

This is an outline, not a manual. The full craft is in Wikibooks — building, carpentry and cob — and in Appropedia, both of which belong in your library. And it is in the hands of anyone who has done it, which is why the best preparation is to **build something small now**: a shed, a coop, a wall. The mistakes you make on a chicken house are cheap. The same mistakes on a home are not.
