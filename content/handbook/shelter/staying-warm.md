# Staying warm and dry

Order of urgency in a genuine emergency: **air, bleeding, shelter, water, food.** Shelter comes third, ahead of water, because exposure kills in hours where thirst takes days.

## The four ways you lose heat

Every warmth decision follows from these:

1. **Conduction** — touching something colder. The ground is the big one; it will drain you all night through a thin mat. Insulate *underneath* before you worry about blankets on top.
2. **Convection** — moving air stripping warmth away. Wind, and draughts. A windproof layer is worth more than a thick one in a breeze.
3. **Radiation** — heat leaving your body as infrared, mostly from the head, neck and torso. Cover them.
4. **Evaporation** — water taking energy with it as it turns to vapour. This is why wet is so dangerous, and why sweating in the cold is a trap.

## Insulation is trapped air

Materials do not keep you warm. **Trapped air kept still does.** Down, wool, fleece, straw, leaves, feathers, crumpled newspaper — they all work by holding air in place. Which means:

- **Compressed insulation does not insulate.** The layer you are lying on is doing almost nothing. Loft is everything.
- **Wet insulation does not insulate**, because water conducts heat about 25 times better than air. This is the whole reason wet cold is so much more dangerous than dry cold.
- **Loose and fluffy beats thick and packed.**

## Clothing that works

Three layers, each with a job:

- **Base** — moves moisture off the skin. Merino wool or synthetic. **Never cotton against the skin in the cold.** Cotton absorbs and holds water and then conducts your heat straight out; the saying "cotton kills" is not hyperbole.
- **Mid** — the insulation. Wool, fleece, down. Add or remove layers to regulate.
- **Shell** — blocks wind and rain. Ideally breathable, but windproof matters more than waterproof if you must choose.

**Wool is the material to prioritise.** It insulates when damp, resists odour, is naturally flame-resistant and does not melt onto skin the way synthetics do near a fire.

**Regulate before you sweat.** Sweat soaks your insulation and then chills you badly when you stop moving. Open up, remove a layer, or slow down *before* you get hot. Take a layer off to work; put it back on the moment you stop.

Extremities: hands, feet and head. Mittens beat gloves. Spare dry socks are worth their weight in almost anything.

## Shelter, smallest first

The mistake is building too big. **Your body is the heat source**, and a small space you can actually warm beats a large one you cannot.

- **Get off the ground.** A thick bed of dry leaves, bracken, spruce boughs, straw or cardboard. Aim for 20–30 cm of loose material; it will compress to a fraction of that.
- **Block the wind.** A wall on the windward side, a hollow, a fallen trunk, a hedge.
- **Keep the rain off.** A tarp is the single most useful shelter item there is. Failing that, a debris hut: a ridge pole, ribs, and a very deep pile of leaves — deeper than looks sensible, an arm's length thick.
- **Small interior volume.** Just enough to lie down in. Plug the entrance with your pack or a bundle of debris.
- **Reflect a fire.** A fire in front of a shelter opening with a reflector wall of logs or stone behind it doubles the useful heat.

Indoors in a powerless house, the same logic: pick one small room, preferably with south-facing windows and away from exposed walls. Hang blankets over windows at night and open them to any sun by day. Hang a blanket across the doorway. Build a tent or a fort inside the room and sleep in it — children take to this readily, and it is genuinely the correct engineering.

## Hypothermia

Know the progression, because the person suffering it will not recognise it in themselves. Impaired judgement is a symptom, not a complication.

| Stage | Signs |
|---|---|
| Mild | Shivering, cold hands, slight clumsiness, complaining |
| Moderate | Violent shivering, stumbling, slurred speech, confusion, apathy |
| **Severe** | **Shivering stops**, rigid muscles, very slow weak pulse, drowsiness, unconsciousness |

**Shivering stopping is a danger sign, not an improvement.** It means the body has run out of the energy to do it.

Treatment: get them out of the wind, **remove wet clothing**, insulate them from the ground, wrap them including the head, and give warm sweet drinks **only if fully conscious and able to swallow**. Skin-to-skin contact inside a sleeping bag works and is not the time for embarrassment.

For severe hypothermia: **handle them extremely gently.** A cold heart is electrically irritable and rough handling can trigger a fatal arrhythmia. No rubbing limbs, no hot baths, no alcohol — all of these drive cold blood from the extremities to the core and can kill. Warm the core, slowly, and keep them horizontal.

## Damp is the enemy

- **Ventilate.** Sealing a shelter completely traps the litres of water vapour a person exhales overnight; it condenses on everything and soaks your insulation. A small opening at top and bottom keeps air moving without a draught.
- **Dry things every chance you get** — sun, wind, or body heat overnight in the sleeping bag for socks and gloves.
- **Never sleep in damp clothing.** Keep one set of sleeping clothes that never get worn in the day and never get wet. That discipline is worth more than any single piece of kit.
