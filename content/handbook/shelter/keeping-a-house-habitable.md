# Keeping a house habitable

Most people will not be building from scratch. They will be in an existing house, and the question is how to keep it working when the services that kept it working have stopped.

A modern house is a machine that assumes electricity, mains water, drainage and a supply chain of parts. Remove those and it is still a superb shelter — far better than anything you could build — but it needs to be run differently.

## The first week without services

**Water.** Turn off the mains stopcock if there is any chance the supply is contaminated. The hot water cylinder holds 100–200 litres of clean water; draw from its drain valve. The toilet cistern is clean water too. Then see the water module.

**Drainage.** Toilets still flush if you pour a bucket of water into the bowl quickly — the sewer does not need power, only the pumping stations do. If drains back up, stop using them and dig a latrine. Do not let sewage into the house.

**Cold.** Shrink the house. Live in one room, ideally south-facing with the smallest external wall area. Hang blankets over doorways and windows. Sleep together. See **Staying warm**.

**Food.** A fridge stays cold for about 4 hours unopened, a full freezer for 48 hours. Eat the fridge first, then the freezer, then the store cupboard. Cook what will spoil.

**Gas.** If you smell it, get out and leave the door open. Do not touch switches.

## Water is the enemy of the building

A house that stays dry lasts indefinitely. A house that gets wet rots from the roof down and the ground up, and it happens faster than anyone expects — a year of a leaking roof can bring down a ceiling and rot the joists.

**Inspect after every storm.** Walk around outside, then check every ceiling and the loft with a torch. Look for staining, dripping, daylight, and the smell of damp.

## Roof repairs

**A tarp over a damaged roof** is the emergency answer and it will hold for months if fixed properly:

- Fix it to **battens** nailed through the tarp into the roof timbers, never with nails through the tarp alone, which tear out in the first wind.
- Run it from **above the ridge down past the damage**, so water cannot get under the top edge.
- Weight or batten every edge. A loose corner in a gale destroys the tarp and often the roof it was protecting.

**Slipped tiles or slates** can be pushed back and held with a strip of lead, copper or galvanised wire bent into a hook over a batten — a "tingle". Replacements can come from an outbuilding, or the least visible face of the same roof.

**Flashing** — the metal where the roof meets a chimney or wall — is the commonest leak point. Lift, clean, re-bed in lime mortar or a bitumen sealant, and dress it flat.

Work on a roof only with a ladder tied off at the top and someone footing it. A fall from a roof, in a world with no hospital, is likely fatal.

## Windows and doors

A broken window is boarded from **outside**, with exterior-grade board screwed into the frame, and the gap between board and frame sealed against rain. From inside, a sheet of clear plastic stapled over the frame gives light and keeps the wind out.

If glass is scarce, salvage it from outbuildings, greenhouses and vehicles. A pane can be cut with a glass cutter, or scored with a hardened steel edge and snapped over a straight edge.

Doors that stick have usually swollen with damp. Plane the edge, and find the damp.

## Damp, and what it means

Three kinds, and they need different treatment:

- **Penetrating damp** — patches that worsen after rain, usually high up or near a chimney. A leak in the roof, gutter or wall. Find it outside and fix it.
- **Rising damp** — a tide mark along the base of a ground-floor wall. Ground water climbing the wall. Lower the outside ground level, clear the drains, and stop sealing the inside with anything waterproof, which just pushes the water higher.
- **Condensation** — black mould on cold surfaces, especially corners and behind furniture. Too much moisture in the air and not enough heat and ventilation. Cook with lids on, dry washing outside, open a window when you bathe, and heat the room a little rather than not at all.

A house that is kept sealed and unheated in winter becomes wet inside. Ventilation costs heat but saves the building.

## Gutters and drains

Clear the gutters every autumn and after every big storm. A blocked gutter overflows down the wall, and that is where most rising and penetrating damp begins.

Keep the outside drains clear — leaves, silt, roots. A rodding kit or a length of stiff hose deals with most blockages. Keep a manhole cover you can lift.

## Chimneys

If you are going to burn fuel indoors — and you are — the chimney is a life-safety system.

- **Sweep it** before first use and every few months of heavy burning. Soot fires in a flue reach over 1,000 °C and set roofs alight.
- Check the flue is **clear and unobstructed** — birds' nests are the classic problem — by looking up it with a torch and mirror, or shining a light down from the top.
- Check the **pot and flaunching** at the top for cracks, and the **brickwork** in the loft for gaps. A cracked flue leaks carbon monoxide into bedrooms.
- If the chimney has been capped, the cap must come off before you light anything.
- **A carbon monoxide alarm** in every room with a fire and every room people sleep in. Batteries changed on a schedule.

## Structure

Houses rarely fall down suddenly. They give warning: **new cracks**, especially diagonal ones stepping through brickwork; **doors and windows that suddenly bind**; **floors that slope or bounce**; **a bulge in a wall**. Any of these means something is moving.

The usual causes are water — a leaking drain washing out the ground, a blocked gutter saturating a foundation — or a tree drawing water from clay under the footings. Fix the water, and in most cases the movement stops.

A crack you can fit a coin into wants watching. One that grows week on week wants **propping**: an adjustable steel prop, or a stout timber post on a spreader board, under the nearest beam or lintel, wedged tight. Prop first, investigate second.

## The tools worth having before you need them

Claw hammer · saw · a good knife · adjustable spanner · pliers · screwdrivers · a brace or hand drill and bits · a plane · a spirit level · a tape measure · a trowel · a bucket · rope · a ladder · tarps · screws and nails in several sizes · wire · lime putty, which keeps indefinitely under water · a rodding kit · a glass cutter.

And **the manuals**: iFixit for anything with a motor, Wikibooks for carpentry and plumbing. A repair you can look up is a repair you can do.
