# Wells, and living through a dry year

Rain fails. Streams dry. A well reaches the water that is still there under the ground, and a well that is dug, lined and protected properly is the most reliable water source a household can own.

## Where the water is

Rain soaks into the ground until it meets rock or clay it cannot pass, and collects above that layer. The top of the saturated zone is the **water table**. A well is a hole that reaches below it.

Signs of a shallow water table: **springs** on the hillside, **rushes, willows and alders**, a **pond that never dries**, **existing wells** in the district (ask how deep), and **valley bottoms** rather than hilltops. On chalk and limestone the water is deep and the well is a serious excavation; on clay or gravel over clay it may be two or three metres down.

**Dowsing** finds water at about the rate chance would — it is the digging that finds it, wherever the stick pointed.

## Digging a hand well

The oldest method, and dangerous in a specific way that has killed more people than any other part of it.

> **Warning** **A hole deeper than your chest can collapse and bury you.** Loose or sandy soil is the worst; damp clay looks safe and is not. Never dig below chest height without lining the hole as you go, and never work in the hole without someone at the top who can raise the alarm. Every year, everywhere, people die in trenches they thought were safe.

1. **Site it** at least 30 m from any latrine, midden or animal pen, and **uphill** of them. Groundwater flows downhill, slowly; whatever is upslope ends up in your well.
2. **Dig a wide hole** — 1.2 to 1.5 m across. Narrow holes are harder to work in and more dangerous.
3. **Line it as you descend.** Concrete rings are the modern answer; failing those, dry-laid stone or brick, or timber cribbing. The lining is what stops the collapse. Sink the lining ring by ring, digging out beneath it so it drops under its own weight — a **caisson** — and the sides are never unsupported.
4. **Below the water table**, digging turns to bailing. A bucket on a rope, emptied by the person above, until you have a metre or two of water depth and can go no deeper. That depth is your reserve; the more the better.
5. **Gravel in the bottom**, so what comes up is water, not mud.

## Protecting it

An open hole in the ground is a well for a season and a cesspit after. What makes it a source for decades:

- **A raised lining** — the wall carried up **at least 60 cm above ground**, so surface water and runoff cannot pour in.
- **A concrete or clay apron** around the head, sloping away, with a drain to carry spilled water off. Mud around a well head is how the well becomes contaminated by what is walked into it.
- **A lid.** Covered always. Light grows algae; open holes take animals, leaves, children.
- **A dedicated bucket and rope** that never touch the ground and are never used for anything else. Better still, a **hand pump** or a windlass, so nothing goes down but the pipe.
- **A fence** to keep animals off.
- **Disinfect it** once built and after any flooding: a strong chlorine solution poured in, left overnight, pumped out until the smell fades.

And still treat the water for drinking, at least at first and at least for the vulnerable. A well protects against most contamination, not all.

## Other ways down

**A driven well** — a pointed, screened pipe hammered into soft ground with a heavy weight — reaches shallow water in sand or gravel with no digging at all, and a hand pump on top draws it. Cheap, fast, and safe; it only works where the ground is soft and the water shallow.

**A bore** with a hand auger — turned like a corkscrew, adding lengths of rod — reaches deeper in clay and sand. Slow, and it makes a narrow hole for a pump, not a bucket.

**Springs** — where the water table meets the surface — need no digging, only protecting: a box built round the eye of the spring, a pipe out, a diversion ditch above to keep runoff off, and the same rules about latrines uphill.

## Drought

The rain stops, the stream shrinks, the tank runs low. What matters is arithmetic done early.

**Know your stock.** Litres in the tank, the well's daily yield (measure it — bail it dry, time the refill), what the stream still gives. Total it. That is your water.

**Know your burn.** Litres per person per day, with what you are actually using it for. Measure this too; people guess low.

**Divide.** Stock divided by burn is days. If the number is smaller than the likely wait for rain, cut the burn today, not in a week.

**Priorities, strictly in this order:**

1. **Drinking and cooking** — 3 to 5 litres a person a day. Never rationed.
2. **Livestock drinking** — a goat needs several litres a day, a cow tens. Animals are slaughtered before people go thirsty.
3. **Hygiene** — hands always; the body a wipe rather than a wash; clothes worn longer.
4. **The garden** — only what will die otherwise, only at the roots, only at dusk. Mulch everything. Let the lawn go.
5. **Everything else** — nothing.

**Reuse.** Washing water goes on the garden. Rinsing water becomes washing water. Nothing clean goes down a drain.

**Collect what there is.** Dew on a tarp at dawn. The last pools in the streambed, dug deeper and shaded. The well bailed to its floor.

**Do not** drink from stagnant pools without treating, and do not let thirst make the decision the water chapter tells you not to make.

## Building the reserve before you need it

A dry summer in Britain is not a desert; the problem is three months, not three years. The answer is **storage** sized for it: enough tanks that a wet winter fills them for a dry summer. A thousand litres per person, plus a working well, covers almost any British drought. See **Harvesting rainwater** for the sizing.

And plant for it. Deep-rooted crops — beans, squash, potatoes mulched thick — survive a dry spell that kills lettuce. A garden planned for a dry year is a garden that has one less way to fail.
