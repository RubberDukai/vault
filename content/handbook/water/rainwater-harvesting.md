# Harvesting rainwater

In a wet country, a roof is the most reliable water source you have. It needs no digging, no pump, no walking, and the water starts out cleaner than anything on the ground.

## The arithmetic

**One millimetre of rain on one square metre of roof yields one litre.** Multiply by about 0.85 for losses to splash, evaporation and the first flush.

A typical British house roof, 60 m² in plan, in a place with 900 mm of rain a year:

60 × 900 × 0.85 = **45,900 litres a year** — around 125 litres a day, averaged.

That is more than a household's drinking and cooking needs several times over. The problem is never the annual total; it is the **gaps between rain**, which is what the tank is for.

Measure the roof in plan — its footprint on the ground — not along the slope. The slope does not catch more rain.

## Sizing the tank

Decide how many dry days you want to bridge, and how much water per day you need in that period.

- Drinking and cooking only: **5 litres per person per day**
- Adding basic washing: **20 litres**
- Three weeks of dry weather, a family of four, drinking and cooking: 4 × 5 × 21 = **420 litres**
- The same with washing: **1,680 litres**

A 1,000-litre IBC — the caged plastic cube industry uses, widely available second-hand — is a very good starting point. Two are better. More is better again, because a full tank at the start of a drought is the only kind that helps.

Storage is cheap. Water is not something you want to be short of by 10%.

## The catchment

**Roof material matters.** Clay tile, slate, glazed metal and modern painted steel are all fine. Cement and concrete tiles are fine once weathered. Avoid collecting from:

- **Asbestos-cement** sheet, still common on outbuildings — fibres shed into the water.
- Roofs with **lead flashing or valleys** if the water is for drinking, or accept the water from those sections is diverted.
- **Freshly treated timber** shingles.
- Roofs under **heavy bird traffic** or overhanging trees, without a good first-flush system.

**Gutters** must fall toward the downpipe — about 1 cm per 3 m — and be kept clear. **Leaf guards** on the gutter or a **leaf trap** at the top of the downpipe save a great deal of trouble.

## First flush — the step that makes it safe

The first rain off a roof washes down everything that landed on it since the last: dust, bird droppings, leaves, insects, soot. That is the dirtiest water you will ever collect, and it goes straight into the tank unless you divert it.

A **first-flush diverter** is a length of vertical pipe on the downpipe, sealed at the bottom, with a small drip hole. The first water fills the pipe; once it is full, a floating ball seals its top and the rest of the flow carries on to the tank. Between storms, the pipe slowly drains through the drip hole and resets itself.

**Size it to hold about half a litre per square metre of roof** — 30 litres for a 60 m² roof, which is roughly 3 m of 110 mm pipe. Bigger is safer.

This one component does more for water quality than any filter downstream of it.

## The tank

- **Opaque.** Light grows algae. A translucent tank needs painting or wrapping.
- **Sealed against mosquitoes.** Every opening screened with fine mesh, including the overflow. A tank breeds mosquitoes in a week otherwise, and in a world without medicine that is not a small matter.
- **An overflow** that carries excess away from the building's foundations — to a soakaway or a second tank.
- **A tap raised above the base**, so sediment that settles stays out of what you draw, and a **drain valve at the very bottom** to flush that sediment out yearly.
- **Off the ground** on a firm base — a full 1,000-litre tank weighs a tonne — and raised enough to get a bucket under the tap. Height also gives you pressure: about 0.1 bar per metre, enough for a gravity-fed hose.
- **Shaded**, which keeps it cool, which keeps it clean.
- **Inlet through a mesh basket** to catch anything the first flush missed.

An IBC or drum that previously held anything but food or water is suspect. Chemical residues do not wash out. Know its history or use it for irrigation only.

## Keeping it clean

- Clean the gutters and the first-flush pipe every few months and after any big storm.
- Drain and scrub the tank once a year, or when sediment builds up.
- Do not let anyone dip into the tank. Water goes out through the tap, always.
- If the water smells or turns green, empty it, clean it, and look for the light or the dead thing that caused it.

## Drinking it

Rainwater from a clean roof through a first flush into a clean tank is close to safe, and in much of the world it is drunk as it comes. But roofs have birds, and tanks have things that die in them, and the cost of being wrong is dysentery.

**Treat it for drinking**, exactly as you would any other source: filter if it is cloudy, then boil, chlorinate or use sunlight. See **Making water safe to drink**. Treat a day's supply at a time, and keep treated water in a separate, covered container.

For washing, laundry, animals and the garden, use it straight from the tank.

## Doing it with less

No gutters: a tarp or sheet stretched with a sag in the middle and a hole at the low point, over a barrel. A single tarp of 4 m × 3 m catches 10 litres per millimetre of rain.

No tank: any clean container. Line a hole with plastic sheet and cover it.

No downpipe: a chain hung from the gutter outlet carries water down it with less splash than an open fall, and it is what Japanese temples have used for centuries.

Rain is the one water source that comes to you. Everything else, you have to go and fetch.
