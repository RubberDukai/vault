# Finding water

Water runs downhill and collects in the lowest place it can find. Almost all field-craft for finding it follows from that one sentence.

## Reading the landscape

- **Follow valleys and gullies down.** Even a dry streambed often has water below the surface at the outside of bends, where the current cut deepest. Dig there.
- **Look for green.** A band of denser, greener vegetation in a dry landscape marks water at or near the surface. Willow, alder, reeds, rushes and cottonwood all mean shallow groundwater.
- **Watch animals.** Grazing animals water at dawn and dusk and their trails converge on it, widening as they approach. Bees rarely range more than a few kilometres from water. Grain-eating birds fly low and direct toward water at dusk.
- **Rock and clay layers.** Where a permeable layer sits on an impermeable one, water seeps out along the join — look for springs at the base of cliffs and along the contact line on hillsides.
- **North-facing slopes** in the northern hemisphere (south-facing in the southern) hold moisture longer.

## Collecting without a source

- **Rain** is the easiest clean water you will get. Any tarp, sheet or roof with a low corner and a container underneath.
- **Dew** can be mopped from grass with a cloth before sunrise and wrung out. It is slow but real — a person working a dewy meadow at dawn can gather a litre or two.
- **Transpiration bag:** tie a clear plastic bag over a leafy branch of a non-poisonous tree, with a corner hanging low. Water evaporating from the leaves condenses inside. Expect 50–200 ml per bag per day. Modest, but it needs no work once set.
- **Solar still:** a hole, a container in the centre, a plastic sheet weighted at the centre and sealed at the edges. Yields are usually disappointing — often less than the sweat spent digging. Know it, but do not rely on it.
- **Snow and ice:** melt it before drinking. Eating snow costs you core temperature you cannot afford. Ice yields more water per unit of fuel than snow.

## Wells and springs

A **spring** where water emerges under its own pressure is the best source you can find: it is filtered by the ground and it flows continuously. Protect it — box in the eye of the spring, divert surface runoff away from it uphill, and keep animals and latrines well clear and downhill.

A **shallow hand-dug well** reaching the water table can be dug with hand tools in soft ground. Line it to stop collapse, cover it, and draw with a dedicated bucket that never touches the ground. The commonest way a good well goes bad is a dirty rope and bucket.

> **Warning** Never enter a deep hole or an old well to work on it. Carbon dioxide and other heavy gases pool at the bottom and are invisible and odourless; they have killed a great many people, including the ones who climbed in to rescue the first. Lower a lit candle first — if it goes out, so will you.

## Water to be careful with

- **Downstream of anything.** Industry, farms, roads, mines, settlements, latrines. Move upstream if you can.
- **Standing water** with no inflow or outflow, especially warm and green.
- **Water near flood debris** after a flood — sewage and fuel go everywhere.
- **Salt water.** Do not drink it, and do not drink urine. Both cost you more water than they provide. Distil sea water if you have fuel.
- **Water with a chemical sheen, foam, or a strong smell.** Filtration and boiling do nothing for chemical contamination. Find another source.

## Storage

Store more than you think. Water is heavy, cheap and impossible to improvise in a hurry.

- Food-grade containers only, kept dark and cool.
- Rotate stored tap water every six months, or treat it lightly with chlorine when filling.
- Keep some containers small enough that one person can actually carry them — a full 25 litre drum weighs 25 kg.
- Know where the water already in your house is: the hot water cylinder holds 100–200 litres, and the toilet cistern (not the bowl) is clean water. Turn off the incoming supply before drawing from either if the mains might be contaminated.
