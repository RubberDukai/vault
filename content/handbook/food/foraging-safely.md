# Foraging without poisoning anyone

Foraging will not feed a family. What it does is fill the gaps — vitamins in winter, protein from nuts in autumn, greens in spring before the garden yields — and it costs nothing but knowledge. The knowledge is the whole thing. A confident forager and a poisoned one differ only in what they knew.

**The rules that keep you alive:**

1. **If you are not certain, you do not eat it.** Certainty means you could name it, name what it might be confused with, and say how you told them apart. "It looks like" is not certainty.
2. **Learn the deadly ones first.** There are only a handful. Knowing them cold is worth more than knowing a hundred edibles.
3. **Introduce new foods one at a time, in small amounts.** A tablespoon, then wait a day.
4. **Do not forage beside roads, downstream of farms, or on old industrial ground.** Plants concentrate what is in the soil.
5. **Mushrooms are a separate discipline.** See below.

## The plants that will kill you

Learn these to the point of recognising them at a glance in any season.

**Hemlock** *(Conium maculatum)* — the one that killed Socrates. Tall, feathery, white umbrella flowers, and looks like cow parsley, wild carrot, or parsley. The tells: **purple blotches on a smooth, hairless stem**, and a **mousy, unpleasant smell** when crushed. Cow parsley has a hairy, ridged stem with no blotches. If in any doubt about anything in the carrot family, leave it — the family contains both dinner and death.

**Hemlock water-dropwort** *(Oenanthe crocata)* — the most poisonous plant in Britain, responsible for more deaths than any other. Grows in and beside water, ditches and wet meadows. Looks like celery or flat-leaf parsley, smells pleasantly of parsley, roots look like small parsnips. A few mouthfuls of the root can kill an adult in hours. **Do not eat anything resembling celery, parsley or parsnip that you find growing wild near water.**

**Foxglove** *(Digitalis)* — the tall spire of purple bells is unmistakable in flower, but the first-year rosette of soft grey-green leaves is easily mistaken for comfrey or borage. Contains the heart drug digoxin; eating it stops the heart.

**Deadly nightshade** *(Atropa belladonna)* — shiny black berries the size of cherries, sweet-tasting, sitting in a green star-shaped calyx. Two or three berries can kill a child. Bittersweet nightshade, with clusters of small red berries, is also toxic though less so.

**Yew** — every part is poisonous except the red flesh of the berry, and the seed inside that flesh is deadly. Children are drawn to the berries. Death is by cardiac arrest.

**Lords and ladies / cuckoo pint** *(Arum maculatum)* — arrow-shaped glossy leaves in spring, a spike of orange-red berries in autumn. The leaves appear alongside wild garlic and get picked with it. Burning pain in the mouth is the first sign.

**Monkshood** *(Aconitum)* — hooded blue-purple flowers, deeply cut leaves. Toxic through the skin as well as by mouth. Do not handle.

**Horse chestnut** conkers are not sweet chestnuts and are mildly poisonous. Sweet chestnuts have a spiny burr like a hedgehog with a pointed tip on the nut; conkers have a leathery case with a few thick spines and a round, shiny nut.

## Worth knowing: greens and roots

**Nettle** — the best of them. Rich in protein, iron and vitamins. Pick the top four leaves with gloves in spring and early summer, before it flowers; older plants develop gritty crystals. Cooking or drying destroys the sting entirely. Soup, tea, or wilted like spinach.

**Wild garlic / ramsons** — broad pointed leaves carpeting damp woodland in spring, unmistakable **garlic smell when crushed**. That smell is the test: the lookalikes (lords and ladies, lily of the valley) do not have it. Check every leaf.

**Dandelion** — every part. Young leaves in salad (bitter), roots roasted, flowers fried. Available almost year-round.

**Chickweed** — a sprawling mat of small paired leaves with a single line of hairs down one side of the stem. Mild, abundant, in salads.

**Cleavers / goosegrass** — the sticky one. Young shoots cooked; the whole plant as a tea.

**Fat hen** — a common field weed, mealy-looking leaves, related to spinach and quinoa and used the same way. Seeds are edible too.

**Sorrel** — arrow-shaped leaves with a sharp lemony taste, from oxalic acid. Pleasant in moderation; avoid large amounts and avoid it entirely with kidney problems.

**Plantain** *(Plantago)* — the broad-leaved lawn weed, not the banana. Edible young, and a genuinely useful wound poultice: chew or crush a leaf and apply to stings and small cuts.

**Burdock** — first-year roots dug in autumn, peeled and cooked. A real calorie source, cultivated in Japan as gobo.

## Fruit

**Blackberry, raspberry, bilberry, wild strawberry** — need no introduction. **Elderberry** — cook it; raw elderberries and any leaves or stems are mildly toxic. Elderflowers make cordial and fritters. **Hawthorn** — the young leaves in spring ("bread and cheese"), the berries in autumn as a jelly or a heart tonic tea. **Rosehip** — the highest vitamin C of any British plant; remove the irritant hairs and seeds, or make syrup and strain. **Sloe** — after the first frost. **Crab apple** — sour, superb for pectin and jelly. **Rowan** — cooked into jelly, never raw in quantity.

## Nuts — the calorie source

Autumn nuts are the only wild food that seriously contributes calories, and they store through winter.

**Hazel** — from August, before the squirrels. Eaten raw or roasted. **Sweet chestnut** — roasted, boiled or dried and ground to flour. A significant staple across southern Europe for centuries. **Beech mast** — small, fiddly, worth it in a good year, and oil can be pressed from it. **Walnut** where it grows.

**Acorns** — abundant every year and full of tannin, which is bitter and hard on the stomach. **Leach them**: shell, grind coarsely, and soak in repeated changes of water — cold over several days, or hot in several boilings — until the water runs clear and the meal tastes bland. Then dry and use as flour. Acorns fed a great many people through a great many bad winters.

## The coast

**Seaweed** — every seaweed on British shores is edible; none is poisonous. Some are unpalatable. Worth knowing: **dulse** (red, dried as a snack), **laver** (the thin dark one, cooked long into laverbread), **sea lettuce** (bright green, raw or cooked), **kelp** and **sugar kelp** (dried and used as stock or crisps). Gather from clean water, from living plants, never from the strandline.

**Shellfish** — mussels, cockles, limpets, winkles, razor clams. Cook them. Take only from clean water, never near sewage outfalls or harbours, and never in a **red tide** — a discoloured sea, which produces a toxin that cooking does not destroy and that can kill. Any shellfish that is open before cooking and does not close when tapped, or closed after cooking, is dead and goes in the sea, not in you.

**Samphire, sea beet, sea purslane** on saltmarsh. **Sea kale** on shingle.

## Mushrooms: a separate answer

Fungi kill people who know a great deal about fungi. The **death cap** looks like several edible species, tastes pleasant, and destroys the liver over days while the person appears to recover. The **destroying angel** is pure white and beautiful. The **webcaps** cause kidney failure that appears two weeks later.

Without hands-on instruction from someone expert, with specimens in front of you, the correct amount of wild mushroom to eat is **none**. That is not caution for its own sake; it is arithmetic about downside. A meal of mushrooms is a meal. A mistake is a death, slow and untreatable.

If you are going to learn, learn a very small number of unmistakable species — **giant puffball** (larger than a fist, pure white inside like marshmallow, no gills), **hedgehog fungus** (spines underneath instead of gills), **cauliflower fungus** — and nothing with gills until you have years behind you.

## The last-resort test

If you must eat something unknown to survive, the **universal edibility test** exists. It is slow — a full day per plant — and it does not work for fungi or for the plants above, so it is not a substitute for knowing.

Separate the plant into parts and test one part at a time. Eight hours without food beforehand. Rub on the inner wrist and wait 15 minutes; touch to the lip, 3 minutes; on the tongue, 15 minutes; chew without swallowing, 15 minutes; swallow a small piece and wait 8 hours, drinking only water. Any burning, numbness, nausea or rash at any step: stop, and rinse or induce vomiting. Passed all of it: eat a small handful and wait another 8 hours.

It is a test for avoiding acute poisoning. It says nothing about cumulative toxins, and it will not protect you from the plants on the deadly list, some of which pass every step and kill days later. That is why the list comes first.

## Where the real knowledge lives

A field guide with photographs of every species in every season, ideally printed. Wikipedia has good entries for each of the plants named here. And a person — a local who forages — is worth more than all of it. Learn from someone, on the ground, with the plants in front of you, while that is still an ordinary thing to be able to do.
