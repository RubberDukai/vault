# Hunting, trapping and fishing

The romantic picture is a person with a rifle. The productive picture is a person checking a line of snares and fish traps at dawn, because **traps work while you sleep** and a hunter works only while awake. Passive methods, set in numbers, are how people have actually fed themselves from the wild.

> **Note** In ordinary times, almost everything here is regulated: closed seasons, protected species, licences, and laws about traps and weapons that vary by country. Learn the skills legally — fishing with a licence, shooting on a permit, ferreting with permission — while there are still people to learn from. The skills are the preparation; the law is the law until it is not.

## Fishing — the best return on effort

Fish are abundant, replace themselves, and are caught by methods that need almost no strength or skill.

**Lines.** A hook, a line, bait, patience. Hooks are tiny, cheap, and keep forever — buy hundreds in several sizes now. Line likewise. Bait is worms, insects, bread, or a strip of the last fish.

**Set lines** are where it gets efficient: a long line with many baited hooks on short droppers, staked or weighted overnight and checked in the morning. Ten hooks fishing all night beat one rod fishing all evening.

**Traps and nets.** A **funnel trap** — a basket or a bottle with an inverted cone at the mouth — lets fish in and not out; baited and left in a stream, it fishes continuously. A **gill net** across a stream catches whatever swims through. A **fish weir** — a fence of stakes across a stream with a narrow gap leading into a trap — is a village-scale device that fed settlements for millennia. Nets can be knotted from any cord with a shuttle and a gauge; learning the sheet bend, which is the net knot, takes an hour.

**Tickling and spearing** work in clear shallow water for the patient.

**Eels** are caught in baited traps, are extremely nutritious and fatty, and keep well smoked. Crayfish walk into a baited trap overnight. **Mussels, cockles and limpets** are gathered at low tide and need no equipment — see the foraging chapter for the safety rules.

All freshwater fish and crustaceans must be **cooked** — many carry parasites. Sea fish can be eaten raw if fresh, but cooking is the safe habit.

## Trapping

**Snares** — a loop of wire that tightens on an animal walking through it — are the highest-yield land method for small game. Rabbit is the target in Britain: numerous, fast-breeding, and worth about a meal each.

- **Brass or steel snare wire**, a loop about a hand's width across for rabbit, set a hand's width above the ground on a well-used run — the flattened paths through grass that rabbits use every night.
- **Set many.** Twenty snares might catch two rabbits. That is the arithmetic; a single snare is a lottery ticket.
- **Check every morning without fail.** An animal in a snare suffers; one left there is stolen by a fox and wasted.
- Anchor to a stake or a heavy peg. A snare tied to nothing is a snare walked away with.

**Deadfalls and box traps** for anything from rats to badgers: a weight propped on a trigger, or a cage with a door that drops. Box traps catch alive, which is useful — you can release the wrong animal, or keep the right one until it is needed.

**Where the animals are** matters more than the trap. Runs, droppings, tracks in mud or snow, gnawed bark, burrows with fresh soil. Set traps where the sign is fresh, and move them if they catch nothing in three nights.

**Pigeons** come to grain scattered in a clear space; a drop-net or a funnel trap over the bait takes them. **Squirrels** are numerous, edible, and take a baited box trap readily.

## Hunting

**Air rifles** are the realistic tool for small game: legal without a licence in much of Britain below a power limit, quiet, cheap to feed, and accurate enough to take rabbits, pigeons and squirrels cleanly at short range. Pellets are tiny and keep indefinitely; buy thousands. This is the one weapon worth learning now that will still be useful later.

**Shotguns and rifles** need licences, ammunition that runs out and cannot be made, and skill that takes years. Their case in a collapse is deer — a red or fallow deer is a hundred kilograms of meat — and defence of livestock from dogs. If you hold one legally, keep the ammunition dry and rotate it.

**Bows and crossbows** can be made and repaired, and their ammunition can be recovered and reused. The skill is real and takes a year of practice to be useful; the range is short. They are the long-run answer once cartridges are gone.

**Catapults** with lead or steel shot are surprisingly effective on small birds and rabbits at close range, silent, and endlessly reloadable. Practise on tins.

Whatever the weapon: **a clean kill or no shot.** A wounded animal that escapes is meat lost and an animal suffering.

**The four rules, for anything that fires a projectile — air rifle, catapult, bow, gun — and for every child who will ever be near one:** treat every weapon as loaded, always; never point it at anything you are not willing to destroy, which includes your own foot; finger off the trigger until the sights are on the target; and know what is *behind* the target — a pellet or arrow that misses keeps going, and a hedge does not stop it. Unload before crossing a fence or entering a house. Store weapons and ammunition separately and locked. Most shooting deaths in a household are not the animal's.

## Butchery, briefly

- **Bleed it** at once — a cut to the throat with the animal head-down. Meat from an unbled animal spoils faster.
- **Look before you gut.** An animal that was slow, thin, staggering or easy to catch was probably ill; do not eat it. Wear gloves (or bags over the hands) for skinning and gutting rabbits and hares especially — **tularaemia** passes through cuts in the skin from an infected carcass, and any animal's blood can carry something. Wash hands and knives after, and keep a knife for butchery that never touches cooked food.
- **Gut it** within the hour: a shallow cut along the belly from ribs to vent, careful not to pierce the gut, and everything pulled out and away. Keep the **liver, heart and kidneys** — the most nutritious parts. A liver with white spots, a gut full of pus, or a smell that is wrong: burn the carcass.
- **Skin it** while warm; it comes off far more easily.
- **Hang it** somewhere cool and fly-proof — a day for small game, several for deer — before jointing. Hanging tenderises and improves it.
- **Cook thoroughly.** Wild meat carries parasites (trichinella in wild boar and bear, tapeworm in deer and rabbit); pink game is a risk, not a treat. Cook every part to 75 °C right through — juices clear, no pink at the bone — and never taste raw meat while butchering.

Nothing is waste. Bones for stock, fat rendered for cooking and candles, skins tanned, sinew for cord, feathers for insulation, guts cleaned for casings, and the rest to the compost or the dogs.

## Sustainability

A wild population can feed a small group for a long time or a large group for a short one. Take what breeds fast (rabbits, pigeons, fish) freely; take what breeds slowly (deer, large birds) sparingly and never the breeding females in season. A wood emptied of game in the first winter is empty for years.

The same logic that runs the garden runs the wild: **leave enough to replace what you took.**

## The kit worth having

Hooks in several sizes, hundreds of them · fishing line, a few hundred metres · snare wire, a roll · cordage for nets · an air rifle and a large tin of pellets · a good knife and a steel · a gambrel and rope for hanging · salt, for the surplus.

Cheap, small, and it turns the countryside into a larder. The knowledge to use it is in the Boy Scouts Handbook and Nessmuk's *Woodcraft*, both on the shelf of this library, and in any old countryman still willing to teach.
