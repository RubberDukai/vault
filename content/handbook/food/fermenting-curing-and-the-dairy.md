# Artisan foods — fermenting, preserving and the dairy

*Preserving food* explains the four levers — water, acid, salt, temperature — and the botulism rule. This chapter is the kitchen: recipes that work with a few tools and no electricity, in the quantities a household actually makes. All of these were ordinary skills two generations ago and every one is learnable in a weekend.

**The tools:** a large pot, a thermometer if you can get one, wide-mouthed jars with lids (Kilner-type or repurposed), muslin or clean cotton, a crock or a big jar for ferments, a wooden spoon, scales, a fine sieve, a potato masher, a clean bucket with a lid, and a cool dark cupboard. Everything scrupulously clean: hot soapy water and a rinse, and for jars, ten minutes in a hot oven or boiling water.

## Sourdough — bread with no yeast to buy

**The starter.** Day 1: 50 g flour (rye or wholemeal is fastest) + 50 g water in a jar, loosely lidded, in a warm room. Each day: discard half, add 50 g flour + 50 g water. By day 5–7 it doubles within hours of feeding, smells sour and yeasty, and is alive. Keep it: feed daily on the counter, or weekly in a cool place. It will outlive you if you let it. A dried smear on paper is a backup that revives in water and flour.

**The loaf** (one large): 500 g flour, 350 g water, 100 g active starter, 10 g salt. Mix flour and water, rest 30 min; add starter and salt, mix; over 3–4 hours in a warm place, stretch and fold the dough in the bowl four times; shape into a tight round, put in a floured cloth-lined bowl, and either prove 2–3 hours warm or overnight cool. Bake in a lidded cast-iron pot preheated as hot as the oven goes: 25 min lid on, 20 min lid off. On a fire: a Dutch oven in the embers with embers on the lid. Flatbreads need none of this — flour, water, salt, a hot pan — and are what most of the world eats.

## Sauerkraut and the salt ferments

**The rule:** vegetables under brine, air excluded, 2% salt by weight, cool room, patience. The salt lets the lactic bacteria win; the acid they make keeps everything else out. A ferment that smells sour and clean is right; one that smells rotten, or has fuzzy mould (not the white film of kahm yeast, which is harmless and skimmed off), is thrown away.

**Sauerkraut.** Shred a cabbage finely; weigh it; add 2% of its weight in salt (20 g per kilo); massage hard until it weeps enough brine to cover itself; pack into a jar or crock, pressing down, brine above the cabbage, a weight on top (a smaller jar of water, a clean stone, a plate); cover loosely against dust but not airtight (gas must escape). Ready in 1–4 weeks depending on warmth; keeps for months in the cool. Add caraway, juniper, apple, carrot as you like.

**Kimchi.** Cut a Chinese cabbage (or any cabbage) into chunks; soak 2 hours in a 5% brine; drain and rinse. Paste: 4 tbsp chilli flakes, 4 cloves garlic, a thumb of ginger, 2 tbsp fish sauce (or soy, or salt), a little sugar or cooked rice flour porridge, a grated apple or pear; sliced spring onions, radish and carrot. Rub the paste through the cabbage, pack into a jar with a little headroom, lid loose. One to three days warm to start, then cool. It gets better for a month and keeps for six.

**Everything else the same way:** cucumbers (with a grape leaf, oak leaf or a pinch of tea for crunch — the tannin), carrots, beetroot, green beans, turnips, garlic, chillies, green tomatoes, any firm vegetable. 2% salt in the brine (20 g per litre of water) for whole vegetables; 2% by weight of the vegetable for shredded ones.

## Vinegar pickles

Acid from outside rather than fermentation: quicker, sharper, and the vinegar must be at least **5% acidity** (most bought vinegars; test your own with pH paper — below pH 4.6 or do not seal it). Vegetables are usually **brined overnight** first (100 g salt per litre) to draw water so the pickle stays crisp, then rinsed, packed in jars and covered with hot spiced vinegar (vinegar boiled with peppercorns, mustard seed, bay, chilli, a little sugar). Seal, leave a month. Pickled onions, gherkins, beetroot, red cabbage, eggs (hard-boiled, in plain spiced vinegar — keep for months), walnuts (green, in July, brined ten days then vinegared).

## Jam, jelly and the setting point

A preserve sets when **pectin, acid and sugar** meet at the right temperature. High-pectin fruit sets easily: currants, gooseberries, damsons, quince, crab apple, cooking apple, citrus. Low: strawberries, raspberries, blackberries, cherries, pears — add lemon juice, or a quarter by weight of crab apple or cooking apple, or use their juice.

**Jam.** Equal weights fruit and sugar (down to 60% sugar for a softer, shorter-keeping jam). Cook the fruit soft with a splash of water first; add the sugar off the heat and stir until dissolved; boil hard. **Setting point:** 105 °C on a thermometer, or the wrinkle test — a drop on a cold saucer wrinkles when pushed with a finger after a minute. Skim, pot boiling hot into hot sterile jars, seal at once. Keeps a year or more; sugar below 60% or a poor seal, and it moulds — scrape it and eat it quickly.

**Jelly** (clear, from the juice): cook the fruit to a pulp, drip overnight through a jelly bag or clean cloth (do not squeeze, or it clouds), measure the juice, add 450 g sugar per 600 ml, boil to setting point. Crab apple, bramble, rowan, hawthorn, quince, redcurrant, elderberry-and-apple.

**Fruit leather and fruit cheese:** cook fruit to a purée, sweeten lightly, spread thin on a tray and dry in a low oven or the sun (leather), or cook down thick with equal sugar until it holds a spoon upright, pot and dry (membrillo, damson cheese). Both keep a year.

**Chutney:** fruit or vegetables (apple, green tomato, marrow, plum, rhubarb, onion) cooked slowly for hours with vinegar, sugar, spice and dried fruit until thick and no free liquid runs into a channel drawn through it. Pot hot. Better after three months; keeps two years. The way to use up every glut.

## Yoghurt

Heat a litre of milk to just below boiling (85 °C) — this improves the set — cool to blood-warm (43 °C, or when you can hold a finger in it for ten seconds), stir in two tablespoons of live yoghurt, and keep warm and still for 6–12 hours: in a wide-necked flask, a pot wrapped in towels in a hay box, by the stove. Keep two tablespoons back to start the next batch. Strain through muslin for a few hours for thick Greek-style yoghurt; a day for **labneh**, a spreadable cheese — salt it, roll into balls, keep under oil.

## Butter

Skim the cream from milk that has stood 12–24 hours (or use a separator). Let it ripen a day in the cool. Churn: a jar shaken, a whisk, a butter churn, a food processor — it goes through whipped cream to grainy yellow lumps in liquid buttermilk within ten minutes. Drain (drink the buttermilk or use it in bread), wash the butter in cold water, working it with a paddle until the water runs clear — **every trace of buttermilk left in makes it go rancid**. Salt at 2% for keeping. Wrapped and cool it keeps weeks; clarified into **ghee** (melted, simmered until the water is gone and the solids brown, strained) it keeps a year without cooling.

## Cheese

Milk goes off; cheese is milk that keeps. Every cheese is the same four steps: **acidify** (a culture, or yoghurt, or just time), **set** (rennet, or acid and heat), **drain** (cut the curd, let the whey run), **preserve** (salt, press, dry, age). Start with the fresh cheeses and move up.

**Paneer / queso fresco — an hour, no rennet.** Heat 2 litres of milk to boiling; add 4 tbsp lemon juice or vinegar; stir; the curds separate from clear yellow whey in a minute. Strain through muslin, salt lightly, tie up and press under a weight for an hour. Fries, grills, keeps a few days. Use the whey in bread or feed it to the pig.

**Rennet.** The enzyme that sets milk into a firm curd for real cheese. Bought rennet keeps a year in the cool; **vegetable rennet** comes from the flowers of cardoon or thistle (steep dried flower heads in warm water overnight) or from lady's bedstraw and nettle (weaker); **animal rennet** is the dried, salted fourth stomach of an unweaned calf, kid or lamb — a strip soaked in brine gives a rennet that sets milk, and a slaughtered animal should have its stomach kept for exactly this.

**Culture.** A spoon of live yoghurt, a little buttermilk, or a cup of whey from the last batch of cheese; or leave the milk to sour on its own overnight (raw milk sours; pasteurised milk rots — start pasteurised milk with yoghurt).

**A simple hard cheese (farmhouse / cheddar-type), 4 litres → about 500 g.** 1. Warm the milk to 32 °C; stir in the culture (2 tbsp yoghurt); leave 45 min. 2. Stir in rennet (as the packet says; for a home strip, enough that the milk sets to a clean break in 45 min — test by pushing a finger in: the curd should split cleanly). 3. Cut the curd into 1 cm cubes with a long knife. Rest 5 min. 4. Heat slowly to 38 °C over half an hour, stirring gently, until the cubes shrink and squeak between the teeth. 5. Drain the whey; let the curd knit in the warm pot 20 min, cut it into slabs, turn them every 15 min for an hour (this is "cheddaring" — it makes the acid). 6. Mill the slabs into thumb-sized pieces; add 2% salt by weight (10 g for 500 g); mix. 7. Pack into a mould lined with cloth (a tin with holes, a section of pipe, a basket) and press: 5 kg for 15 min, flip, 10 kg for an hour, flip, 20 kg overnight (bricks, a lever, a car jack). 8. Dry at room temperature 2–3 days, turning, until a rind forms; then rub with oil or wax, or bandage in muslin brushed with lard, and age in a cool (10–13 °C) damp cellar for 2–12 months, turning weekly and wiping off mould. Keeps a year. The first ones are for learning; the fifth is good.

**Soft ripened cheese** (camembert-style) needs its white mould culture and a controlled cellar — later. **Halloumi** is the hard-cheese curd cooked in its own whey and salted; keeps months in brine. **Feta**: the curd salted heavily and kept under brine — the simplest keeping cheese. **Goat's milk** makes all of these; sheep's milk the richest.

## Vinegar

Any alcohol left open to the air with a "mother" (the rubbery film from live vinegar) turns to vinegar in a month or two: cider to cider vinegar, wine to wine vinegar, a mash of apple peelings and cores with a little sugar in water to a weak but usable vinegar in six weeks. Keep a jar going permanently; feed it. Vinegar preserves everything else.

## Cider, perry and country wine — surplus fruit that keeps

Crush apples (a scratter, a mallet in a bucket, a mincer), press the juice through cloth (a fruit press, or a car jack on a stack of cloth parcels in a frame), and leave it in a fermenter — a bucket with a lid and an airlock, or a demijohn with a bit of cloth over the neck — for two to three months; the wild yeasts do the rest. Rack off the sediment, bottle when still, or with a teaspoon of sugar per bottle for fizz. Six to eight percent alcohol keeps it for a year and it is safer than untreated water ever was. Perry is the same with pears; **country wines** are the same with elderberry, blackberry, gooseberry, rhubarb, dandelion, parsnip, plus sugar (about 1 kg per 4.5 litres) and a pinch of yeast or a slice of bread on the surface to start. Beer needs malt (sprouted, dried, cracked barley) and hops or gruit herbs — a chapter of its own; the encyclopedia has it.

## Bacon, ham and sausages

**The nitrite question.** Salt alone preserves pork; it does not stop *Clostridium botulinum* growing in the anaerobic depths of a thick joint. **Curing salt** (salt with 6.25% sodium nitrite, "Prague powder #1") does; the pink colour and the flavour of bacon come from it, and the botulism protection is real. Buy it now and store it; it keeps for ever. Without it, cure only thin pieces (belly bacon under 4 cm), keep them cold, and cook them thoroughly — or air-dry hard and long with a high salt content the way Parma ham is made, which is a skill of its own.

**Dry-cured bacon.** For each kilo of pork belly: 30 g salt, 15 g sugar, 2.5 g curing salt #1, black pepper, bay, juniper. Rub in; put in a bag or a covered dish in the cold (fridge, cellar under 5 °C), turning daily and pouring off liquid, for 7 days (a day per 1.5 cm of thickness). Rinse, dry, hang in a cool airy place for two days to form a pellicle. Slice and cook; keeps three weeks cold, or smoke it (below) and it keeps two months.

**A ham** is the same on a leg, with the cure injected along the bone as well as rubbed on, for three weeks, then soaked, dried and either boiled or smoked.

**Sausages.** 700 g minced pork shoulder + 300 g pork fat, 2% salt (20 g), pepper, sage or mace, a splash of water, mixed until sticky; stuffed into cleaned intestines (the pig's own small intestine, turned, scraped, salted — or bought salted hog casings, which keep a year in salt) or simply rolled as patties. Fresh sausages keep three days; to keep longer, add curing salt at 2.5 g/kg and either smoke or dry.

## Smoking

**Cold smoke** (below 30 °C) flavours and dries, adds a little preservation, and does not cook: bacon, ham, cheese, fish, sausages, for 8–24 hours in a box or barrel fed by smoke from a separate fire a few metres away through a pipe, or from a smouldering coil of sawdust. Wood: oak, beech, apple, cherry, alder, hickory — never conifer, never treated timber. Cold-smoked food must be cured first. **Hot smoke** (70–120 °C) cooks as it smokes: fish (mackerel, trout — brined an hour first), chicken, sausages; eaten within days. A dustbin with a rack and a tray of wood chips over a small fire is a hot smoker; a wardrobe with a hole in the floor over a distant fire pit was the traditional cold one.

## Drying and the store cupboard

Everything above sits beside the basics in *Preserving food*: dried beans and grain, dried fruit and mushrooms and herbs, salt fish, rendered lard, potted meat under fat, root-clamped vegetables, and eggs in lime-water (waterglass or slaked lime: 1 part to 10 of water, eggs submerged, they keep six months). Together they are the difference between a harvest and a winter.
