# Keeping animals

Animals turn things you cannot eat — grass, scraps, weeds, insects, acorns — into things you can: eggs, milk, meat, honey, and manure for the garden. They also need feeding every day of the year, including the days you are ill, and they die of neglect faster than a garden does. Before keeping anything, answer three questions honestly: **what will it eat in February**, **who feeds it when you can't**, and **how will you replace it** when it dies or is eaten. An animal you cannot replace is a meal, not a livestock system.

Start small, with one species, and add another only when the first is routine.

## Chickens — start here

The best first animal. A hen lays an egg most days from spring to autumn for two or three years, eats kitchen scraps, weeds and pests, and needs a metre or two of space each.

**Housing.** A dry, draught-free but ventilated house with a perch 30–60 cm off the floor and one nest box per three or four hens, lined with straw or dry leaves. The whole thing must shut fast at night: foxes take a flock in one visit, and they will dig, so bury the run's mesh 30 cm or lay it flat outward on the ground. Rats come for the feed — keep it in a metal bin.

**Feed.** Bought layers' pellets are the easy answer while they exist. Without them: grain (wheat, barley, oats, maize), kitchen scraps, garden greens, and free range for insects and grit. A laying hen needs protein: worms, insects, a little meat scrap, peas or beans. She also needs **grit** to grind food and **calcium** for shells — baked, crushed eggshell or crushed oyster shell in a dish. Water always, unfrozen.

**Winter.** Laying slows or stops with short days; do not cull for it. Keep the house dry, the water liquid, and feed a little more grain in the evening so they go to roost with a full crop.

**Breeding.** One cockerel to eight or ten hens. A **broody** hen — one that sits tight on the nest and growls when moved — will hatch a clutch of ten to twelve eggs in 21 days and rear the chicks herself; some modern breeds have had broodiness bred out, so keep a couple of an old-fashioned breed or bantams for the job. Chicks need warmth for the first weeks (the hen does this), chick-sized feed, and protection from rats and crows. Half will be cockerels: eat them at four to six months.

**Health.** A healthy hen is bright-eyed, red-combed and busy. A hunched, fluffed-up bird standing apart is ill; separate it. Red mite live in the house, not on the bird, and come out at night — check the perch ends with a torch, and treat the house with boiling water and lime wash. Worm the flock with garlic and pumpkin seed in the feed if nothing better is to hand. Scaly legs: rub with vegetable oil weekly.

**Ducks** lay as well as hens, often better in winter, are hardier, and eat slugs, which alone justifies them. They need water deep enough to submerge their heads, not necessarily a pond, and they turn any wet ground into mud. Same predators, same rules.

## Rabbits — meat from grass

The most efficient meat animal for a small space. A doe produces six to ten kits per litter, four or five litters a year, and each kit is table weight at ten to twelve weeks — from one buck and three does, over a hundred rabbits a year, fed largely on grass, weeds and garden waste.

**Housing.** A hutch off the ground, dry, shaded in summer; one doe per hutch, the buck separate, young together until ten weeks then split by sex. Wire floors keep them clean but hurt their feet — give a solid board to rest on.

**Feed.** Hay always; grass, dandelion, plantain, clover, brassica leaves, carrot tops, apple prunings to gnaw; grain sparingly. Never lawn clippings that have heated, and introduce fresh greens gradually or they scour. Water always.

**Breeding.** Take the doe to the buck (never the other way), leave them ten minutes, repeat next day. Gestation 31 days; put a nest box with straw in on day 28. Do not disturb the nest for the first week. Wean at six to eight weeks.

**The warning.** Rabbit is almost fat-free. A person eating little else develops **protein poisoning** — nausea, diarrhoea, hunger that food does not satisfy — within weeks. Rabbit must be paired with fat: butter, lard, oil, nuts, fatty fish, or the fat of other animals.

## Goats — milk from rough ground

A goat in milk gives two to four litres a day for most of the year from browse — brambles, hedges, nettles, bark, scrub — that nothing else will eat. Goat's milk makes cheese, butter and yoghurt, and the kids are meat.

**They escape.** From anything. Fencing must be 1.2 m minimum, tight, and checked daily; tethering works for a few hours if the tether cannot tangle and there is shade and water. They strip bark and kill young trees; keep them out of the orchard.

**Housing.** A dry shed with a raised sleeping bench; goats hate rain and wet ground. Hay and a mineral lick. They need company — keep two.

**Milking.** Twice a day, twelve hours apart, from about a month after kidding for up to ten months. Clean hands, clean udder, a clean bucket, strain through cloth, cool at once. A doe must kid to come into milk, so you need a billy (which stinks and is dangerous when in rut) or access to one. Gestation 150 days; twins are usual.

**Sheep** give wool, milk and meat and thrive on grass alone; they need more land, good fencing rather than tall fencing, shearing every summer, foot-trimming, and watching at lambing (spring, five months after tupping). Ewes lamb outdoors in most British weather; the losses are to foxes, crows and cold wet nights.

## Pigs — the waste converter

A pig eats what nothing else will — whey, windfalls, acorns, spoiled crops, kitchen waste (raw meat and cooked kitchen scraps are illegal feed under current law; the law may not outlast the internet, but the disease risk that made it does) — and turns it into pork, lard and bacon at eight months. Two weaners bought in spring and killed in autumn is the traditional pattern and needs no breeding.

They need strong fencing (electric, or heavy rails and a dug-in bottom board), a dry ark, mud or shade in summer because they cannot sweat, and water they cannot tip over. They will plough any ground they are on, which clears rough land for next year's garden. A pig at slaughter weight is 80–100 kg: killing, scalding, scraping and butchering one is a two-person, whole-day job and needs to be learnt from someone who has done it, or from a book, *before* the day.

## Bees

A hive gives 10–30 kg of honey a year, wax for candles and waterproofing, and pollination for the fruit trees. Bees feed themselves.

What you need: a hive (a box with removable frames — a home-made top-bar hive works), a veil, a smoker, and a swarm, which arrives free in May or June if you set an empty hive in a warm spot with a little old comb inside. Look in every three weeks in summer for disease and space; take honey in August, leaving them at least 15 kg of stores to overwinter on. Wax moth and varroa mite are the enemies; a strong colony handles both. Stings are the price; anyone who has ever reacted badly to a sting should not keep bees.

## Feeding through winter

This is where livestock systems fail. Work out in summer what each animal eats per day, multiply by 150 days, and make, grow or store it:

- **Hay** for goats, sheep, rabbits: cut long grass in June on a dry spell, turn it for three days, stack it dry under cover. A goat eats about 1 kg a day.
- **Grain** for poultry and pigs: wheat, barley, oats. A hen eats about 120 g a day.
- **Roots** — mangolds, fodder beet, swedes, turnips — store in a clamp and feed to everything.
- **Kitchen waste, whey, windfalls** — to pigs and poultry.
- **Tree hay**: ash, elm, willow, lime and hazel branches cut in leaf in July and dried, fed to goats and sheep in winter. The old way, before hay meadows.

An animal that cannot be fed through winter is eaten in autumn. That is not a failure; it is how every farm managed before feed lorries.

## Killing and dressing

Do it calmly, quickly and out of sight of the others. The animal should be unstressed — a frightened animal gives tough, dark meat.

- **Chicken:** hold the legs, stretch the neck downward and twist sharply upward to break it, or use a killing cone and a sharp knife across the throat. Pluck at once while warm (or scald for a minute in water just too hot for the hand). Cut around the vent, draw the innards, keep the heart, liver and gizzard, discard the green gall bladder without breaking it.
- **Rabbit:** hold by the hind legs, a sharp blow to the back of the neck behind the ears, or a firm chin-pull to dislocate. Skin by cutting around the hocks and peeling the skin down like a jumper; open the belly, draw the guts.
- **Larger animals** need a captive-bolt or a firearm to the brain, then bleeding by cutting the throat immediately. Hang, skin, gut, and cool the carcass for a day before butchering. Learn this from someone, and practise on poultry first.

Use everything: bones for stock, fat rendered for cooking and candles, skins tanned (see *Cloth, rope and leather*), feathers for bedding, blood and offal eaten fresh, guts to the pigs or the compost. Meat that cannot be eaten within two days must be salted, smoked, dried or cooked and potted under fat (see *Preserving food*).

## The daily round

Morning: let out, feed, water, look at every animal. Evening: feed, water, count, shut in. Weekly: clean housing, check fences, check feet and skin. It takes half an hour a day for a small holding and cannot be skipped. Write down what you did and what you saw; that record is how you will notice a problem a day before it becomes a death.
