# Growing food and saving seed

A vegetable garden and a food supply are different projects. Salad leaves are pleasant and nutritionally useful and contain almost no calories. If you are growing to survive, you are growing **carbohydrate and fat first**, and everything else second.

## Calories per square metre

This is the number that matters and it is rarely discussed.

| Crop | Rough kcal per m² per season | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Potatoes** | 2,500–5,000 | The highest reliable calorie yield in temperate climates |
| **Sweet potatoes** | 2,000–4,000 | Warmer climates; leaves also edible |
| Maize / corn | 1,500–2,500 | Also stores well dry |
| Winter squash | 1,000–2,000 | Excellent storage, low effort |
| Beans (dry) | 500–1,000 | Protein, and they fix nitrogen |
| Wheat | 400–800 | Needs land, threshing and grinding |
| Cabbage / brassicas | 300–600 | Vitamins, cold-hardy, long season |
| Salad leaves | 50–100 | Nutrition, not calories |

An adult needs roughly **2,000–2,500 kcal a day**, so about 800,000 a year. At potato yields that is **200–300 m² per person minimum**, before crop failure, pests, poor years and the fact that nobody can live on potatoes alone. Plan for double, and grow variety so that one failure is not a catastrophe.

This arithmetic is sobering and it is better to meet it now than later.

## Soil

Soil is a living system, and in a world with no fertiliser deliveries it is your only source of fertility. Everything you grow removes nutrients; everything must be returned.

- **Compost everything organic.** Kitchen waste, weeds before they seed, leaves, straw, cardboard, wood ash in moderation. Alternate green (nitrogen) and brown (carbon) layers, keep it damp as a wrung-out sponge, and turn it.
- **Human urine** is a genuinely excellent nitrogen source, diluted 1:10, applied to soil not leaves. It is free, sterile from a healthy person, and produced daily.
- **Wood ash** supplies potassium and raises pH. Use sparingly, and not around acid-loving plants.
- **Nitrogen-fixing plants** — beans, peas, clover, vetch, lupins — put nitrogen back. Grow them in rotation and dig the residues in.
- **Mulch heavily.** It suppresses weeds, holds moisture, moderates soil temperature and feeds the soil as it breaks down. In a drought it is the difference between a crop and no crop.
- **Do not dig more than you need to.** Repeated deep digging destroys soil structure and burns through organic matter.

**Rotate crops.** The classic four-year cycle: legumes → brassicas → roots → potatoes, then back. It breaks pest and disease cycles and balances nutrient demand. Growing the same thing in the same place year after year is how you get a garden full of disease.

## Seed saving

Bought seed is a supply chain. Saved seed is not — but only if you save the right kind.

> **Warning** **F1 hybrid seed does not come true.** The plants grown from seed you save off an F1 hybrid will be a scattered, mostly inferior mix of the parent genetics. Almost all commercial vegetable seed is F1. For a seed supply that renews itself you must grow **open-pollinated** or **heirloom** varieties. Check the packet, and start converting now — it takes several seasons to build up a reliable stock.

Basics by difficulty:

- **Easiest — self-pollinating:** tomatoes, beans, peas, lettuce. They rarely cross, so you can grow several varieties nearby and still save true seed. Start here.
- **Harder — insect-pollinated:** squash, cucumbers, brassicas. They cross readily; you need isolation distance, or hand pollination with bagged flowers.
- **Hardest — biennial:** carrots, beets, onions, cabbage. They only flower in their second year, so plants must be overwintered specifically for seed.

Practical rules: save from **many plants, not the best single one** — genetic diversity is what gives resilience. Dry seed thoroughly, store cool, dark and dry, label with variety and year. Most vegetable seed stays viable 3–5 years; onion and parsnip barely a year. Test germination each spring on a damp cloth before committing a bed to it.

**Store a deep seed reserve now** — a sealed, dated box of open-pollinated varieties in a cool dark place, refreshed on a rotation. It is small, cheap and irreplaceable.

## Perennials — the best return on effort

Annual vegetables need replanting, replanning and reworking every year. Perennials, once established, produce for decades with a fraction of the labour.

Fruit and nut trees · soft fruit bushes · rhubarb · asparagus · globe artichokes · perennial herbs · walking onions · Jerusalem artichokes (enormously productive, extremely hard to get rid of, and — fair warning — famous for causing flatulence).

**Plant trees now.** A fruit tree planted this year begins producing in three to eight years. There is no way to hurry that, and no better argument for starting before you need to.

## Water in the garden

- **Mulch** before irrigation. It halves water demand.
- **Water deeply and less often** — it drives roots down. Frequent shallow watering makes shallow-rooted plants that die the moment you stop.
- Water at the **base, in the morning**. Wet leaves overnight invite fungal disease.
- **Swales and contour beds** on a slope catch runoff and let it soak in rather than run off.
- Greywater from washing is fine on established fruit trees and non-food plants.

## Pests without pesticides

- **Physical barriers** first: netting, fleece, collars around stems, copper against slugs.
- **Encourage predators** — ladybirds, hoverflies, birds, frogs, ground beetles. A pond and some rough corners do more than any spray.
- **Rotation and timing** — sowing outside a pest's peak breaks its cycle.
- **Diversity.** A monoculture is a target; mixed planting confuses pests and limits the damage from any one.
- Accept some loss. Aiming for a perfect crop costs more than the loss does.

## Livestock, briefly

- **Chickens** are the best first animal: eggs almost daily, they eat scraps and pests, they need little space, and they are simple to keep. Consider whether you can hatch replacements — a cockerel and a broody hen make the flock self-sustaining.
- **Rabbits** breed fast, eat greens humans cannot, and are efficient producers of meat in small spaces. Rabbit meat is extremely lean; a diet of nothing else causes protein poisoning, so it must be paired with a fat source.
- **Goats** browse rough ground, give milk, and escape from anything you build.
- **Bees** give honey, wax and — far more importantly — pollination for everything else you grow.

Whatever you keep, you need a plan for feeding it through winter, and a plan for breeding it. An animal you cannot replace is a meal, not a livestock system.
