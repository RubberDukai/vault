# Starting a food garden from nothing

*Growing food* covers the principles — calories per square metre, soil, water, seed, pests. This chapter is the *how*, for a person standing in front of a patch of grass with a packet of seeds and no idea. It is written for Britain; the last section translates it.

## What to expect

- **The first year feeds you a little and teaches you a lot.** A well-run 100 m² plot (a standard allotment is 250 m²) yields perhaps a quarter of one adult's calories in year one and half by year three. Expect that, plan on the stores, and be pleased with the surplus.
- **Time:** an hour a day in spring and early summer, less after. It cannot be skipped in May.
- **The order of work:** clear → dig or cover → beds and paths → sow the reliable crops → water → weed → harvest → store → save seed → cover for winter.

## Tools — buy old and good, or make

Essential: **spade, fork, rake, hoe** (a Dutch hoe for weeding standing up; a draw hoe for earthing up), **trowel, hand fork, watering can** with a rose, **wheelbarrow, string and pegs, a knife, secateurs, buckets, gloves**. Very useful: a dibber (a pointed stick), a garden line, a riddle (sieve), a small sharpening stone, netting, canes or hazel rods, cloches (a jar, a cut bottle, a window), a rain gauge. For a bigger plot: a scythe and a mattock. Nothing needs a motor. Keep tools clean, dry, sharp and oiled (see *Tools and keeping an edge*).

## Year zero: making ground

**Grass to garden, the easy way:** in autumn or winter, mow it short, cover the whole area with **cardboard** (overlapping, no gaps), and put 10–15 cm of manure, compost, leaf mould or even just grass clippings and straw on top. By spring the grass is dead, the worms have pulled the cardboard in, and you plant straight into the top. This is "no-dig" and it is the best method for a beginner: fewer weeds, less work, better soil, and no one needs to be able to double-dig. If you have no mulch material, dig instead: skim the turf off and stack it grass-down to rot into loam, then fork the soil over, removing perennial weed roots (couch grass, bindweed, dock, dandelion — every white root you leave regrows).

**Beds and paths.** Make beds **1.2 m wide** (reachable from both sides without treading on the soil) with 40 cm paths between. Never walk on a bed; compaction is the enemy of roots. Raised edges (planks, logs, stones) are optional and help on wet clay.

**Compost.** Start the heap on day one: everything green (kitchen scraps, weeds without seed, grass), everything brown (cardboard, straw, dead leaves, woody prunings chopped small) in roughly equal amounts, kept damp, turned when you remember. A year later it is the best thing you can put on a bed. Manure from any herbivore, stacked six months. Human urine, diluted 1:10, is a first-rate nitrogen feed and a waste to flush.

## The first-year crops — reliable, filling, forgiving

Plant these and little else in year one:

| Crop | Why | How much for four people |
|---|---|---|
| **Potatoes** | Most calories per m², break new ground, almost never fail | 20–30 m², 5 kg seed |
| **Onions / garlic / leeks** | Store all winter, flavour everything | 10 m² |
| **Climbing beans and broad beans** | Protein, dry for winter, feed the soil | 10 m² |
| **Squash and pumpkin** | Calories that store till spring with no work | 6 plants |
| **Kale, cabbage, chard** | Greens through the whole winter and hungry gap | 10 m² |
| **Carrots, beetroot, parsnips** | Roots that store in the ground or in sand | 10 m² |
| **Peas** | Eat fresh, dry the rest | 5 m² |
| **Courgettes, lettuce, radish, spring onions, herbs** | Fast, morale, salad | a few m² |
| **Jerusalem artichoke** | Unkillable perennial calories in a corner | one corner, permanently |

Skip in year one: sweetcorn, cauliflower, celery, tomatoes outdoors, anything that needs a greenhouse or perfect timing.

## The sowing calendar — Britain

Dates are for lowland England; add two weeks in the north and uplands, take a week off in the south-west. "Last frost" is mid-May in most of Britain; nothing tender goes out before it.

| Month | Sow indoors / under cover | Sow direct outside | Plant out | Harvest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Jan** | onions from seed, broad beans in pots | — (garlic if not done) | — | leeks, parsnips, kale, sprouts, stored crops |
| **Feb** | leeks, lettuce, early brassicas, chillies | broad beans (mild areas), early peas under cloches | garlic, shallots, chitted early potatoes under fleece (late Feb, south) | as January; forced rhubarb |
| **Mar** | tomatoes, celery, brassicas, lettuce, leeks | peas, broad beans, parsnips, early carrots, radish, spinach, spring onions, beetroot (late) | early potatoes, onion sets, shallots | purple sprouting broccoli, kale, leeks, spring cabbage |
| **Apr** | courgettes, squash, sweetcorn, French and runner beans (late), cucumbers | maincrop carrots, beetroot, peas, turnips, lettuce, chard, salad, radish, spinach | maincrop potatoes, onion sets, leeks (as seedlings), brassica plants under net | spring greens, asparagus, rhubarb, salads, the last leeks |
| **May** | — (last sowings of tender crops) | runner and French beans (after mid-month), sweetcorn, courgettes, squash (late), carrots, beetroot, chard, salads, swede, kale | tomatoes, courgettes, squash, beans, sweetcorn (after last frost), brassicas | broad beans, peas, salads, radish, asparagus, early potatoes (late) |
| **Jun** | — | beans, beetroot, carrots, turnips, salads, chicory, kale for winter | leeks, brassicas, the last squash and beans | peas, broad beans, early potatoes, salads, strawberries, courgettes begin |

| **Aug** | — | spring cabbage, winter salads, spinach, turnips, oriental greens, green manure on empty beds | spring cabbage plants (late) | onions, garlic, beans (dry some), tomatoes, sweetcorn, courgettes, plums, early apples |

**Succession:** sow salads, radish, carrots and beetroot little and often — a metre row every fortnight — rather than once and all at once.

## Rotation — the four-year wheel

Do not grow the same family in the same bed two years running: pests and diseases build up in the soil, and each family takes and gives different things. Divide the plot in four and move each group on a bed every year:

1. **Potatoes** (and tomatoes) — manure the bed first
2. **Legumes** (peas, beans) — they leave nitrogen behind →
3. **Brassicas** (cabbage, kale, swede, turnip, radish) — use that nitrogen; lime the bed if acid →
4. **Roots and onions** (carrot, parsnip, beetroot, onion, leek, garlic) — no fresh manure, or the roots fork → back to potatoes.

Squash, sweetcorn, salads and chard go wherever there is room. Perennials (rhubarb, asparagus, artichokes, fruit, herbs) have their own permanent beds.

## Water, weeds and the rest

- **Water** seedlings and transplants daily, established crops deeply once a week rather than a little every day; mulch to keep it in. Squash, beans and leafy crops need most; onions and roots least. See *Water in the garden*.
- **Weed** with a hoe on a dry day when weeds are seedlings — ten minutes a week beats a day a month. Never let a weed set seed: "one year's seeding, seven years' weeding".
- **Protect:** netting for brassicas and fruit (pigeons, butterflies, birds), fleece for carrots (fly) and early sowings, a fence or a dog for rabbits and deer, beer traps and evening rounds for slugs, and hens after harvest to clear a bed.
- **Feed:** compost or manure on the potato and brassica beds each year, comfrey liquid (comfrey leaves rotted in a bucket, diluted) for fruiting crops, wood ash for potash. See *Soil*.
- **Save seed** from the best plants of peas, beans, tomatoes, squash (one variety), lettuce, onions in year two; see *Seed saving*.

## Perennials and the orchard — year one's best investment

Plant in the first winter and they feed you for decades: **apple, pear, plum, damson, cherry** (bare-root, November–March, on a rootstock suited to your space), **currants, gooseberries, raspberries, rhubarb, asparagus** (three years to crop), **globe artichoke, perennial kale, Jerusalem artichoke, herbs**, a **hazel** or two, a **walnut** if there is room, and a hedge of hawthorn, blackthorn, crab apple and elder that feeds the family and the bees. A tree planted the year the internet goes down is what the children eat in ten years.

## The hungry gap and the year's rhythm

From March to May the store empties and the garden has not started. What bridges it: leeks, kale, purple sprouting broccoli, spring cabbage, chard, overwintered onions and broad beans, stored roots and squash, the last potatoes, and the hedgerow (see *Foraging*). Plan for it in the previous July, which is when the winter crops are sown. A garden is planned a year ahead; the calendar above is the plan.

## Taking it to another climate

Find the local last and first frost dates and the wettest and driest months; stretch or compress the calendar to fit. In a Mediterranean climate the dead season is summer, not winter, and the garden runs October to May. In the tropics there is no frost and the calendar follows the rains. The crops change — cassava, sweet potato, taro, maize, plantain, beans, squash, greens — but the method does not: make ground, compost, beds, reliable staples first, water, weed, store, save seed, rotate. The encyclopedia article for any crop gives its sowing needs; the local people give the timing.
