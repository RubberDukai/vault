# Preserving food

A harvest is not food security. A harvest you can still eat in February is.

## The four levers

Every preservation method in history works by removing something microbes need:

1. **Water** — drying, salting, sugaring. Below about 0.6 water activity, nothing grows.
2. **Warmth** — cold storage, freezing, root cellaring.
3. **Neutral pH** — pickling and fermenting push acidity below pH 4.6, where the dangerous organisms cannot function.
4. **Oxygen** — fermenting and sealing, which favours the organisms you want over the ones you do not.

Most good methods use two or three at once. Salted, dried and smoked fish removes water, adds antimicrobial compounds and keeps the surface hostile.

## Drying

The oldest and least demanding method. It needs warmth, moving air and low humidity.

- **Slice thin and evenly.** Thickness controls drying time and uneven pieces spoil in the middle.
- **Air-dry** on racks in shade with good airflow, or in a solar dryer — a dark box with vents and a clear lid, which reaches higher temperatures than open air without cooking the food.
- Target **60 °C or below** for fruit and vegetables. Higher and the outside seals, trapping moisture inside — "case hardening", which produces something that looks dry and rots from the centre.
- **Test properly:** it should be leathery to brittle with no moisture when a piece is torn and squeezed. Cool it first — warm food always feels drier than it is.
- **Condition** dried fruit before storing: seal it loosely in a jar for a week and shake daily. Any remaining moisture equalises, and condensation on the glass tells you it is not dry enough.
- Store in airtight containers away from light. Check for mould in the first fortnight.

**Meat** dries safely only when very lean, sliced thin, and either salted first or dried hot enough. Fat does not dry; it goes rancid. Trim it all off.

## Salting and curing

Salt draws water out of both the food and the microbes.

- **Dry curing:** 2–3% salt by weight of the meat for a fresh cure; far more for long keeping.
- **Brining:** a heavy brine floats a fresh egg. Traditional, imprecise, and it works.
- Salt-cured food must be **soaked or rinsed** before cooking or it is inedible.

> **Warning** Curing salts containing nitrite (pink salt, Prague powder) are used in traditional charcuterie specifically to prevent botulism in cured meats. They are also toxic in quantity and are dyed pink so they are never confused with table salt. If you are curing whole muscle or sausages for long keeping, learn the correct rates from a proper reference and measure by weight — this is not a place for estimation.

## Fermenting

Lactic acid bacteria, which are already present on vegetables, convert sugars to lactic acid, dropping the pH below where spoilage organisms survive. It is the safest of the traditional methods and it *increases* nutritional value.

- **Brine at 2–3% salt by weight** of vegetables plus water. For 1 kg of vegetables and water, use 20–30 g of salt.
- **Keep everything below the brine.** A weight, a cabbage leaf, a bag of brine. Anything above the surface will mould.
- Ferment at room temperature for days to weeks, then move somewhere cold to slow it.
- It should smell sour and clean. **Fuzzy mould, pink or black growth, or a putrid smell means throw it out.** A white film on the surface (kahm yeast) is harmless but unpleasant — skim it.
- Sauerkraut, kimchi and salt-brined cucumbers, beans and carrots all follow this one procedure.

Fermented vegetables were how sailors and northern Europeans got vitamin C through winter. That is not a small thing — scurvy is a genuine risk on a preserved-food diet.

## Canning, and the part that kills people

This is the one method that can kill a healthy person who does everything else right.

**Clostridium botulinum** forms spores that survive boiling. In an anaerobic, low-acid, moist environment — precisely the inside of a sealed jar of green beans — those spores germinate and produce botulinum toxin, among the most lethal substances known. It is odourless and tasteless. The food looks and smells fine.

The dividing line is **pH 4.6**.

**High-acid foods (pH below 4.6) — water bath canning is safe.** Boiling water at 100 °C is sufficient because the acidity prevents the spores germinating.
Fruit · jams and jellies · pickles in vinegar · tomatoes **with added acid** (bottled lemon juice or citric acid — modern tomato varieties are not reliably acidic enough on their own) · properly fermented vegetables.

**Low-acid foods (pH above 4.6) — pressure canning only.**
All vegetables · meat, poultry and fish · beans · soups and stews · anything with dairy · mixtures containing any of these.

These require a **pressure canner reaching 116 °C (240 °F)**, held for a tested time. There is no substitute. A boiling water bath, an oven, a microwave or a pressure *cooker* not rated and tested for canning will not do it.

> **Warning** If you have no pressure canner, do not can low-acid food. Dry it, ferment it, salt it, or keep it cold instead. And never taste-test suspect preserved food to check it — the toxic dose is measured in nanograms. Discard bulging, leaking, spurting or foul-smelling jars without opening them where possible, and treat the contents as hazardous. When in doubt, throw it out.

Symptoms of botulism appear 12–36 hours later: double or blurred vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, difficulty swallowing, then descending paralysis. It is a medical emergency requiring antitoxin. There is no home treatment.

## Cold storage

- **Root cellar:** ideally 0–4 °C with high humidity. Below frost line, north-facing, ventilated. Stores potatoes, carrots, beets, turnips, cabbages and apples for months.
- **Keep apples away from everything else.** They release ethylene, which sprouts potatoes and ages other produce fast.
- **Clamps** — root vegetables in a heap on straw, covered with more straw and then earth — work with no construction at all.
- **Sand boxes** for carrots and beets: layered in barely damp sand, they stay firm for months.
- Do not wash produce before storing. Brush the soil off dry, and cure potatoes, onions and squash for a week or two before they go in.

## Fats and oils

Fats go rancid through oxidation, driven by light, heat, air and time. Store in dark, cool, full, sealed containers. Rendered animal fat — lard, tallow, ghee — keeps far longer than most vegetable oils, and it is calorie-dense in a way that matters when calories are short. Rendered and stored properly in a cool dark place, tallow keeps for a year or more.

## Storing grain and beans

- **Dry to below 13% moisture**, sealed, cool, dark, and protected from rodents and insects.
- Oxygen absorbers in sealed buckets or mylar kill insect eggs and prevent rancidity. Properly done, grain keeps for decades.
- **Whole grain keeps far longer than flour.** The moment you break the germ, the oils start oxidising — flour goes stale in months, wheat berries last twenty years. Store whole and grind as needed, and own a hand grinder.
