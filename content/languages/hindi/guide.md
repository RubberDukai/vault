# Hindi — how it works

Hindi is spoken across northern India and understood far beyond it; with Urdu, which is the same spoken language in a different script, it reaches well over half a billion people. It is an Indo-European language — a distant cousin of English — and a surprising amount is familiar: *nām* (name), *mātā* (mother), *do* (two), *nau* (nine), *dānt* (tooth). The grammar is regular, the spelling is perfectly phonetic, and the sounds are the main hurdle.

## Devanagari — the script

Forty-odd letters, each with one sound, written left to right, with a line along the top joining the letters of a word. Each consonant carries an inherent short *a* (क *ka*); other vowels are marked by strokes around it (का *kā*, कि *ki*, की *kī*, कु *ku*, के *ke*, को *ko*). A consonant with no vowel takes a small slash underneath (क् *k*).

Learn it. It takes a couple of weeks, and once you know it you can read every word aloud correctly, which is more than can be said of English. The decks give transliteration alongside every card until then.

## The sounds

**Vowels** come in short and long pairs, and the length matters: *kal* (yesterday / tomorrow) and *kāl* (time) differ only in the vowel.

| | Sounds like |
|---|---|
| **a** | *u* in cup (short) |
| **ā** | *a* in father (long) |
| **i / ī** | *i* in sit / *ee* in seen |
| **u / ū** | *u* in put / *oo* in moon |
| **e** | *a* in gate |
| **ai** | *a* in bat |
| **o** | *o* in go |
| **au** | *o* in hot |

**Consonants** are where an English speaker has to work. Hindi distinguishes four kinds of *t* and four kinds of *d*:

- **Dental** त *t*, द *d* — tongue against the teeth, soft, like Spanish or Italian.
- **Retroflex** ट *ṭ*, ड *ḍ* — tongue curled back to the roof of the mouth, hard. This is the sound that makes Indian English distinctive, and it is a different letter.
- **Aspirated** versions of both — थ *th*, ध *dh*, ठ *ṭh*, ढ *ḍh* — with a puff of air.

The aspirated / unaspirated distinction runs through the whole alphabet: *k / kh*, *p / ph*, *c / ch*, *b / bh*, *g / gh*, *j / jh*. Hold a hand in front of your mouth: aspirated sounds move it. *Kal* and *khal* are different words.

Nasalised vowels — marked in transliteration with a tilde or *n*, and in the script with a dot above — are said through the nose: *hān* (yes), *main* (I).

## Word order

**Subject – Object – Verb**, like Japanese. The verb comes last.

*Main pānī pītā hūn.* — I water drink am. *I drink water.*
*Vah ghar jātā hai.* — He home goes is. *He goes home.*

Everything before the verb is fairly free in order; the verb stays at the end.

## Postpositions, not prepositions

Where English puts a word *before* the noun, Hindi puts it *after*:

| Hindi | English | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **में** men | in | *ghar men* — in the house |
| **पर** par | on, at | *mez par* — on the table |
| **से** se | from, by, with | *Dillī se* — from Delhi |
| **को** ko | to (a person); marks the object | *mujhko* / *mujhe* — to me |
| **का / की / के** kā / kī / ke | of, 's | *Rām kā ghar* — Ram's house |
| **के लिए** ke lie | for | *āp ke lie* — for you |
| **के साथ** ke sāth | with | *mere sāth* — with me |
| **तक** tak | until, up to | *shām tak* — until evening |

The possessive *kā / kī / ke* agrees with the thing possessed, not the possessor: *Rām kā ghar* (house, masc.), *Rām kī bahan* (sister, fem.).

## Gender and agreement

Every noun is masculine or feminine. Mostly, nouns ending in **-ā** are masculine (*laṛkā*, boy) and in **-ī** feminine (*laṛkī*, girl); the rest must be learned. Adjectives ending in -ā agree: *acchā laṛkā*, *acchī laṛkī* (good boy, good girl), *acche laṛke* (good boys). Other adjectives (*sundar*, beautiful) never change.

Verbs agree with the subject in gender and number too, which is the main way Hindi differs from English in feel: a man says *main jātā hūn* (I go), a woman *main jātī hūn*.

## Verbs — one pattern

The dictionary form ends in **-nā**: *jānā* (to go), *khānā* (to eat), *karnā* (to do). Remove *-nā* for the stem: *jā-*, *khā-*, *kar-*.

**Present** — stem + *-tā / -tī / -te* (masc. sing. / fem. / masc. plural) + the verb "to be":

| | masculine | feminine |
|---|---|---|
| main (I) | jā**tā hūn** | jā**tī hūn** |
| tum (you) | jā**te ho** | jā**tī ho** |
| āp (you, polite) | jā**te hain** | jā**tī hain** |
| vah (he / she) | jā**tā hai** | jā**tī hai** |
| ham (we) | jā**te hain** | jā**tī hain** |
| ve (they) | jā**te hain** | jā**tī hain** |

**Continuous** (am doing): stem + *rahā / rahī / rahe* + "to be". *Main jā rahā hūn.* I am going.

**Future**: stem + *-ūngā / -egā / -enge…* *Main jāūngā.* I will go. *Vah jāegā.* He will go.

**Past** (simple): stem + *-ā / -ī / -e*. *Vah gayā.* He went (irregular: *jānā* → *gayā*). *Usne khāyā.* He ate — note **ne** after the subject with transitive verbs in the past, the one genuinely tricky rule.

**"To be"**: *hūn, ho, hai, hain* (present); *thā, thī, the, thīn* (past).

**Negation**: *nahīn* before the verb. *Main nahīn jātā.* I don't go. (The *hūn* is usually dropped in the negative.)

## Three levels of "you"

- **तू tū** — intimate, or rude. Children, God, very close friends. Avoid it.
- **तुम tum** — familiar. Friends, family, people younger than you.
- **आप āp** — polite. Everyone else, and anyone older. **Use this by default.** It takes the plural verb form.

Politeness is built into verbs: the polite request is *-ie* on the stem — *dījie* (please give), *āie* (please come), *baiṭhie* (please sit) — and *jī* after a name or a yes/no adds respect: *hān jī*, *nahīn jī*.

## Questions

**क्या kyā** at the start makes any statement a question: *Kyā āp ṭhīk hain?* Are you all right? Otherwise question words sit where the answer would: *Āp kahān jāte hain?* You where go? *Yah kyā hai?* This what is?

## Numbers

*Ek, do, tīn, cār, pānc, chah, sāt, āṭh, nau, das.* Then *gyārah* (11) … *bīs* (20), *sau* (100), *hazār* (1,000), and the South Asian units **lākh** (100,000) and **karoṛ** (10,000,000). The numbers from 11 to 99 are each irregular and are learned one by one, slowly, by everyone.

## Learning it

1. The script — one letter group a day for two weeks.
2. The sounds: dental against retroflex, aspirated against not. Hand in front of the mouth.
3. The survival phrases, with *āp* and *-ie* forms, so you are polite from the first word.
4. The present tense pattern, in both genders, with the postpositions.
5. Core words. The verb *karnā* (to do) makes hundreds of verbs from nouns: *kām karnā* (to work), *madad karnā* (to help), *bāt karnā* (to talk).
