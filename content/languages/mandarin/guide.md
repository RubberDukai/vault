# Mandarin — how it works

Mandarin has the simplest grammar of any major language and the hardest writing system. **No verb conjugation, no tenses, no plurals, no gender, no cases, no articles.** Words never change shape. What makes it hard is that every syllable has a **tone** that is part of the word, and that reading requires thousands of characters. Learn to speak with Pinyin first; the characters come slowly and separately.

## Pinyin — the Latin alphabet for Chinese

Pinyin writes Mandarin sounds in Roman letters. It is how Chinese children learn, how phones type Chinese, and how you will learn. A few letters do not sound as English expects:

| Pinyin | Sounds like |
|---|---|
| **q** | *ch* in cheese, with the tongue tip down: *qǐng* |
| **x** | between *sh* and *s*, tongue tip down: *xièxie* |
| **zh** | *j* in judge, tongue curled back: *zhè* |
| **c** | *ts* in cats: *cèsuǒ* |
| **z** | *ds* in beds: *zài* |
| **r** | between *r* and the *s* in measure: *rén* |
| **ü** | *u* with lips rounded as for *ee*: *nǚ* (woman) |
| **-i** after z, c, s, zh, ch, sh, r | a buzz, not *ee*: *shì* is roughly "shr" |
| **-ian** | *yen*: *tiān* is "tyen" |
| **-ong** | *oong*: *dōng* |

## The four tones

The same syllable in a different tone is a different word. **Mā** (mother), **má** (hemp), **mǎ** (horse), **mà** (to scold). The tone marks in Pinyin are not accents; they are as much part of the word as the consonant.

| Tone | Mark | Shape | Say it like |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st | **ā** | high and flat | singing a steady note |
| 2nd | **á** | rising | a question: "what?" |
| 3rd | **ǎ** | falling then rising | a doubtful "we-ell…" |
| 4th | **à** | sharp fall | a command: "No!" |
| neutral | **a** | short, light | the *a* in "sofa" |

Practise tones on single syllables until they are automatic — *mā má mǎ mà* — and then on two-syllable words, where the third tone before another third tone becomes a second: *nǐ hǎo* is said *ní hǎo*.

Get the tones wrong and you will often still be understood from context — but *mǎi* (buy) and *mài* (sell) are a bad pair to confuse.

## Word order

**Subject – Verb – Object**, like English. *Wǒ hē shuǐ.* I drink water. *Tā shì yīshēng.* He is a doctor.

**Time goes before the verb**, and usually right after the subject: *Wǒ míngtiān qù.* I tomorrow go. *Wǒ zuótiān chī le.* I yesterday ate.

**Place goes before the verb** too: *Wǒ zài jiā chīfàn.* I at home eat.

## No tenses — time words instead

The verb never changes. When something happens is shown by a time word, or by a particle:

- **Time words:** *jīntiān* today, *míngtiān* tomorrow, *zuótiān* yesterday, *xiànzài* now, *yǐqián* before, *yǐhòu* after.
- **了 le** after the verb: the action is completed. *Wǒ chī le.* I ate / I have eaten.
- **过 guo** after the verb: have had the experience of. *Wǒ qù guo Zhōngguó.* I have been to China.
- **在 zài** before the verb: in the middle of doing. *Wǒ zài chīfàn.* I am eating.
- **会 huì** or **要 yào** before the verb: will, going to. *Wǒ huì qù.* I will go.

## Questions

Three ways, all easy:

- **吗 ma** at the end of a statement. *Nǐ hǎo ma?* Are you well? *Nǐ yǒu shuǐ ma?* Do you have water?
- **Verb-not-verb:** *Nǐ yǒu méi yǒu shuǐ?* You have not-have water? *Nǐ qù bu qù?* Are you going?
- **Question words** in place of the answer, with no change in word order: *Nǐ qù nǎlǐ?* You go where? *Zhè shì shénme?* This is what?

| | |
|---|---|
| 什么 shénme | what |
| 哪里 nǎlǐ | where |
| 谁 shéi | who |
| 为什么 wèishénme | why |
| 什么时候 shénme shíhou | when |
| 怎么 zěnme | how |
| 多少 duōshao | how many, how much |

## Negation

**不 bù** before the verb: *Wǒ bù qù.* I'm not going. *Bù hǎo.* Not good.

**没 méi** for *yǒu* (have) and for completed actions: *Wǒ méi yǒu shuǐ.* I don't have water. *Wǒ méi chī.* I didn't eat.

## Measure words

You cannot say "three people" — you say "three *units-of* people": *sān **ge** rén*. Every noun takes a measure word between the number and the noun. There are dozens; **个 ge** is the general one and works for almost anything when you do not know the right one. A few others worth knowing: **杯 bēi** (cups: *yì bēi shuǐ*, a cup of water), **本 běn** (books), **条 tiáo** (long things: rivers, fish, roads), **只 zhī** (animals), **张 zhāng** (flat things: paper, tables).

## Adjectives and 很

Adjectives are verbs. *Tā gāo* means "he is tall" — no *is* needed. But a bare adjective sounds like a comparison, so in ordinary statements a **很 hěn** goes in front: *Tā hěn gāo.* He is (very) tall. The *hěn* has almost no meaning here; it is grammatical padding.

## 的 de — the all-purpose possessive

**的 de** links a possessor to a thing, or a description to a noun. *Wǒ de shuǐ* — my water. *Hóng de huā* — red flower. *Zhōngguó de rén* — Chinese people. It is the most common character in the language.

## Characters

Each character is one syllable and one unit of meaning, made from strokes written in a fixed order. About 3,000 cover daily life; 1,000 cover 90% of ordinary text. Most are built from a **radical** hinting at meaning and a component hinting at sound: 河 (river) has the water radical 氵 and the sound component 可 *kě*. Learn radicals first, then the hundred commonest characters by meaning, and read anything with Pinyin above it.

Simplified characters (mainland China, Singapore) and traditional (Taiwan, Hong Kong) are the same language in two scripts; the Vault's decks use simplified.

## Learning it

1. Pinyin and the four tones, aloud, until *mā má mǎ mà* is automatic.
2. The survival phrases, with tones, from the deck.
3. The core words. With no conjugation, vocabulary is nearly all of it.
4. The question patterns and *le*, *bù*, *méi*, *de*, *hěn* — six particles that carry the grammar.
5. Characters, a few a day, by radical.
