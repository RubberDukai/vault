# Japanese — how it works

Japanese has a reputation for difficulty that is only half deserved. **Speaking it is easier than most European languages** — five vowels, no tones, no gender, no plurals, no verb agreement, and a grammar so regular that exceptions are rare. **Reading it is hard**, because it uses three scripts at once, and one of them has thousands of characters. Learn to speak and listen first; read alongside; do not let the writing stop you talking.

## The sounds

Every syllable is a consonant plus a vowel, or a vowel alone, or the lone **n**. The five vowels never change:

| | Sounds like |
|---|---|
| **a** | f**a**ther |
| **i** | mach**i**ne |
| **u** | fl**u**te, lips relaxed not rounded |
| **e** | b**e**d |
| **o** | m**o**re |

Every syllable takes the **same length of time** — Japanese is a rhythm of even beats, not stressed and unstressed syllables. *Sa-yo-u-na-ra* is five even beats. Getting this rhythm right does more for being understood than anything else.

**Long vowels matter.** *Obasan* (aunt) and *obaasan* (grandmother) are different words. *Kōkō* (high school) has two long o's. In romanisation a long vowel is marked with a line (ō, ū) or doubled (oo, uu).

**Doubled consonants** are a pause: *kite* (come) and *kitte* (stamp) differ by a held beat before the *t*.

**r** is a light tap of the tongue, between English *r*, *l* and *d*. **fu** is breathier than English *f*. **Pitch** varies between regions and rarely changes meaning; ignore it at first.

## Three scripts

**Hiragana** (ひらがな) — 46 flowing characters, one per syllable. Used for grammatical endings, particles, and words with no kanji. **Learn this first, completely.** It takes a week, and it unlocks everything else, including reading kanji through the small hiragana printed above them in children's books.

**Katakana** (カタカナ) — the same 46 sounds in angular shapes. Used for foreign words, names, sounds, and emphasis. *Kōhī* (coffee), *terebi* (television), *Rondon* (London). Learn it second; it is the script on menus, signs and packaging.

**Kanji** (漢字) — Chinese characters, each carrying meaning as well as sound. There are about 2,000 in daily use; children learn 1,000 in primary school. Each has a Japanese reading (*kun*) and one or more Chinese-derived readings (*on*): 水 is *mizu* alone and *sui* in compounds like 水曜日 *suiyōbi*, Wednesday. Learn the first hundred by meaning and shape; readings come with the words you meet them in.

## Word order

**Subject – Object – Verb.** The verb comes last, always.

*Watashi wa mizu o nomimasu.* — I (topic) water (object) drink. *I drink water.*

Everything that is not the verb can be dropped if it is understood. *Nomimasu* alone means "I drink / I will drink" in context, and Japanese conversation drops far more than English does. Do not say *watashi* every sentence; it sounds insistent.

## Particles — the joints of the sentence

Small words after a noun that say what job it does. These are the grammar. There are only a dozen that matter.

| Particle | Marks | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **は** wa | the topic — what we are talking about | *Watashi **wa** isha desu.* I am a doctor. |
| **が** ga | the subject — who or what does it, or what exists | *Mizu **ga** arimasu.* There is water. |
| **を** o | the object — what the verb acts on | *Pan **o** tabemasu.* I eat bread. |
| **に** ni | destination, location of existence, time | *Tōkyō **ni** ikimasu.* I go to Tokyo. *Roku-ji **ni**.* At six. |
| **で** de | where an action happens; by means of | *Ie **de** tabemasu.* I eat at home. *Basu **de**.* By bus. |
| **へ** e | direction toward | *Kita **e**.* Toward the north. |
| **と** to | and (joining nouns); with | *Pan **to** mizu.* Bread and water. |
| **の** no | possession, of | *Watashi **no** ie.* My house. |
| **か** ka | question, at the end | *Isha desu **ka**?* Are you a doctor? |
| **も** mo | also | *Watashi **mo**.* Me too. |
| **から** kara / **まで** made | from / until | *Ichi-ji **kara** san-ji **made**.* From one until three. |

**は versus が** is the famous difficulty. Roughly: は sets the topic ("as for X…"), が picks out the subject with emphasis or introduces something new. *Watashi wa gakusei desu* — as for me, I'm a student. *Dare ga kimashita ka?* — WHO came? Beginners can use は for the topic of a sentence and が after question words and with *arimasu/imasu*, and be understood.

## Verbs

Verbs do not change for person or number. *Ikimasu* is I go, you go, she goes, we go. What changes is **tense and politeness**.

The **-masu form** is polite and the one to learn first. Four forms cover most of what you need:

| | Present / future | Past |
|---|---|---|
| **Affirmative** | 食べ**ます** tabemasu — eat, will eat | 食べ**ました** tabemashita — ate |
| **Negative** | 食べ**ません** tabemasen — don't eat | 食べ**ませんでした** tabemasen deshita — didn't eat |

There is no separate future tense: *ikimasu* is "go" and "will go" depending on context.

The **dictionary form** (*taberu*, *iku*, *nomu*) is the plain, casual form and the one in dictionaries. Verbs fall into three groups by how they change; the -masu form is made by:

- **-u verbs** (most): change the final *u* to *i* and add *masu*. *Nomu → nomimasu. Iku → ikimasu. Kaku → kakimasu.*
- **-ru verbs** (ending in *-iru* or *-eru*): drop *ru*, add *masu*. *Taberu → tabemasu. Miru → mimasu.*
- **Two irregulars**: *suru* (do) → *shimasu*; *kuru* (come) → *kimasu*.

The **-te form** is the workhorse for requests, continuous action, and joining verbs: *tabete kudasai* (please eat), *tabete imasu* (am eating). Its formation has rules by verb ending; learn it from the pattern deck and from hearing it.

## Adjectives

Two kinds, and they behave differently.

**い-adjectives** end in *-i* and conjugate themselves: *samui* (cold) → *samukunai* (not cold) → *samukatta* (was cold) → *samukunakatta* (was not cold).

**な-adjectives** need *na* before a noun and use *desu* to conjugate: *shizuka na heya* (a quiet room), *shizuka desu* (is quiet), *shizuka ja arimasen* (is not quiet), *shizuka deshita* (was quiet).

## "To be"

**です** *desu* — is, am, are, for describing and identifying. *Kore wa mizu desu.* This is water. Negative *ja arimasen* (or *dewa arimasen*); past *deshita*.

**あります** *arimasu* — exists, for things and plants. **います** *imasu* — exists, for people and animals. *Mizu ga arimasu.* There is water. *Kodomo ga imasu.* There is a child / I have a child.

## Numbers and counting

*Ichi, ni, san, yon, go, roku, nana, hachi, kyū, jū.* Then *jū-ichi* (11), *ni-jū* (20), *ni-jū-ichi* (21), *hyaku* (100), *sen* (1,000), *man* (10,000). Japanese counts in units of ten thousand: 100,000 is *jū-man*.

Things are counted with a **counter word** after the number, chosen by the shape or kind of thing — long things, flat things, small animals, people. There are dozens; three cover most emergencies: **-nin** for people (*san-nin*, three people; but *hitori*, *futari* for one and two), **-ko** for small objects, and the general **-tsu** series (*hitotsu, futatsu, mittsu, yottsu, itsutsu, muttsu, nanatsu, yattsu, kokonotsu, tō*) which works for almost anything up to ten when you do not know the right counter.

## Politeness

Japanese is spoken at different levels depending on who you are talking to. The **-masu / desu** level is polite and safe with everyone. Plain forms are for family and close friends. Honorific and humble forms exist for formal situations; you do not need them to survive, and a foreigner using polite forms is never rude.

**すみません** *sumimasen* — excuse me, sorry, thank you — is the most useful single word. **お願いします** *onegaishimasu* softens any request. **どうぞ** *dōzo* — please, go ahead, here you are.

## Learning it

1. Hiragana, completely, first. The deck is here.
2. The survival phrases, out loud, until they come without thinking.
3. Katakana. Then you can read a menu.
4. The sentence patterns — a dozen frames into which any vocabulary slots.
5. Kanji by meaning, a few a day, forever.
6. Listen to anything, constantly. The rhythm is caught, not taught.
