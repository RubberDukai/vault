# German — how it works

German and English were one language fifteen hundred years ago, and it shows: *Wasser, Haus, Brot, Milch, Hand, Finger, Winter, gut, alt, jung, kalt, warm* need no translating. The sounds are learnable in an afternoon. What takes longer is the machinery — every noun has a gender, every noun changes with its job in the sentence, and the verb wanders — but the machinery is regular, and even bad German is understood everywhere from the North Sea to the Alps.

## The sounds

Spelling is reliable: once you know the rules, you can say any word you can read.

| Letters | Sound |
|---|---|
| **w** | English *v*: *Wasser* = VASS-er |
| **v** | English *f*: *Vater* = FAH-ter |
| **z** | *ts*: *zehn* = tsayn |
| **s** before a vowel | *z*: *sieben* = ZEE-ben |
| **sch** | *sh*; **sp, st** at the start of a word: *shp, sht* — *Straße* = SHTRAH-seh |
| **ch** after a, o, u | the throaty sound in Scottish *loch*: *Buch, acht* |
| **ch** after e, i, ä, ö, ü, l, r, n | a soft hiss like the *h* in *huge*: *ich, nicht, Milch* |
| **j** | English *y*: *ja* = yah |
| **ei** | *eye*: *nein, drei, Wein* |
| **ie** | *ee*: *vier, Bier, sie* |
| **eu, äu** | *oy*: *neun, Freund, Häuser* |
| **au** | *ow* as in cow: *Haus, Frau* |
| **ä** | like *e* in bed (short) or *ai* in fair (long): *Männer, spät* |
| **ö** | say *ay* with rounded lips: *schön, zwölf* |
| **ü** | say *ee* with rounded lips: *fünf, über, Tür* |
| **ß** | double *s*: *Straße, groß* |
| **-e** at the end | always sounded, a short *uh*: *bitte* = BIT-uh |
| **-er** at the end | a light *ah*: *Wasser* = VASS-ah |
| **r** | in the throat, or rolled in the south |

Stress is on the first syllable of a native word (*AR-beit*, *VAS-ser*); separable prefixes take it (*AUF-stehen*); foreign words keep their own (*Poli-ZEI*, *Stu-DENT*).

## Nouns have a gender — and a capital letter

Every noun is written with a **capital letter**, which makes German easy to read. Every noun is masculine, feminine or neuter, and the gender is part of the word: learn *der Tisch*, *die Tür*, *das Fenster*, never just *Tisch*. Some patterns help: **-e** is usually feminine (*die Lampe, die Straße*); **-ung, -heit, -keit, -schaft** always feminine; **-chen, -lein** always neuter (so *das Mädchen*, the girl); **-er** for people masculine; days, months, seasons and weather masculine. Plurals are irregular — learn them with the noun: *das Haus, die Häuser*; *das Kind, die Kinder*; *die Frau, die Frauen*; *der Apfel, die Äpfel*.

## Cases — the noun's job in the sentence

The article changes to show what the noun is doing. Four cases, but at A1 two do nearly all the work:

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Nominative** (the subject: *who* does it) | der / ein | die / eine | das / ein | die |
| **Accusative** (the direct object: *what* is done) | **den / einen** | die / eine | das / ein | die |
| **Dative** (to/for whom; after many prepositions) | dem / einem | der / einer | dem / einem | den + -n |
| **Genitive** (of, 's) | des | der | des | der |

Only the masculine changes between nominative and accusative — *Der Hund sieht den Mann; der Mann sieht den Hund.* Get that one right and you sound like you know what you are doing. The dative comes with *mit, nach, aus, zu, von, bei* (with, to, from, to, of, at) and with giving: *Ich gebe dem Kind das Brot.*

**Pronouns:** *ich, du, er/sie/es, wir, ihr, sie* (I, you, he/she/it, we, you-all, they) and **Sie** — you, polite, to any adult you do not know well, with the same verb form as *sie*. Accusative: *mich, dich, ihn/sie/es, uns, euch, sie/Sie*. Dative: *mir, dir, ihm/ihr/ihm, uns, euch, ihnen/Ihnen*. *Wie geht es dir?* — how goes it to-you?

## Verbs — the present tense

Take the infinitive (*machen*), drop *-en*, add the endings:

| | mach**en** (do, make) | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| ich | mach**e** | wir | mach**en** |
| du | mach**st** | ihr | mach**t** |
| er / sie / es | mach**t** | sie / Sie | mach**en** |

The present covers the future too: *Morgen gehe ich* — tomorrow I go. Some common verbs change their vowel in the *du* and *er* forms: *sprechen → du sprichst, er spricht*; *essen → isst*; *sehen → sieht*; *fahren → fährst, fährt*; *schlafen → schläft*; *nehmen → nimmst, nimmt*; *geben → gibt*.

**The three you cannot avoid:**
- **sein** (to be): *ich bin, du bist, er ist, wir sind, ihr seid, sie sind*
- **haben** (to have): *ich habe, du hast, er hat, wir haben, ihr habt, sie haben*
- **werden** (to become; makes the future): *ich werde, du wirst, er wird, wir werden, ihr werdet, sie werden*

**Modal verbs** — *können* (can), *müssen* (must), *wollen* (want to), *dürfen* (may), *sollen* (should), *möchten* (would like) — take an infinitive, which goes **to the end**: *Ich kann Deutsch sprechen. Ich muss jetzt gehen. Ich möchte Wasser trinken.* Their *ich* and *er* forms are the same: *ich kann, er kann; ich muss, er muss; ich will, er will; ich möchte, er möchte.*

**Separable verbs** split, and the prefix goes to the end: *aufstehen* (get up) → *Ich stehe um sechs auf.* *ankommen* (arrive) → *Der Zug kommt an.*

## Word order — the one rule and the one exception

**The verb is the second idea in the sentence.** Whatever comes first — subject, time, place — the verb comes next: *Ich gehe morgen nach Hause. Morgen gehe ich nach Hause. Nach Hause gehe ich morgen.* All correct. Time before manner before place: *Ich fahre morgen mit dem Zug nach Berlin.*

**Questions** put the verb first: *Gehst du? Hast du Wasser?* Question words come first, verb second: *Wo ist der Bahnhof? Was kostet das? Wann kommt der Zug?*

**The exception:** after *dass* (that), *weil* (because), *wenn* (if, when), *ob* (whether) and the other subordinating words, the verb goes **to the end of its clause**: *Ich bleibe zu Hause, weil ich krank bin.* — I stay at home, because I ill am. *Ich weiß, dass er kommt.*

**Not:** *nicht* after the verb and its object: *Ich verstehe das nicht. Ich habe kein Geld* — *kein* is "not a / no" before a noun.

## The past — what you need at A1

**The perfect** is the spoken past: *haben* (or *sein* for movement and change) + a participle at the end. Regular participles: *ge-* + stem + *-t*: *machen → gemacht; kaufen → gekauft; arbeiten → gearbeitet.* Irregular ones end in *-en* and change the vowel: *gehen → gegangen; kommen → gekommen; essen → gegessen; trinken → getrunken; sehen → gesehen; schlafen → geschlafen; nehmen → genommen; sprechen → gesprochen; finden → gefunden; schreiben → geschrieben.*
*Ich habe Brot gekauft.* — I have bought bread. *Ich bin nach Hause gegangen.* — I went home (movement: *sein*). *Wir sind gekommen.*

*Sein* and *haben* have their own simple past you will hear constantly: *ich war* (I was), *ich hatte* (I had); *es war kalt; wir hatten kein Wasser.*

## Numbers

*null, eins, zwei, drei, vier, fünf, sechs, sieben, acht, neun, zehn, elf, zwölf, dreizehn … neunzehn, zwanzig.* Then the famous back-to-front tens: **einundzwanzig** (one-and-twenty), *zweiundzwanzig … dreißig, vierzig, fünfzig, sechzig, siebzig, achtzig, neunzig, hundert, tausend.* 1,234 = *tausendzweihundertvierunddreißig*, one word. Dates: *der erste Mai* (first of May); the time: *um drei Uhr* (at three), *halb vier* = **half to four = 3:30**, the trap for every English speaker.

## Learning it

1. The sounds, and *ch*, *ü*, *ö*, *ei/ie* until they are automatic. Read everything aloud.
2. The survival phrases.
3. *sein, haben*, the modal verbs, and "verb second".
4. Nouns **with** their gender and plural, twenty a week.
5. The accusative *den/einen* and the dative after *mit, zu, von*.
6. The perfect tense, and you can say what happened yesterday.
7. Then the rest of the cases, adjective endings and the subjunctive can wait; nobody in a farmyard will mind.
